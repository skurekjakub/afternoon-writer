The market gate let Andorhal have its grain one wagon at a time, and every wagon stopped under the arch while a clerk wrote it into a book.

Sylvanas stood in the queue on foot, behind a cart of barley, with the last of the sun lying flat across the back of her neck and her legs stiff from the saddle. Behind her the line ran back up the road past the first houses, wagon after wagon, the teams standing hipshot in the traces and a carter somewhere at the back telling the whole road what he thought of the market gate. The horses were a long way back up the road in a carter's yard outside the wall, where the ostler had watched four tall Thalassian animals come in through his gate sweating and walling their eyes, and had taken her silver without a word about any of it. The mare had gone sideways the whole way down the hill with Jaina on her back. She had shied again at the yard gate, and Lyriel had lifted Jaina off her there, in the straw, before she could try it a third time.

Sylvanas had written the pass on the lid of the ostler's feed bin with the ostler's pen: *The bearer, Jaina Proudmoore of Dalaran, travels on the business of the Kirin Tor under escort of the Farstriders of Quel'Thalas. Give her passage.* Under that went her name and her rank, because she had no other name to sign that would get a human mage and three elven bows through a gate, and then the leaf-and-arrow, pressed into wax she had softened at his lamp. The wax had run on the left side, the way it always did when she was quick about it.

Now the gate. She read it the way she had read every gate since the Pass: where it was weak, and when.

The wall was field stone, no higher than a man's chest from the road, the mortar crumbling and patched with worse. There was a walk along the top wide enough for one guard. A boy could have gone over it with a tailboard for a ladder, and nobody had built it to keep boys out, or anything else that came over walls. The arch itself was barely wider than a wagon. Two men in militia leather stood in it, each with an iron grain spike as long as his arm and grooved down one side, and they drove the spikes into the sacks on every cart, drew them out and looked at what came up in the groove. A third man leaned on the wall above with his back half turned to the road. Just inside the arch, in the shade, a clerk sat at a table.

He was young and already going bald on top, with ink to the cuff of his right sleeve. Beside him stood a board with a row of wax seals pressed onto it and a name inked under each—the granaries' own seals, she supposed, kept there to check against—and he held each carter's bill up beside the board and looked from one to the other before he wrote. His pen hand shook. The nib chattered on the paper at the start of every line and settled by the end of it.

"Whose yard?" he said to the barley man.

"Hollin's. Same as yesterday. You saw me yesterday."

"Seal."

The barley man held up his bill. The clerk found Hollin's in the row and looked from the board to the bill and back, while the guards spiked the sacks. Grain ran out of the holes when the spikes came out, a thin trickle off the tailboard, and went down into the cobbles with the rest. The stones under the arch were pale with it, every crack packed full, and the threshold in front of them was mud trodden so hard by boots and hooves that it shone. The whole gate smelled of warm grain, dry and sweet, like bread before it goes in the oven.

"Since when," the barley man said, "do you spike barley at the market gate?"

"Since the Exchange said." The guard wiped his spike on his thigh. "Move on."

Since the Exchange said. Which exchange, and who in it, and why this week? It could be a town looking at its own grain at last, after a summer of burying people. It could as easily be somebody inside who wanted every load counted and every seal matched, so he would know at once what came in that wasn't his. For most of a week she had found every door shut just before she reached it. Either one fit, and the gate couldn't tell her which.

A gate was a weak place; that was the whole use of a gate. You found the hour the watch got bored, the guard who watched the road and not the ditch, the corner the lamp didn't reach. This one had a weak place a child could find, and nobody needed it, because they had a man with a book. Was he tired, or told to look tired? Tired; look at the bald patch, look at the pen. Anyone could be told to shake. No. Nobody could order a man's nib to chatter at the start of a line and steady by the end of it. Then he was honest and worn out, and she still couldn't have told him from a man who was worn out and paid, not from here and not from the arch. She had come down that hill meaning to read Andorhal better than the people who lived in it. Sun's sake. Half the ground in this town was ink.

"Ranger-General." Cyndia, low, at her left shoulder. "Two in the arch with the spikes. One on the walk, left of the arch, and he hasn't looked out at the road since we joined the line. Two more inside by the trough, sitting down." Her hand rested on her quiver strap. "Four of the five are watching the wagons."

Sylvanas gave her a nod. She hadn't asked for it.

Jaina stood at Sylvanas's right with the staff in the crook of her dead arm and the pass held up in her two bare fingertips, her jaw shut so hard that Sylvanas could see a muscle jumping under her ear. There was a brown crust at the edge of one nostril that she had missed. The linen on her left hand had gone stiff and rust-coloured across the knuckles where she had wiped her lip on the hill, and the crystal by her cheek gave about as much light as a coal going grey. Lyriel stood half a step behind her on the right, close enough to get an arm under her if her knees went, and wasn't looking at Jaina at all. She was watching the man on the wall.

The barley cart rolled through. "Next," the clerk said.

They stepped up onto the hard mud of the threshold. Jaina held out the pass. The clerk took it, read it, looked at the seal and then at his board of granary seals, though the leaf-and-arrow would never be on it, and back at the pass.

"Dalaran," he said.

"Yes."

"Business in the city?"

"The Kirin Tor's. I'm to report to their house in the mage quarter."

"Any business with the granaries? Buying or selling. A factor, the guild, a yard."

"None."

"Carrying grain? Flour, meal, seed, malt?"

"No."

"Lodging?"

"With the Kirin Tor."

"For how long?"

"Until the business is done."

He looked up at that. Jaina looked back at him with her chin level and blood dried in her nose, and waited, in the plain flat way of someone who had stood at a good many tables while a good many clerks decided whether to be difficult. He dipped his pen.

Sylvanas could not have done that. Somewhere around the grain question she would have asked him what business it was of his what the Kirin Tor bought, and they would all still be standing on the threshold.

He tipped the pass toward the light to read the bottom of it. "Windrunner," he said under his breath. "Ranger-General." He looked along the three of them, Cyndia and Lyriel and herself, and his eyes stopped on her. "Which—"

"My escort," Jaina said.

He wrote. Sylvanas watched the pen go: *Proudmoore, Dalaran, Kirin Tor. Escort, Farstriders, three.* Then her own name, copied off the foot of the pass in his shaking letters, into a book that lay open on a table in a gate, where anyone with a reason could turn back a page and find it.

She had signed it herself. There was no other name that would have got them through without an argument under the arch. That had been true at the feed bin and it was true now, and she liked it no better.

"Through," the clerk said. He handed the pass back and was already looking past them at the next wagon.

Lyriel took the pass out of Jaina's fingers before she could fumble it and slid it inside the front of Jaina's coat, and the four of them went in under the arch.

The street on the other side was loud. Iron tyres ground on the cobbles; somebody was hammering at a hoop; a woman leaned out of an upper window shouting a boy's name over and over. The grain dust was everywhere, worked into the gaps between the stones all along the street and lying in a fine pale skin on the sills. Twenty paces in, the carts bore off left down a broad lane toward the granaries, and at the corner of that lane a man sat on an upturned barrel with a slate on his knee. He wore no militia leather, only a brown coat and a cap. He chalked a mark on the slate for every wagon that turned down the lane. When the four of them came level he looked up, looked at the bows, and chalked again, and Sylvanas could not see whether that mark was for them or for the wagon behind.

So the granary quarter kept its own count on top of the gate's. Whose count? A factor's. The Exchange's. Somebody who wanted to know every cart that came into his streets and where it went once it did.

She walked on and didn't look back at him.

On the left, past the lane, a squat stone house stood with its door open, a lamp already lit inside against the shadow of the street, and a bench outside the door with nobody on it. The militia post, where the tally-man had said it would be.

The post was one low room with a beaten earth floor, and the lamp smoked. The smoke hung under the beams in a flat grey layer and stung the eyes. A table ran across the back. On the wall behind it hung a map of the district, drawn on a sheet of canvas nailed up at the corners, and it had been chalked on and rubbed out so many times that the streets had gone grey. On the near corner of the table lay a stack of papers a hand high, curled at the edges and furred with dust, held down by a river stone that had cracked clean across and been fitted back together on top of the pile.

Two men were at the table. The one sitting was saying, "—leave it with the others, lad, and I'll get to it when—" and he stopped when Sylvanas came in.

He stood up. He was not tall, but he was broad and square, built like a man who had carried sacks before he ever carried a truncheon. His militia leather was dusted grey from the street to the thigh. His hair was cropped close and going iron at the sides, and the backs of both his hands were seamed with old white scars, rope or fire, she couldn't tell which. His right hand hung beside a truncheon on his belt with a grip worn pale. He looked at her, then at Cyndia behind her, at the bows.

"Ranger-General," he said. Then he looked past her to the door, where Lyriel was bringing Jaina in with one hand under her left elbow. "My lady. Sit, please. You look done in. Corin—"

The other man was already moving. He was young and thin, in a plain grey coat with a violet cord stitched round the collar, and a satchel on a strap across his chest. There was ink on every finger of his right hand and on two of his left, and the skin under his eyes was dark as a bruise. He pulled the stool out from the table for Jaina so fast that it tipped, and he caught it before it went over.

Jaina sat. She set the heel of the staff on the earth floor between her boots and leaned on it. Cyndia went to the wall beside the window, where she could see both the street and the door. Lyriel stayed in the doorway with her shoulder to the post, facing out.

"Thank you," Jaina said. "Jaina Proudmoore, of Dalaran. A man on the road, a tally-man from the granaries, told us to ask here for Captain Havel."

"You've found him." Havel sat back down on the edge of the table rather than the chair. "They do send them to me. I'm cheaper than a factor, and I'm still here after dark."

"Proudmoore," the young man said. "Lady Proudmoore. From the— is it the Citadel? Did someone finally—" His inky hand went flat against the satchel and pressed. "Were you sent? About the shipments?"

"No," Jaina said. "Nobody sent me."

His hand stayed on the satchel.

"Where are people sick, Captain?" Sylvanas said.

Havel answered at once. He got up and went to the canvas on the wall and put two fingers on it, held flat together like a blade. "Mill lane. Here, from the weir bridge up to the Corn Exchange. There's four yards on it: Hollin's, Fenner's, the Exchange granary, and Pell's. It's them and every street that buys its bread off the bakers at the bottom of the lane. The old ones go first, and the babies. A cough, and they get thin, and then one morning they don't get up. The physicians call it the river damp."

"Is it?"

"Damp doesn't stop at a street corner." His two fingers slid along the grey lane and stopped where a cross street met it. "This does. I've a widow on this corner who's lost both her lodgers, and her sister across the way has never had so much as a sniffle. They buy from different ovens."

"Since when?"

"There's been some since spring, an old man here and there, the kind you put down to his age. It went bad at high summer. It went bad when Fenner's and the Exchange changed their suppliers, three months back, the both of them in the same week. New carters, new loaders, and a factor's name on the contracts that nobody at the Exchange has ever shaken by the hand. I asked."

"The loaders."

"Hired in, from outside the town." His right hand had gone down to the truncheon, and it stayed there. "I've stood at Fenner's gate at the end of a shift and watched them come out. They come in twos, and nobody calls it. When the front pair stop at the corner the lot of them stop and wait, and then they go on again. I've men on this post I've drilled since they were boys, and I couldn't get that out of them. And there's not one of them been seen in the Wheatsheaf since midsummer, when every other loader on the lane drinks there."

Drilled, then. Somebody had stood those men in a yard and walked them up and down it until they stopped at a corner without being told. His hand was on his truncheon, and the hand agreed with the mouth.

"Where do they sleep, Captain?" Cyndia said from the window.

"Behind Fenner's. There's a long shed back there that used to be a rope-walk. The new supplier put a door on each end of it and a lock on each door."

"Why haven't you taken them?" Sylvanas said.

"I took two." He said it flat. "At midsummer, for brawling. There'd been no brawl; I wanted them in a room with a lamp and a door I could shut. The magistrate had them out again by dinner, and their factor wrote to the town council about my manners, and the council had the letter read out in the hall." He rubbed the back of one scarred hand with the thumb of the other. "I've twelve men between this gate and the mill lane. When I take that crew, I want the whole of it at once, and I want it done so the magistrate can't open the door the same afternoon."

Three months. Three months he had stood at a yard gate watching drilled men come off shift, and gone back to his post and written it down and waited for a magistrate to grow sense. She'd have had the rope-walk shut the first night. With what—twelve men and a council that read out letters about his manners? Then with twelve men. And the magistrate would have had them out by dinner, and the crew would have been warned. She'd still have done it.

"It isn't four yards," the young man said.

"Lad."

"Sorry. Captain. I— it isn't, though." He patted the satchel, then unbuckled it and drew out a fold of papers, and looked round for somewhere to put them down flat. He lifted the cracked river stone off the dusty pile on the corner of the table and set it on his own sheets to hold them. Sylvanas looked at the pile he had taken it from. The top sheet was in the same small upright hand as the new ones, and so was the one under it.

"Those are mine," he said, when he saw her looking. "Notices. If an inspector flags a shipment, the notice comes to the gate post. That's the ordinance. So I bring them here." He didn't look at Havel. "These are copies of waybills, Ranger-General." He said the rank carefully, getting all of it in. "The inspection office keeps the second copy of every bill that leaves the city under a yard's mark. This is Fenner's mark for the south road." He turned one round to face her and put an inky finger beside a small drawn sign in the corner: a hook with two bars through the shank. "Fenner's clerk starts the hook at the top and pulls it down. This one was started at the bottom. You can see where the ink sat before the pen moved."

There was a dark bead at the foot of the hook, where the pen had rested. On the bill beside it the hook began clean and thin at the top.

"And on the day on that bill," he said, "Fenner's own book has both its south wagons in the shed with a cracked axle. Somebody's copying the mark."

"How many?"

"Forty-one I'd swear to, since midsummer. Fenner's mark, the Exchange mark, Pell's. And it isn't only the south road; there are some out on every road from the city." He put his hand flat on the old pile, on the dust. "When a village takes sick the reeve writes in to the town, and those letters come here too. I put them beside the bills. Most of the villages on a copied mark have written in since. Some haven't. Yet."

"Forty-one," Havel said, "out of how many thousand loads we've sent out since midsummer? I don't say the lad's wrong, Ranger-General. He's careful, I'll give him that. But it's a crew in four yards that's filling those carts. Take the crew and you've stopped the carts."

"And the forty-one already gone?" Corin said.

"Are gone. I can't arrest a village, lad."

"I'm not asking you to arrest a village, I'm asking you to—"

"You're asking me to go into the Exchange with a pile of copies and tell the guild it's been sending poison down the King's road under somebody else's seal. They'd have me off this post by morning, and you back to Dalaran in a box."

The young man shut his mouth. His face had gone red to the ears.

Sylvanas looked from the canvas on the wall to the bills under the cracked stone. One man had the yards and the other had the roads. Havel knew where the poison went into the sacks and Corin knew where the sacks went after, and they had spent the summer at this table with their halves arguing about which was bigger. Neither of them had a name for whoever stood the loaders in a yard and drilled them.

"I want to see Fenner's," she said.

"You will." Havel went back to the wall at once. His two flat fingers left the mill lane and went down a narrower street behind it. "Come at it from the back, down Hurdle Lane from here. My lads walk Hurdle Lane day and night; nobody gets behind you there. You'll come out by the rope-walk."

She looked at the canvas. The mill lane ran straight from the weir bridge to Fenner's front gate, where the carts went in and the loaders came out in their twos. Hurdle Lane came in behind the rope-walk, where she would have a back wall to look at and very little else. A captain who liked strangers kept on his own streets, then. Or something else. She couldn't tell from a chalked wall, and she let it lie.

"The man on the barrel's packed up his slate," Lyriel said from the doorway. "There's a boy come up the street with a pole, lighting the lamps."

Jaina hadn't said a word since she sat down. She had been sitting with the staff between her boots and her eyes half shut, and the crystal by her cheek had not grown any brighter since the gate. Now she opened her eyes and looked at the young man.

"Corin," she said. "Who did you write to? In Dalaran."

He straightened. "The inspection desk at the Citadel, Lady Proudmoore. Every week, from the first copied mark I found. Then, when nothing came back, the Archmage's secretary. I kept copies of all of them, if you'd—"

"Did anyone answer?"

"No."

The two bare fingertips on the staff went white. She said nothing to that. She leaned forward on the staff instead and looked at the bills under the stone, the hook with the dark bead at its foot.

"The flagged loads," she said. "When an inspector flags one, does the office keep any of the grain?"

"A bag." He said it fast, and he was nodding before she'd finished. "Every flagged load leaves a bag in the sample room. Sealed, and marked with the bill. They're on shelves. Nobody's opened one since— nobody opens them."

"Can I put my hand on them?"

"The sample room wants a magister's ring on the door." He patted the satchel. "I can get you in."

Sylvanas looked at Jaina. Jaina was already getting the staff under her and her feet under the staff, and Lyriel had come in from the door to put a hand beneath her elbow before anyone asked.

Out in the street the sun had gone down behind the roofs on the far side, and only the granary lane still had it. The light came up the lane long and yellow and lay across a wagon drawn up at the corner, so that the ropes threw shadows over the sacks and the stitching showed along every seam. The lamplighter had got as far as the post. He reached up with his pole to the bracket over the bench, the wick took, and he went on up the street whistling the same three notes he had been whistling at the gate.

Havel came out behind them, pulling a fold of papers from inside his leather. He crossed to the warehouse on the near corner of the lane, a blind stone wall with one hoist door high up and shut, and spread the sheets against it with the flat of his hand, one overlapping the next. They were his own. The district was drawn on them in pencil, smaller and closer than the canvas inside, every yard with its name written in and small marks crowded into the margins that she couldn't read from where she stood.

"Hurdle Lane," he said. His two fingers went down it. "Behind Hollin's from here, past Pell's back gate, and you come out at the rope-walk end of Fenner's. The day crews go off at the Exchange bell, when it's full dark, and the night crews go in after. If you want to see that lot come out, that's when."

Corin had the satchel open on the bench under the new lamp and was going through it with both hands. Brass clinked. He brought out a ring of tags on a cord, each one stamped with a mark, and turned them to the light one after another and put the ring back and went in again, for a small leather pouch this time. "Not that one. That's the copy room. This is— no. Sorry. A moment, Lady Proudmoore. The sample room has its own, because the magisters wanted—"

Jaina stood by the bench with the staff in the crook of her dead arm and the wrapped hand resting on it, quite still, two fingertips showing at the end of the linen. She watched his hands go in and out of the satchel. The bench was right behind her knees, and she had not sat back down on it.

The sample room was across the city in the mage quarter, behind a door that wanted a magister's ring, and the people inside it would be magisters and clerks and boys like this one. Sylvanas would be a bow in a corridor. Every head in the building would come round to look at her, and none of them would look at Jaina, and that would be the most use she was. The bags were Jaina's work. The men were down this lane, and Jaina couldn't tell a drilled man's walk from a tired one if she watched it till morning. So: each to what she could do. She had known it since the boy said *bag*.

The boy, though. She had known him for the length of one argument at a table. He had kept a summer of notices under a cracked stone and written every week to people who never once wrote back, so he was an honest man or a very patient one, and she could think of uses for a patient man with the keys to the inspection office of a poisoned town. Without him the mage would get a bench in a hall till they lit the lamps, as the tally-man had promised. They were lighting them now.

Lyriel, then. Lyriel would watch the corridors. And when Jaina was in a room of flagged grain with her two fingers on a sealed bag and the staff dragging at her the way it had on the hill, Lyriel could catch her when she went over, and that was the whole of what anybody with a bow could do about it.

It had been easier at Corley's. Jaina at the crossing with her mile-post, Sylvanas at the clerk's table, and never more than a shout between them; she had only to lift her head from the book. Did she trust the mage, then? She trusted what the mage came back with. Whether that was the same thing could wait for a night with less in it.

She'd rather have had Jaina's eyes on Havel than her own, if it came to that. Jaina had sat through all of his talk at the table without a word, and she would have an opinion. No. She would rather have had Jaina where she could see her. That was a different thing, and she left it alone.

Corin held up a brass tag with a ring stamped into it. "This one."

"Proudmoore," Sylvanas said. "The sample room. Take him."

Jaina was already turning on the staff. "I'll know what's in the bags," she said. "I won't know who put it there."

"That's mine."

Jaina nodded once. She didn't ask where Sylvanas was going, or tell her she ought to come and see the bags for herself, and she didn't point out that she could barely stand. Sylvanas had a short answer ready for each of those, and nobody gave her a use for it. Lyriel was still at Jaina's elbow. Cyndia had come round onto Sylvanas's left with her bow in her hand.

"Back here," Sylvanas said. "When the Exchange rings the night shift."

"When it rings."

"I can send a lad across with you, my lady," Havel said from the wall. "The Exchange steps are bad after dark."

"Thank you, Captain. I have one."

"Two," Lyriel said, "and one of them knows the steps."

Corin went red again and set off up the street too fast, stopped, came back, and set off a second time at Jaina's pace, with the satchel held against his chest in both arms. Lyriel went on Jaina's right with her hand under the elbow and her eyes on the windows above them. The staff knocked on the cobbles, and the knock went on up the street after the three of them, under the noise of the wheels.

Sylvanas turned to Havel's papers before they reached the corner.

"Hurdle Lane, Captain."

Hurdle Lane ran along the backs of the granaries, wide enough for one wagon and a man flat against the wall beside it. The granaries stood over it on their stone feet, timber from the first floor up, and their hoist beams stuck out overhead with the ropes hanging slack and the hooks swinging a little whenever a wagon went under. The lane was in shadow from end to end. Up above, the last of the sun lay along the gables and turned the dust in the air there gold, and down at the bottom, where they walked, the dust was only grey and got into the mouth.

Every few yards another lane came in from one side or the other, loading lanes for the yards, and every one of them came in blind. The walls ran right up to the corner, so that she couldn't see a stride into any of them until she was standing in its mouth. Wagons backed out of them without warning. She counted the corners, because she couldn't help it: eleven before Pell's, and she stopped counting.

Havel walked a half-step ahead of her on the left and talked the whole way, the way a man talks about a house he has lived in all his life.

"That's Hollin's. Old Hollin's boy has it now. His men were with the father, most of them, and their fathers before that, and you can leave Hollin's out." His hand came up, the two fingers flat, and went to a gate on the right. "Pell's. Yard-reeve's a man called Dunmore. He drinks in the Wheatsheaf and owes money in it, and he's not a careful man, but he's not a clever one either. Pell's took a little of the new factor's grain at Lammas and stopped. Dunmore told me the price was too good, and he didn't like it."

"Who else took it?"

"Fenner's and the Exchange, as I said, the same week. And two I left out at the post." He looked at her sidelong, as if she might hold it against him. "Ashby's on Tenter Row, and the old malt-house at the weir end that Pell leases. They're small. They take what the big yards haven't room for. The same factor, the same month."

"Four, then."

"Four."

At Pell's back gate a boy was lighting the lamp on the gatepost. The glass had been wiped and put back on, and dust had settled on it already, so the new flame showed brown through it. A cart came out of the next lane with its axle squealing, and for as long as it took to pass, Sylvanas couldn't hear her own feet or Havel's or anybody else's. She looked back up the lane at that. A man with a sack on his shoulder. Two women with a basket between them. Nobody she couldn't account for.

The mouth of Tenter Row came on the right. It was narrower than the rest and ran straight for thirty paces and then bent left, and over the bend, against the sky, she could see the gable of a granary and its hoist beam with no rope on it.

"Tenter Row," Havel said. His fingers went past it and pointed on down Hurdle Lane. "Dyers, mostly, and Ashby's at the bottom. Ashby shut his gate at noon. You'll get nothing there tonight, and you'll get Fenner's crew if we go on while the light holds."

That was the second time those fingers had pointed her somewhere other than where she was looking. Once at the post, now here. A man who knew his own streets and wanted a stranger on the ones he trusted would point like that. So would other men. She went on down Hurdle Lane at his side, and she marked the bend in Tenter Row as she passed it.

The lane opened out at the bottom into a yard of beaten earth behind Fenner's, with the rope-walk shed along one side of it, long and low and windowless, a door at each end, a hasp and a padlock on each door. Fenner's granary stood over the yard. The day crew was coming down out of it. Men sat on sacks against the granary wall with their caps off, men were drinking at the trough, a man on the hoist platform was coiling rope, and two boys were sweeping spilled grain into a heap with brooms worn down to the binding.

"My lad's in the cooper's door there," Havel said, and nodded across the yard. "You can stand with him, and nobody looks twice at a man in a doorway."

"Here will do."

She stood where she was, at the corner of the lane on the far side of a wagon whose team was hanging its heads into nosebags, and watched the yard over the horses' backs. Cyndia stood at her left shoulder. Havel stood a step away, put his thumbs in his belt, and said nothing more about the cooper's door.

Most of the yard was tired. She could see it in how the men sat and how they stood up again, a hand on a knee, a wince, a man arching his back with his fists in it. They had carried sacks all day, and they were going home.

Some of them weren't.

Two men stood at the near corner of the rope-walk with their weight square on both feet and their hands empty at their sides. There was a wall right behind them, and neither of them was leaning on it. At the trough a second pair drank one at a time. When the first bent to the water the other stood with his face to the lane, and when he had done they changed over without a word, and the second man's face went to the lane in his turn. Up on the hoist platform the man coiling rope had stopped coiling. He held the coil in both hands and looked down into the yard, not at the gate, and not at anybody. He was simply looking at the yard the way a man on a wall looks at a road.

They were waiting, all of them, and none of them was tired. A tired man would have leaned on that wall.

She had seen that kind of standing before, once, in a boy on a hoist at Brask's granary who had bitten open his own collar sooner than speak to her.

"Ranger-General." Cyndia, very low. "Two at the corner of the shed. Two at the trough, and they take turns. The one up on the hoist. And three by the gate, sitting down. The one in the middle of the three hasn't taken his cap off, and the other two keep their feet under them." She said it in the order she had seen it. "Eight that I'm sure of."

Eight. Eight men in one yard at the end of one shift, placed—the corner, the trough, the platform and the gate—so that between them they had the whole of the yard and both ends of the shed and the mouth of the lane where she was standing. Somebody had put them there. And she had walked into Andorhal thinking it was sick, a town eating bad grain at the far end of a bad road, and that she would find whoever was selling it and break his arm.

This wasn't a sickness, then. It had sentries.

"Are those your loaders, Captain?"

"Those are them." Havel's jaw had gone tight. "The ones by the gate came in at Lammas. The pair at the trough in high summer, with the first lot."

"Who's their gaffer?"

"Fenner's yard-reeve is Coll Simmons. He's been here twenty years and he's frightened of them." He watched the men at the gate. "He'll not say so. I've asked him."

The light had come down off the gables. A boy went round the yard with a taper lighting the lanterns on their hooks, and one by one the faces came out yellow under them, and when the lamp over the trough was lit the pair there turned their heads away from it, both together.

The man on the hoist platform hung his coil on its peg and came down the outside ladder. At the bottom he said something to the pair at the shed corner, and one of them answered, and he walked across the yard toward the lane alone, round the tail of the wagon and past Sylvanas so close that she could have put out her hand and taken his sleeve. He was a big man going soft, with a loader's leather pad on one shoulder and a brown beard, and grain dust grey in the hair of his forearms. He did not look at the wagon, or at her, or at Havel, whom every other man in that yard had looked at at least once.

He kept his eyes in front of him and went up Hurdle Lane. At the mouth of the first loading lane, where the corner of a wall stood out with the mortar gone soft and crumbling between the stones, he put his left hand flat on the stone as if to steady himself, the way a tired man does. His thumb pushed something into a crack between two stones at the height of his hip, and went on pushing until whatever it was had gone in. Then he took his hand away and walked on.

He hadn't looked round. Not before, and not while he did it, and not after.

A man who didn't look round had nothing to hide, or he had somebody else to do his looking for him. She looked up. Hoist beams, loft doors, a pigeon walking the ridge of Pell's roof; nothing on the gables that she could see from the bottom of a lane at dusk, which was not the same as nothing on the gables.

The crack. She could have whatever was in it out on the point of her knife and know. And then whoever came for it next would put his thumb in an empty crack, and by morning the eight would be gone from Fenner's yard with their locks and their factor, and the rope-walk would have a new crew who had never heard of any of them. Leave it in the wall. Take the man.

"Captain," she said. "Show my ranger the shed doors, both ends, and which way the crew goes out at night. I want it from above."

"There's a ladder up to Fenner's loft. I'll have Simmons—"

"I'll find my own."

Cyndia's eyes went once to the corner where the man had gone and came back to Sylvanas. "Ranger-General." She turned to Havel and asked him which end of the shed they unlocked first, and he began to tell her.

Sylvanas went up Hurdle Lane.

She didn't hurry. The loader was forty paces ahead of her and in no hurry himself, the leather pad on his shoulder showing pale in the dusk, and he walked up the middle of the lane like a man who had been on his feet since dawn. She walked at the side of it, where she could step into the mouth of a loading lane if he stopped. He didn't stop. He passed the crack in the wall without a glance, and at the mouth of Tenter Row he turned in.

Tenter Row. Where Havel's fingers had gone past it. She went after him.

The row was straight to the bend and empty, and if he turned round in it there was nowhere to be. So she waited at the corner with her shoulder to the stone until he had reached the bend and gone round it, and then she went down the row fast and quiet. The dyers' yards on either side gave off a sour stink of the vats. In one yard the day's cloth still hung on the tenter frames, long sheets of it, blue and rust-red, stretched on their hooks and not moving at all in the still air. The dust lay thick at the edges of the cobbles and showed every print, so she kept to the crown of the row where the wheels had ground the stones clean, and left nothing behind her.

Round the bend the row met a wider street, and there was traffic on it, a wagon going down toward the weir with its load roped and the carter walking at the horses' heads. The loader fell in behind the wagon. Sylvanas came out of the row behind him and crossed to the wagon's other side, and walked there with the high wheels between them and the axle grinding loud enough to cover a troop of horse, let alone her feet. Through the spokes she could see his boots. At the next corner the boots went off to the left, and she let the wagon carry on without her and stood a moment at the corner before she looked.

It was a back lane, long and straight. Loading doors ran along both sides of it, every one of them shut and barred, and the cobbles were white with dust from wall to wall. There was a lamp lit at the near end and another at the far end, and between them nothing at all: no wagon, no doorway deep enough to stand in, no turning. He was a third of the way down it.

If he looked back now he would see a tall elf standing alone at the end of an empty lane.

A water butt stood against the wall at the corner, under the eaves of a lean-to. She went up onto the rim of the butt, then onto the lean-to roof, and from there she reached for the edge of the main roof. The two fingers Tam had bitten wouldn't close properly on it. They were purple at the middle joint and still slow, and they caught on the edge late and held on badly. She put her weight on the other two and her forearm and went up anyway, and lay flat on the slates at the top with grit pressing into her cheek and her heart going harder than the climb deserved.

Up here she had him. The roofs along the back lane ran on one into the next with hardly a step between them, slate and then old thatch and then slate again, and the chimneys stood up out of them at intervals like posts on a fence line. She went along them bent low, keeping the ridge between herself and the lane where she could, and when she had to come over it she came over flat. The slates were gritty with the same dust as the street. Soot came off the chimney stacks onto her hands and her sleeves. One chimney she put her palm against was still warm from somebody's supper fire, and in the house underneath it a woman was singing to a child, the same line over and over, not quite in tune.

Below her he walked down the white lane in the lamplight and turned in at the last door on the left. It was a granary, smaller and older than Fenner's, stone below and black timber above, with its hoist beam bare. She knew that beam. She had seen it over the bend in Tenter Row with the last of the sun on it.

Ashby's. Ashby shut his gate at noon.

She came along the last roof on her elbows and stopped behind a chimney stack across the lane from it, and looked.

The granary's upper floor had a row of small windows along the lane side, the panes thick with dust and old cobweb, and lamplight behind them, a lot of it. It was a warm night with no wind, and somebody inside had knocked the bottom pane out of the nearest window to let some air in. Through the gap she could see half the length of the loft floor.

Sacks stood along it in a row, open, their mouths rolled down. On the side of the nearest she could make out a sheaf in a ring, stencilled, and letters under it that she didn't need to read. Her hand went to the front of her coat, where the square she had cut from Brask's loft was folded against her ribs, and stayed there.

There were five men in the loft. Every one of them had a cloth tied over his nose and mouth, and every one of the cloths had a dull shine to it under the lamps—waxed, like the cloth over the mouth of the jar under Brask's floor. They wore leather gloves that came up past the elbow. One of them had a crock between his feet. He bent and put his gloved hand into it and brought it out full of something dark and thick that hung off his fingers like cold dripping, and he pushed his hand down into the open sack, deep, his arm in the grain to the elbow, and worked it there. Then he drew it out and went to the next sack and bent to the crock again. Behind him a second man rolled up the mouth of the sack he had finished and began to sew it shut with a long needle, the same quick stitch as on the wagon at the corner by the gate, and a third took the closed sacks away to a stack by the stair.

The man she had followed came up the stair into the lamplight with his beard and his leather pad and no mask at all. He stopped at the top. He didn't come any nearer the sacks than that, not one stride. The man with the crock straightened, came across to him, and stood holding his gloved hands up and away from both their bodies while they talked. She couldn't hear a word of it. The loader said something short. The masked man nodded. The loader went back down the stair, and the masked man went back to his crock.

So there were two kinds of them. The one who carried word between cracks in walls and never went near a sack, and the ones in the loft who did the sacks and never came out to the yards. Eight at Fenner's. Five here. How many more cracks in how many walls?

The man nearest the window finished his sack. He stepped back from it and peeled his gloves off one at a time without letting them touch his skin, and dropped them into a bucket by the wall. Then he washed in a basin, hands and forearms, up to the elbow, and only after he had dried them did he pull the waxed cloth down off his face and drink from a jug. He drank with his head turned away from the sacks. He put the cloth back up before he took a new pair of gloves off the pile.

Every hoist man at Fenner's had worked all day in that dust with his face bare and his shirt open. The guard in the arch had wiped grain off his spike onto his thigh. The whole city breathed this stuff and ate it baked. These men scrubbed to the elbow before they would put a jug to their mouths. What were they keeping out of them? Not the dust. Whatever came out of that crock.

And Jaina was across the city by now, in a room full of sacks that had come out of rooms like this one, putting her two bare fingertips to them one after another to see how bad it was. She'd be white by the time the bell went. Whiter.

Her elbow moved on the slates. A little run of grit went out from under it and down the slope of the roof, very small, and pattered into the gutter.

Inside the loft the man at the window lifted his head.

He stood with his gloved hands held out from his sides and looked at the window. He looked at it for a long time. From in there, with the lamps behind him, the glass would give him back the lamps and his own masked face and nothing much else; a dark roof across a dark lane, and a chimney. Probably that. She lay still with the grit against her cheek and her bad fingers curled under her and didn't so much as blink. If he had seen her, he would not stand at the window like that. He would go to the stair, and the loader would be back in the lane below with others. Unless he had been taught to stand at the window like that first.

He turned away and picked up his sack needle.

Then the bell. It came across the roofs from the middle of the city, heavy and slow, the Exchange calling the night shift, and down in the lanes she heard the yards answer it—doors, a shout, wheels starting up. In Ashby's loft nobody looked up from the sacks. They had a night's work in front of them, and by first light the sacks would be stitched and stacked and on a wagon for some village baker, with a bill and a seal on it that would pass any table in the city. A guard could spike every one of them and see nothing in the groove but good grain.

The bell was still going. She went back along the roof on her elbows to the far side of the chimney, and down.

She came back to the post by the streets, at a walk, like anybody going home after the bell. Soot from the chimneys had blacked both her sleeves to the elbow, and the grit off the slates was still stuck to the side of her face; she rubbed at it with her wrist as she went and only spread the soot. Behind the houses the yards had woken for the night shift. Hoist wheels squealed, and lantern light swung across the mouths of the loading lanes whenever a wagon turned in. Out on the street there was nobody but a yellow dog working along the gutter with its nose down, eating spilled grain.

Cyndia sat on the bench outside the post with her bow across her knees. Havel stood in the doorway with his thumbs in his belt. Cyndia got up when she saw her coming, and Havel took his thumbs out of his belt, and before Sylvanas reached them the knock of the staff came down the street from the other end.

Jaina came down it on Lyriel's arm. She held the staff out in front of her and put her weight on it every step, and every few steps she stopped, and Lyriel stopped with her and waited without looking at her. Corin walked on her dead side with the satchel hugged to his chest and one hand held out a finger's width from her sleeve, never quite touching it. Under the new lamp over the bench her face had gone grey to the lips. The crystal at the head of the staff gave no light at all, and the two bare fingertips at the end of her linen were white to the first joint, with frost furred on the cloth round them, on a night so warm the dog was panting.

"Inside," Sylvanas said.

The post was empty. The night men had gone out on the lanes at the bell, Havel said, and wouldn't be back through the door till the Exchange rang again. Someone had trimmed the lamp since the afternoon, and it smoked less, but the grey layer was still lying under the beams. Lyriel sat Jaina on the stool at the end of the table, took her hand off her only when she was sure of her, and went to the window. She pulled the shutter nearly closed and stood at the gap with her shoulder to the frame. Cyndia took the door. The night came in through the gap in the shutter, warm, and sweet with grain.

Corin went into the satchel and came out with a stub of candle. Of course he had one. He lit it at the lamp and stood it on the table in a puddle of its own wax, because the lamp was no good for reading, and then he went in again and brought out a sheet folded once and opened it flat in the candlelight. It wouldn't lie flat. He put the ring of brass tags on one corner of it and the cracked river stone on the other, and a moth came in through the gap in the shutter and landed on the paper, and he moved it off with the back of his inky hand without looking.

The sheet was in his small upright hand, a column down the left side of it: a bill number, a yard's mark drawn small beside it, and one word after. *Heavy. Heavy. Clean. Heavy. Heavy.* The hook with two bars through the shank came again and again down the column. At the top of the sheet, above where his hand began, there were three lines that weren't his: blots, a stroke that had gone through the paper, a word she couldn't make out at all.

"I tried," Jaina said. "Then I talked, and he wrote."

"All the shelves," Corin said. "She did all of the shelves. I said she ought to stop at the first—"

"Corin."

He stopped.

Jaina leaned forward on the staff. Her voice was hoarse, and she spent it carefully. "Every bag on one of Corin's copied marks is bad, Ranger-General. Every one. The bags on true marks are clean."

Corin put his finger on the column, on the first *Heavy*, and then took it off again as if the paper had been hot. He didn't say anything. At this table in the afternoon Havel had sent him red to the ears; now he went white round the mouth instead.

"It isn't mixed into the grain," Jaina said. "It's put on it. A coat, too thin to see. Somebody puts it on by hand, after the grain's dressed and before the sack's sewn up, and works it through so that every grain gets some." She lifted the two white fingertips off the staff and rubbed them together, slowly, as if there were something between them still. "On its own the coat does very little. It's there to carry the rest. Then the bread's eaten, and the body finishes the work." She put the fingertips back on the staff. "That's what's on the shelves. It doesn't tell me whose hands."

Sylvanas took the folded sheet out of the front of her coat, the one she'd carried since Corley's, with Jaina's blots down one side and her own names down the other. She turned it to the clean back and pressed it flat beside Corin's, and got the stub of lead out of her pocket. It was worn so short she had to hold it in her fingertips like a seed. A square for Fenner's yard. A long box for the rope-walk. Eight strokes, put where the eight had stood. A cross on the wall at the mouth of the first loading lane. Then Tenter Row, and the bend, and a back lane, and a second square at the end of it with five strokes inside it and a ring for the crock.

"I have the hands," she said.

She turned the sheet round to face Jaina and put her sooty finger on the second square. "Ashby's. Upper floor. Five men in waxed cloths, gloves to the elbow. One of them has a crock of something black and thick between his feet. He fills his hand from it and puts his arm down into the open sack, to the elbow, and works it there. One sews behind him. One stacks."

Jaina looked at the ring she'd drawn for the crock, and then at her own word on Corin's sheet, the first *Heavy*, and then back.

"With his arm," she said.

"To the elbow."

"Then that's where it goes on." She said it quite flat. "Whoever filled that crock knows his way round an alchemist's bench, and round the other craft as well. Both. That isn't something you'd teach a loader."

"They don't teach the loaders." Sylvanas moved her finger back to Fenner's yard, to the eight strokes, and to the cross on the wall. "These stand sentry. They watch the yard and the lane, and they take turns at the trough so somebody's always facing out. This one"—she touched the single stroke she'd put halfway up the lane—"carries word. He pushed something into that crack with his thumb and walked to Ashby's and went up the stair, and never came a stride nearer the sacks than the top of it. The masked ones never come down. The man at the window washed to the elbow before he'd put a jug to his mouth, and put his cloth back up before he touched a fresh pair of gloves."

Jaina looked at her for a moment. "Then they know what it does."

Sentries in a yard, a runner working the walls, and five men upstairs who never set foot in the lane. That was a company, and somebody had raised it. At Corley's she and Jaina had drawn two pictures on one sheet and argued over them half the night, till Cyndia counted the book and one of them had to be moved. Here nobody had moved a mark. Her crock sat beside Jaina's word, and the two sheets read as one.

"Ashby's," Havel said. He had come round behind Corin to look down at her sheet, and he rubbed the back of one scarred hand with the thumb of the other. "Ashby shut his gate at noon. I'd have sworn to it."

He'd pointed his two fingers past Tenter Row on the way down, too. She didn't say so.

"Well. Then we have them." Havel straightened. There was a stub of chalk on the table in an iron holder, the kind a carpenter keeps, and he picked it up and went to the canvas on the wall. "Now we do it properly. Here's Ashby's." He drew a ring at the foot of Tenter Row, over a dozen old rings that had been rubbed out to grey. "The night crews come off when the Exchange rings the day in. They come out tired, and they come out together. I put four lads at the Hurdle Lane end of the row and four at the weir end, and we close it on them like the neck of a sack." The chalk went down Hurdle Lane from the gate to Tenter Row, along a line already worn pale into the canvas. "I'll have the magistrate's clerk out of his bed and standing at the bend with a lantern, so he sees them come out with the stuff on their gloves. Then there's no letter to the council, and nobody gets them out by dinner."

"They'll be gone by then," Corin said.

"Lad."

"No, I— sorry, Captain, but they will." He went into the satchel and came out with another fold of paper without even looking for it. "Ashby's bills. The office copies. Look at the days on them." He spread them over his own sheet, and the moth went up off the paper and round the candle. "Every bill on Ashby's mark is written for the first carts out. The south road, the weir road. Ashby keeps nothing overnight. Whatever they sew up tonight is on a wagon before your clerk has his boots on, and the crock goes with it, or into the weir. And then we're standing in Tenter Row arresting eight tired men for carrying sacks."

"I'd not call that nothing, lad."

"And there's nobody else." Corin had gone pale again. He kept his hand flat on the bills. "From Dalaran, I mean. I've asked. Every week since the first copied hook, I've asked, Lady Proudmoore knows I have. If we wait and do it properly, we'll do it with nobody, and after they've gone."

He stopped there, and his ears went red, but he didn't take his hand off the bills.

Havel looked at him for a while. Then he rubbed his scarred knuckles and nodded. "The lad's right about the carts. Fair enough." He turned back to the canvas. "So we stop the first carts at the market gate, and I'll have the spikes into every sack on Ashby's mark, and we take the crew at the shift change like I said." The chalk went down Hurdle Lane again, top to bottom, in the same groove.

Sylvanas watched the chalk and not the man.

There it went: Hurdle Lane, for the third time tonight, over the line he'd worn into the canvas all summer. A man who owned a lane drew it like that, over and over, the way a farmer walks the bounds of his own field. Or a careful captain drew it, in a town that wasn't hers, because it was the lane he knew and walked, and she had no better reason to doubt him than that he was slow and she was sick to death of slow men. The Convocation would have loved him. A sensible local officer, mindful of the magistrate, wanting it done properly; they'd have sent him a commendation and her a letter about her manners. That counted against him. It wasn't fair, and she kept it anyway. And the Dalaran boy, who had tried three tags off his ring before he found the right one and pulled out a stool so fast it tipped, had been right every time he opened his mouth.

"Captain," Jaina said.

Havel turned round with the chalk in his fingers. "My lady."

"When your men close the row," Jaina said, "what's left in the loft?"

"The crew. And whatever they've—"

"Not the sacks. Corin's put the sacks on the south road." She didn't look at Corin. "The crock?"

Havel opened his mouth and shut it again.

"Washed out," Corin said quietly. "Or in the weir."

"Seven carts gone down the lane, Ranger-General." Lyriel, from the gap in the shutter, without turning round. "Loaded, every one. And one coming up empty, with the carter asleep on the seat; the horse knows the way better than he does."

Cyndia came in two steps from the door. "Ranger-General. The rope-walk." She put her finger on the long box on Sylvanas's sheet, at the far end of it. "The crew went in at this door when the bell rang. The captain's men watched them in and the lock went on from inside. The pair from the trough went in last, together. The three from the gate didn't go in. They went up Hurdle Lane." She took her finger off the sheet. "Not out of the yard by the front. By the lane."

By the lane, toward the wall with the crack in it, toward Tenter Row. More of them walking her own way in the dark.

Morning, then, with the magistrate's clerk holding a lantern at the bend, and a crew coming out of a scrubbed loft with clean hands. She'd been a day behind them since the miller's buy-back. The depot had been swept before she reached it and the stove stuffed with its own books, and the overseer had smiled and sent her up the wrong road. Every door on the way south had been shut the night before she got there. Was that why she wanted it tonight, then? Because she was sick of arriving to a cold stove? Partly. Mostly, though, it was because by first light there would be nobody left in that loft to take.

"Tonight," she said.

Havel turned from the canvas. "Ranger-General—"

"Tonight, Captain, while they're still sewing." She pulled her belt knife and drove the point through the corner of her sheet into the table, so it would stop curling. "They think the city's theirs, and while they think it they'll leave one man coming and going alone through that door. By first light they won't need to."

Jaina was getting up. Sylvanas heard the heel of the staff grind on the earth floor, and the stool scrape back, and then Jaina came round the end of the table with Lyriel half a step off the shutter to catch her and not needing to. She stopped at Sylvanas's shoulder, on the same side of the table as her, facing Havel across it, and set the staff down there.

"It's wet in that loft tonight," Jaina said. "On the gloves, in the bucket, on the floor round the crock. I can still read it there. Once they've burned the gloves and swilled the boards, all I'll have left is sacks again, and I've read enough sacks."

Sylvanas hadn't asked her. She'd been about to. Jaina had come round the table before she could, and Sylvanas had seen it, and she wasn't going to say anything about it to anybody. She looked at the sheet.

"They don't talk," she said. "Brask's boy had his death sewn into his collar. These will have the same, or worse."

"Then I'll take his jaw first," Jaina said.

"Before he gets a hand to his collar."

"Before he knows he's been taken." Jaina's two white fingertips were flat on the staff, very still. "I can do one. Not two. Give me one man close enough to touch."

One, then. It had to be one man, taken quietly at Ashby's door, with Jaina standing near enough to freeze his jaw and Sylvanas near enough to put him on the ground. Without Jaina's frost they'd have a body by morning and nothing to show for it but another collar. Without Sylvanas, Jaina was a woman with a staff in a white lane between two lamps. It needed both of them there at once.

"Cyndia. The roofs over the back lane, by the chimney across from the loft. Anyone at the windows, anyone on the gables, anyone coming down the lane behind us. You don't loose until I call."

"Ranger-General."

"Lyriel. The bend in Tenter Row. Nobody comes down it at our backs, and when we come out, we come out past you."

"Mine."

Two bows to hold two ends of a lane in a city full of men who drilled at corners. She was the one who'd put them there. She had sent Neral north with the child, and three horses back up the road, and now she was spreading what she had left of the detail so thin she could see light through it, and calling it a perimeter.

"At least let my lads have the Hurdle Lane end, Ranger-General," Havel said. "If it goes wrong, you'll want it shut behind you."

"Keep them there, then."

"I'll see to it." He turned back to the canvas, and the chalk went down Hurdle Lane again.

Corin was already buckling the satchel. He stopped with the strap in his hands. "The loft door on the lane side has a hasp. A long iron one. The inspection office's tag won't open it, but I know the kind of lock that's on it, and—" He swallowed. "I'll come. I can tell you what's on their bills when you've got him, and you'll want that before he's had time to think."

Sylvanas looked at him for a moment, at the ink and the satchel and the bruised eyes. Then she pulled her knife out of the table, and the sheet curled up on itself at once, round her crock and her five strokes.

"Stay behind Proudmoore," she said, and pinched the candle out.
