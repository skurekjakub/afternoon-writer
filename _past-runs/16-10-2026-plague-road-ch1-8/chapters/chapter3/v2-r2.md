# Chapter 3: The Dead Village

At the crossroads the inn sign creaked on its bracket, a dry complaint and a pause and the same complaint again, and nobody came out under it to see who had ridden in.

Sylvanas held the mare to a walk. The hooves rang on the stone, the sound running on ahead between the houses and coming back off the walls, and under it there was nothing but the stream going over the race below the bridge. It was the hour when a village this size should be banking its fires and shouting its children in off the lanes, and every chimney was cold.

The mare blew hard through her nose and went sideways.

Sylvanas sat it, waiting for her to stand. A horse bred at the wards sidestepped once at a stone, thought about it, and stood like a post while her rider laid a bare hand on the rock; this mare had done it at stone after stone without fuss. Now she blew again with her ears pinned flat and backed a step on the cobbles. Sylvanas held her there by the knee and the mouth. Behind them a horse went up. Neral's voice came low and unhurried in Thalassian—*stand now, stand, you great fool*—the same voice she had used in the ditch over the doe. The spare grey answered with a long rattling snort, like a bellows with a split in the leather.

Cyndia's gelding threw its head. The mage's borrowed mare tried to turn for the hill, and Lyriel leaned across, took her by the cheekpiece and brought her round without a word, while the mage rode it out with her left hand wound in the mane and the staff jammed across the saddle-bow under her dead right one.

Sylvanas watched her own mare's ears.

Horses, again. A stone she could read, and a stone would have told her by the cold under its warmth that it was wrong. Here there were only cobbles and thatch, and the horses telling her what her hand could not. Any ranger who took her reports from a horse's ears would walk the outer ring for a month. Well; the horse had the better ears tonight. She would listen to the horse.

The inn door stood open a hand's width, swinging in on the wind from the east road, knocking the frame, swinging out and knocking again. Someone had nailed a horseshoe over the lintel with the points down; humans hung them so when they wanted their luck to pour out over whoever walked in under it. It had not done the door much good.

The wind brought cold hearth-ash and wet thatch and, from some kitchen, the thin sour smell of milk gone off in a crock. No rot under it, and she liked that no better.

She had not slept since the night before the troll hollow. Her eyes were full of sand, her breeches wet at the knees from the ditch, the doe's blood dried brown in her knuckles.

"Down," she said. "Neral, the horses into the inn yard and keep them there. Nose to the wall if they won't settle." She tipped her chin past the inn. "Bridge."

The fourth ranger was already turning that way.

"Nothing comes over it from either side till I call. Cyndia, Lyriel, the east side. Every house, cellar door to roof-tree. You go in together and you come out together, and nobody stands in a doorway with the light behind them."

"Ranger-General."

"Mage. With me."

The mage had got down and stood holding the stirrup leather with her good hand, not ready yet to let go of it. Her face in the last of the light was the colour of tallow. She tipped the staff toward the middle of the crossing, the crystal shivering in its claws.

"Ranger-General, it's right under us. If I put my hand down here now, before it's full dark—"

"Houses first."

"It's strongest *here*."

"Then it will still be here when we've opened the doors."

The mage's mouth shut. She brought her left hand up off the stirrup, palm out, to begin some shape in the air for the slow soldier, then stopped and put it back down. Sylvanas turned away before she had to watch her decide not to argue.

The mage could tell her what lay in the ground under the crossing. She could not tell her who might be standing behind a pantry door with a billhook, and she had one hand that worked, and that one shaking.

She nocked an arrow and took the west side.

The first building past the inn was a wheelwright's, open-fronted, a half-built wheel lying flat on the bench. One spoke was clamped in the vice, shaved round on one side and still square on the other, the drawknife laid across it with its handles squared to the edge of the bench. Sylvanas drew a finger along the blade and it came away grey; dust lay on the steel fine as flour, and on the handles where a man's palms should have kept them bright.

He had stopped in the middle of a spoke, in the middle of the day with the light still good, and laid the knife down straight, the way a man lays down a tool he means to pick up again after he has eaten. He had not come back for it.

She had a drill for a steading burned out by trolls and a drill for a yard with the dead lying in it. What drill did she have for a drawknife set down tidy?

Open the doors.

The house behind it was one long room with a loft. The table was laid: four bowls, a spoon in each, a loaf gone hard enough to ring if she knocked it, a wedge of cheese sweated and dried and cracked across the top. The pot on the hook over the hearth had a skin on whatever was in it, dark and leathery. Cyndia had given her a farmhouse in the same words on the road that afternoon: the door on the latch, the bowls on the table, a spoon in each. Here it was again with more chairs.

"Stay at the door," she told the mage. "Where I can see your hands."

Sylvanas went up the loft ladder with her knife out and put her head over the edge into the dark. Two straw pallets, the blankets folded square. A shutter stood open on the night and the loft was as cold as the yard. Nobody.

In the next house a porridge pot stood by the hearth with a crust dried on it, one bowl scraped clean beside it, the beds unmade. The next had its shutters open in every room, in this cold, and a bed turned down, a basin on the floor beside it, a rag folded over the rim, a cup with a little water still in it. Someone had sat by that bed, and wrung that rag out and folded it, and gone to fill the cup.

She stood in that one longer than the others.

Every peg by every door had a coat on it. She went back and checked. The coats were on the pegs and the bread on the boards, and the boots and the people were gone. Nobody had broken a door in. There was no blood on any threshold; she had read each one as she would a trail, low, with the light slanting. No pile of dead anywhere, burned or unburned. No dogs. In a village this size there should have been a dog in every third yard, and she had not found one, not one chain with a dog gone off the end of it.

After the Amani came through a steading you could read it from the gate: the door broken in, the thatch fallen, the people where they had stood to fight or turned to run. Here everybody had got up from what they were doing, one house at breakfast and the next at supper and the next at a sickbed, each at a different hour, and gone out of the door and shut it.

Where had they gone?

Fled, then. People who flee take the coat, and the bread and the cow and the child's toy, and leave a trail a blind man could follow. Taken, then. Taken by whom, with every door whole and no hoofprint in the lanes but their own?

Or they had got up and walked out.

The doe had run on with Sylvanas's arrow through her heart, strings of blood from her nose, and Sylvanas had put that away to think about when there was time. There was no time. The doe had been alive when she ran; the mage had said so, with her arm in the doe to the elbow, *it was in her while she lived*. A doe was a doe. A wheelwright was something else.

Movement at the corner of the lane.

She had the string half-drawn. A big brindled barn cat came out from between two garden walls with its tail straight up and crossed the lane without a glance, thick through the shoulders, its coat clean. It went up the step of the house with the basin and began to wash a hind leg with its toes spread.

She eased the string.

The cat looked better fed than her rangers and did not care in the least who had gone out of this village or how.

Under the table of the last house on the west side there was a wooden horse with a short leg, carved by somebody who had never studied a horse. Sylvanas picked it up because things on floors get trodden on in the dark and set it on the table between the bowls, already annoyed with herself.

When she came back to the crossing it was nearly dark. Cyndia and Lyriel were waiting by the well with their bows in hand, watching the lanes.

"Ranger-General." Cyndia stood with her weight even. "East side's clear. Every house, the smithy, the inn's back rooms and loft. No one. No blood, no broken doors. Food on most tables, beds made in most, some shutters open. The smithy fire was out and the ash cold to the bottom. I'd say they went a few at a time."

"Coats?"

"On the pegs."

"Dogs?"

"None. And no chains."

Lyriel shifted her bow to the other hand. "There's a cradle in the house by the smithy with a blanket in it and no baby, Ranger-General. The blanket's been tucked." Her voice was quiet.

Sylvanas stood at the well with the cold coming up through her wet knees. No bodies. Nothing to turn over, nothing to measure, no wound to tell her teeth or blade or fever.

Nothing to read, then. Fine; the mage. She was already getting up from the horse trough in the inn yard when Sylvanas turned.

"Now," Sylvanas said.

The well stood at the dead middle of the crossing, four roads running away from it into the dark. She would not have put a sick mage on her knees there for any money in Silvermoon, so she made it better. Lyriel went up the outside stair of the inn to the gallery, where the roof covered her back and she had the north road and the west. Cyndia took the corner of the smithy wall, covering the east road and the lane behind it. Sylvanas walked a tipped handcart back out of the mouth of the south road so the fourth ranger on the bridge could see straight up to the well, and stood with her back to the well-head, her bow in her hand.

"Here," she said.

"Water first." The mage glanced at the windlass, at her right hand, at Sylvanas. "Would you? I can't—"

Slinging the bow, Sylvanas took the handle. The rope was damp all the way up, slick with the green that grows on a well rope. The windlass gave a long iron squeal that went out across the empty crossing and came back off the inn front. She stopped. Nothing answered. She wound on a quarter-turn at a time and brought the bucket up slopping over its lip.

The mage put her left hand in to the wrist and shut her eyes.

"It's in the water." She opened them. "Thinner than in the doe. It's in everything here a little, I think, and some things a great deal more." She wiped her hand on her robe and went down on her knees beside the well, laying it flat between two stones. "The same as this morning. If I don't come up—"

"I'll call you."

"Louder than last time, please. I went further than I meant to. I need to hear all of them at once, every line coming in. That's further down than the culvert."

"Then go."

The mage bent her head and went.

Sylvanas watched the roads, then the mage kneeling with the staff across her thighs, then the roads again. She did not like having her back to the inn door. It went on knocking.

The crystal began to buzz, a fine high whine at the top of her hearing, a gnat at night, until it was a blur in its claws. Frost came out from under the mage's palm. It ran into the joints between the cobbles first, white threads, then up over the stones, until it lay round the mage's hand on the road in a white plate. In the inn yard a horse squealed, high and ugly, Neral's voice going on under it without a break.

The mage was very still, the steam of her breath nearly stopped.

"Mage."

Nothing.

"Proudmoore."

Nothing. The frost crept another finger's width toward the well.

"*Proudmoore!*"

It cracked off the inn front and the smithy and rang back across the crossing. The mage did not lift her head. Sylvanas went down on one knee, took her by the wrist and pulled the hand up off the road. It came with a small tearing sound. A grey print of skin stayed on the frosted stone, four fingertips and the heel of a palm, and the mage jerked upright, gasping, and doubled over her knees.

"I'm—" She coughed. "Light. Yes. I'm here."

Sylvanas let go of her wrist and stood up. The mage knelt with her left hand held off the ground, palm up, shaking, frost grey at the edges of it. The skin had come away from the pads of three fingers and left them raw and shining.

"Well?"

"They're all heavy. Every line that comes in here." The mage's voice was scraped thin, but it came out in order. "If it were rising here, it would be thickest under the crossing and thin out the same way down every line. It doesn't. Two of them are heavier a long way out than they are under us."

"Then it's draining in."

"Then the far ends would be late, each line by how far it had travelled. The two from the north are. The east one doesn't lag at all. The west one I couldn't hear. It's so thick under the crossing that it drowns the lines out."

"Which is it?"

"I don't know." The mage held the raw hand cupped off the stones. "I told you on the road I would. I'm sorry, Ranger-General. There's more of it here than anywhere I've touched since Dalaran. I can't tell you which way it's running."

*I don't know.* It was a straight answer, at least, and Sylvanas had not had many of those from a mage in her life.

"Where's it worst?"

The mage got the staff upright with her elbow and the heel of her bad hand and turned it slowly from one road to the next. The crystal rattled, hardest toward the bridge and the mill.

"Down there. And under us, somewhere. There's bound to be a cellar under the inn. Anywhere things are kept."

The inn cellar was behind the bar, a trapdoor with an iron ring. Sylvanas took the lantern Neral had lit in the kitchen and went down the ladder first with her knife out. The air came up warmer than the night, close and stale, smelling of spilled beer gone sour and apples going soft in their straw. She went down the row of barrels, behind each one and into the dark under the stair. Nobody.

"Come down."

The mage tried the ladder with one hand round the staff and the other no use for anything. Sylvanas went up two rungs and held out her hand. "Give it here."

In her fist the staff buzzed, a fine hard tremor going into the bones of her hand and not stopping. Her own palm told her nothing; it was a stick, and cold. She handed it back the moment the mage was at the bottom.

The mage set the heel of it on the earth floor and leaned her ear to the wood the way Sylvanas had once seen a horse-doctor lean to listen at a gut.

"Worse than the well," she said. "Worse again down by the mill."

The storehouse stood on the near side of the bridge beside the mill, a stone building with a slate roof and a door wide enough for a cart. Sylvanas put Cyndia in the mill yard and Lyriel on the bridge with the fourth ranger, propped the far door open with a sack to give herself a clean line through the building to the water, and let the mage in.

Grain dust came up as soon as they walked on it, pale and soft as ash, the lantern turning the air into a slow gold fog. Sacks stood stacked to the height of her shoulder. A bin of loose grain stood open by the door with the wooden scoop still in it, the handle standing up out of the heap. Somebody had been filling sacks.

The mage went down on her knees in the middle of the floor.

"Not the hand," Sylvanas said. "Use the staff."

"The staff only tells me where it's loud. I need to hear what it's doing." She laid her left hand flat on the boards, raw fingers and all. Her whole arm flinched before she bent her head.

The crystal rattled in its claws hard enough to knock, a dry clicking, a beetle in a box. Frost spread from under her palm across the boards, the dust taking it and going grey, further than it had at the well. Sylvanas stood in the doorway where she could cover the mage, the yard and the far door all at once, sweeping the yard and coming back to the frost.

"Mage."

The mage lifted her head at once, white to the lips. She left skin on the boards again without seeming to notice.

"This is the loudest place in Millhaven," she said. "Louder than the crossing."

"Then this is where it started."

"I can't tell you that."

"You can tell me it's loudest."

"Yes."

"The loudest place on your whole map, and you won't call it."

"No." The mage sat back on her heels in the dust, holding up her hand. "Ranger-General, a store is where things end up. Everything a village has goes into a place like this sooner or later, and whatever's wrong with that village goes in with it. I can hear how much of it is here, and that's all I can hear. Which came first, I can't, not from this. If I tell you it started here and it came from a day's ride off, you'll ride the wrong way, and so will I."

Sun's sake. Sylvanas had expected the mage to grab it with both hands. Any magister in Silvermoon would have had it on paper before the dust settled—*source of the contamination, the Millhaven granary*, signed and sealed. And here was the mage with her palm gone grey, kneeling in the middle of the loudest thing she had ever found, saying no to it. It was the right answer. The ground in a clearing that was trampled deepest was where the herd had milled about, never where the herd came in. She had walked Cyndia round a clearing again and again, as a recruit, until she saw it.

Damn her, then. The hand.

"Close it."

The mage tried. The fingers curled halfway and stopped, stiff as a dead bird's foot. The right hand was still tucked up useless in her armpit.

"You're done reading with those tonight."

"I can still use the staff."

"You can still hold the staff, which isn't the same." Sylvanas stepped back out of the doorway into the yard. "How much is here, you've told me. Which way it went, you can't."

"No."

"Then I'll find out which way it went. Cyndia. Bring the lantern."

Behind the gardens on the east side a lane ran along the backs of the houses, between the garden walls and the first field. When Cyndia lowered the lantern to it Sylvanas stopped at the end.

The lane was gone. Something had been over it the way cattle go over a gateway in a wet spring, again and again, until the mud stood in ridges and holes a boot deep from one wall to the other. Water lay black in the holes, holding the lantern. A garden wall had lost its top course into the lane. Past that a gate lay flat in the mud with its hinges still screwed to it and splinters of the post hanging off them. It had been pushed outward until the post gave. Nobody had lifted the latch.

"Keep the light low," Sylvanas said. "Off the field."

Cyndia hooded the lantern in a fold of her cloak until only a slit of light came out along the ground and went down on her heels at the edge of the churn. Sylvanas crouched beside her, keeping her own shadow off the prints, and out of old habit laid her right palm flat on the mud. Cold and wet and gritty, and her eyes were a beat behind the light tonight.

Cyndia moved the light a hand's width at a time, reading as she went.

"Feet," she said. "Boots. Clogs. Bare, here." She held the slit over a print, long and flat, the toes spread, the heel pressed as deep as the ball. "Bare again here. This one drags the right toe. There's a furrow in front of every print; he never lifted it." She moved the light. "That's a longer drag. Could be a foot. Could be something pulled along. I can't tell you which."

"Count."

"I can't. They're on top of each other, deep, and they've gone back over their own tracks, and there's been rain in them since. I'd rather not give you a number and then take it back."

Sylvanas had put that answer into her herself, years ago, over a muddier ford than this.

A little way down the lane a wooden clog stood up out of the mud on its own, a man's, sucked down to the instep and standing upright where the foot had come out of it. The print after it was bare. So was the next, and the one after that, on into the churn until she lost it under somebody else's.

Nobody with feeling in his feet walks out of his clog in autumn mud and keeps going. He stops, and swears, and hops back on one leg and digs for it with his fingers. This one had stepped out of it and walked on barefoot with the cold coming up into him and had not cared.

The doe had not cared either. She had come on with a split heart, alive when she ran. Were these alive when they walked? A man walks out of his shoe if he's drunk. If he's mad. If he's—

Don't. Don't make them into the doe because it's late and you want a story to put on it.

"Mage."

The mage came over slowly from the corner of the wall, picking her way round the holes.

"The prints. Can you hear anything in them without your hands?"

Leaning on the staff, the mage set its heel in the bare print after the clog and bent her head to the wood. She lifted the staff and set it on the bank under the garden wall, where the mud was smooth and nobody had trodden, and listened there before she went back to the print.

"It's in the prints," she said. "Heavier where they stepped than in the ground round them. A good deal heavier. The way it was in the doe." She swallowed. "But there's no doe here to put a hand in. I'd want one of them. I'd want to know if they were—"

"Yes."

Sylvanas stood up, wiping her palm down her thigh. A great many of them had gone out on their own feet, empty-handed and at a walk, through the gates instead of over them and into the dark.

"They walked out," she said.

The slit of lantern light lay on the mud.

"Which way?" the mage said.

Beyond the lantern the field was a black slope with nothing on it she could read. Every way out of the lane was trodden over and trodden again coming back, if anything had come back. Rain since. Full dark, and the lantern smearing gold over everything she tried to see.

North. Everything in her wanted to say north. Her line was north, and the stones were north, and the noon stone with the cold under its warmth that she had carried in her palm since yesterday. So she would not say it. Call it north and be wrong, and she sent a rider to Silvermoon to turn every bow on the wall the wrong way. And what would she be calling it on? A clog and a feeling.

"I don't know," she said. "Not on this ground. Not in the dark."

Cyndia lowered the lantern a little.

Somebody had stood at a window or a door or a gap in a wall in this village while it went out of its gates and marked which way it turned at the end of the lane, and that somebody was the only clean track she was going to get.

She put two fingers to her mouth and whistled, short, twice. Boots came down the lanes between the gardens: Lyriel off the bridge with the fourth ranger behind, and Neral last, calling something soft over her shoulder to the horses she had left tied in the yard.

"Back to the houses," Sylvanas said. "All of them, both sides. Every cellar, every loft. The bread ovens. Under the beds and inside the chests, and the space behind the chimney breast where they keep the hams."

"We did the houses, Ranger-General," Lyriel said. She was asking.

"You did the rooms. Now do the places a person would fold up small and keep quiet." Sylvanas took the lantern from Cyndia. "Somebody in this village watched them go. Find them."

She turned back up the lane toward the dark houses with the light held low against her thigh, hearing them come after her.

Neral found it in the end house.

The end house stood where the garden walls gave out, its back wall right on the lane. Cyndia had been through it on the first sweep and called it empty: one room, a box bed against the far wall, a hearth with the ash gone grey. Sylvanas was up a ladder in the loft of the house next door, holding the lantern out over the rafters, when Neral said her name through the wall.

"Ranger-General." Then, in exactly the same voice: "In here, please."

Sylvanas came down the ladder two rungs at a time and went round by the lane.

Neral knelt on the floor of the end house with both hands spread flat on the boards in front of her, the way she laid her hands on a horse's neck before she asked it for anything. The box bed had been pulled out from the wall and stood skewed across the room on its short legs. Where it had stood there were two long pale scrapes in the floor and, between them, a square hatch set flush with the boards, an iron ring let into it.

"It was over this," Neral said, not turning her head. "The bed. Somebody dragged it across from up here, you can see where the legs went. I tried the ring. It's bolted underneath."

"Underneath."

"And then I heard it." Neral's voice went lower still. "There's somebody breathing down there."

Sylvanas set the lantern on the table between two bowls with a soft grey fur grown over the dregs in them and a spoon stuck upright in the nearer. Five days, perhaps.

Bolted underneath, and the bed dragged over it from above. Two hands: one down there to draw the bolt, one up here to drag the bed across and walk away. After days in the dark, what was still breathing down there? The doe had breathed, with an arrow through her heart.

Don't. A doe is a doe. Find out.

She went to the door. Cyndia was in the lane with her back to the wall and an arrow on the string. Sylvanas kept her voice down.

"Lyriel to the top of the lane. The fourth to the front, on the road. Then bring me the mage."

Cyndia went. Sylvanas came back into the room, put an arrow on her own string and stood off from the hatch a long step back and to the side, where she could cover the ring, the door and Neral's back all at once. Neral knew she was there, and kept her back to her and her hands flat on the boards.

The mage came in with the staff hooked in the crook of her elbow, holding her bad hands up in front of her.

"There's somebody under the floor," Sylvanas said. "Is there anything else down there with them?"

The mage got down on her knees beside Neral and set the heel of the staff against the boards and bent her head to the wood. Her mouth pressed thin and stayed that way.

"It's loud," the mage said. "The floor's loud. The whole street is. If there's something down there carrying more of it than the ground does, I can't pick it out through the boards." She lifted her head. "I'd have to be in there with it."

"Not yet."

Neral had begun to talk.

She talked low to the hatch in Thalassian, the long easy run of nothing that Sylvanas had heard her use on the bay in the ambush with a wolf under its feet. *Easy now. Easy. Nobody's going to hurt you. Stand, now.* The sound of it went down through the cracks, warm and unhurried. After a while she changed to the common tongue without changing the voice at all.

"We're not from here. We came down off the hill on horses. I've got water."

Nothing came up. The lantern hissed on the table. Down in the lane the mill race went on and on.

Then, from under the floor, a whisper scraped down to nothing:

"Say it again."

Neral said it again. The Thalassian first, then the rest. *We came down off the hill. I've got water.*

"You talk funny."

"I do," Neral said.

A long quiet. Sylvanas's fingers sat on the string. She made them lie still there.

"Da said not to open it." The whisper broke in the middle and went on. "Not for anybody. Not if it was him."

*Not if it was him.* What kind of man tells his child that and then goes out of his own door into the dark? A man who'd already seen something come back, and knew what was going to walk in.

"Your da was right," Neral said.

"Is he there?"

Neral turned her head at last. Sylvanas shook hers, once.

"There's nobody up here but us," Neral said to the floor. "Five of us, and a lady with a stick, and some horses."

A boot heel would split that hatch, pine and not new. And then what? A child in the black at the bottom of a hole with the lid smashed in over her and an elf's boot coming down the ladder. She'd be in the far corner of it, all teeth. Let Neral do it.

Neral unstoppered the water skin and tipped it onto the boards beside the hatch. The water ran along the floor to the crack round the lid and went down it with a small, quick trickling. It pattered on something underneath.

"That's water," Neral said. "There's more."

A scuffle under the floor, a stop. Then the bolt began to move, a long dry scrape of iron that stopped halfway, and a small sound of someone sobbing without the breath to do it properly, and the scrape again. The hands down there were too weak for it. Neral did not touch the ring. The bolt went back the last of the way with a clack.

"I'm going to lift the lid now," Neral said. "Slowly. I'm going to put the light at the side, so it isn't in your eyes."

She lifted the hatch a finger's width and waited, then a hand's width, and laid it back on its hinges against the box bed without a sound. She set the lantern on the floor at the side of the hole and slid the shutter half across. Sylvanas brought the bow up, the point sitting low on the dark below the rim. If anything came up that ladder that should not, it would come up into Neral's face, and Neral was kneeling with her hands open and no weapon in either.

The air that came up out of the hole was cold earth and damp and piss and turnips, the staleness of the inn cellar, but closer, breathed over and over. At the bottom of the ladder the light caught a heap of pale turnips from a spilled bin, and a face behind the heap ducking away from it. A child's face, smeared, with a mouth too dark at the corners.

"I'm going to come down the ladder now," Neral said. "I'm going to sit on the bottom of it. That's all."

She went down backwards a rung at a time and sat on the bottom one. The face came no closer and went no further back.

"Mage. Go down after her and tell me what else is in it."

The mage lay on her stomach on the boards and put her legs over the edge of the hole. She went down on her elbows and the heel of her left hand and the toes of her boots, and dropped the last of the way into the dirt. Sylvanas handed the staff down after her and let go as soon as the mage's elbow had it.

The mage sat at the foot of the ladder, set the heel of the staff in the earth floor, listened, moved it, listened again. Into one corner. Into the next. Into the spilled turnips. She kept the turnips between herself and the child. Sylvanas kept the bow on the dark round the edges of the lantern light and listened to the door at her back.

"Nothing down here but her," the mage said at last. Her voice came up hollow out of the hole. "It's in the ground, the same as everywhere. Nothing that's been in here was carrying it."

Nothing that's been in here. The man had not come back down to her.

Sylvanas lowered the bow.

"Cyndia." Cyndia was in the doorway; she had been there for some time. "Your water."

Cyndia unhooked her skin from her belt and put it in Sylvanas's hand. Sylvanas went down on one knee at the edge of the hole and held it down at arm's length. The mage reached up with her left hand, but the fingers would not close on the strap. It slid. Neral took it out of the air under the mage's hand.

"Small sips," Neral said into the dark. "There's more where that came from."

It was a long time before the face came round the heap of turnips, on hands and knees. A thin arm, a fist clenched round a scrap of blue cloth, and then two hands round the neck of the skin with Neral's hand under them both, holding the weight. The girl drank too fast, coughing. Neral took the skin away and gave it back and took it away again, and said *small* in Thalassian every time.

"Are they gone?" The whisper had a little more in it now, wet and hoarse.

"Who?" Neral said.

"All of them."

Neral waited.

"Mam died," the child said. "And then in the night she—"

She stopped. She put her mouth back on the skin and would not take it away, and Neral let her keep it.

In the night she what? You didn't ask a child that at the bottom of a hole with a lantern in her eyes. You asked it later, above ground, and you asked it of the ground as well.

"I'm going to lift you up now," Neral said. "I'm going to carry you up the ladder, and there's a blanket up there. You can bring that." She meant the blue scrap. "You hold on to my neck."

The child did. Neral came up the ladder one-handed with the girl's arms locked round her neck and the girl's face pressed into her collar. The arm Neral had under her was the arm she would have used for a saddle, and it bore no weight at all. Eight years old, perhaps. Nine. Hair matted flat against her head. One stocking on, the other foot bare and black to the ankle, and the stocking had a darn at the heel in a different wool from the rest, a brown in a grey, done close and neat.

Neral sat down on the edge of the box bed with the girl in her lap and pulled the blanket round both of them. The girl turned her face into Neral's shoulder, hiding it from everyone else in the room. The blue scrap stayed in her fist.

Sylvanas put herself by the door, half in and half out, where she could watch the lane both ways and the room at once, and gave Neral the room.

Neral asked her name first. The girl shook her head against Neral's collar, and Neral let the name go. She gave her the skin, took it back, gave it her, keeping quiet in between.

"Who was sick first?"

A long breath. "Mister Pell."

"Mister Pell."

"No." The girl's face came a little way out of the collar. "Missus Hale's baby. Then Mister Pell. Then everybody was coughing at the well, and Mam said don't stand near them."

"The baby first," Neral said. "Then Mister Pell. Then everybody at the well."

The girl nodded, her fist clenched round the scrap of blue so hard that the knuckles had gone white under the dirt.

"Did your mam get sick?"

Another nod. "Her breath went sweet."

"Sweet," Neral said.

"Pears gone brown." The girl said it to Neral's shoulder, very matter of fact, the one thing in all this that made no sense to her. "She said she'd been at the pears. We didn't have any pears."

On the floor by the hatch the mage lifted her head. She was sitting with her back to the wall, her hands upturned on her knees, so still that Sylvanas had nearly forgotten her.

"Was it before she was ill, the sweetness?" The mage's voice was pitched down nearly as low as Neral's, and slower than she ever spoke on the road. "Or after?"

The girl turned to Neral for it, and Neral tipped her head at the mage.

"Before," the girl said. "Before she was hot."

The mage had nothing to write with, and no hand to write it with if she had.

Sweet breath. A drinker's breath went sweet, and so did a dying man's sometimes, in the bad fevers. It meant nothing to Sylvanas. It might mean something to the mage.

"Who came to the door, the last days?" Neral said.

The girl thought about it. She had some rhythm now that the first words were out. "Gran Ottley, to sit with Mam. The newer grain seller—Da had words with Mam about him, because he was cheaper than Mister Hollis. The tinker, for the pot."

"What grain seller?" the mage said.

"The new one." The girl's shoulder came up and went down under the blanket.

Sylvanas turned it over once. A cheaper sack and a quarrel in a kitchen over the price of it. Every village south of the Pass had a new man with a cart once a season, and a husband and wife with words to say about him. She put it at the back of her head with the hoofprints she couldn't place, and let it lie.

"And then?" Neral said.

"Mam died."

The mage spoke before Neral could. "How did you know?"

Neral turned her head.

"Da held the glass to her mouth." The girl's voice did not change at all. "His shaving glass. It didn't go misty."

"Thank you," the mage said quietly. *That one's true.* A frightened child didn't make up a shaving glass.

"And then she got up," the girl said. She said it more quietly than anything yet. "In the dark. And Da put me down the cellar, and said bolt it. Not for anybody. Not for him."

"And you did," Neral said.

A nod.

"What did you hear?"

"Feet." The fist on the blue cloth clenched and unclenched. "Going past. And past. Dragging. Up the back."

The hole under the hatch was a cellar dug into clay, with a ladder and a turnip bin, nothing in it to tell a child down there which way anything was going in the world above her. Sylvanas spoke in Thalassian, to Neral, in her ordinary voice, so it would come to the girl as noise.

"Ask her how she knows which way. She was under the floor."

Neral slowed down and asked her in the common tongue.

"The little window," the girl said, pointing down into the hole without turning her head.

Sylvanas took the lantern from the floor and went down on one knee at the edge of the hatch, holding it down at arm's length. There on the far side, in the house's back wall where it stood on the lane, right up under the beams, was a slot a hand high and two hands long, with iron bars across it and a crust of dirt along the sill. A cellar light, to keep the turnips from going damp. From the top of the turnip bin a child could have put her face to it and seen the lane at the height of a dog's nose.

Feet going by in the dark.

"Which way were the toes pointing?" the mage said from the wall. "Toward the mill? Or away from it?"

The girl thought. A small furrow came and went over the dirt between her eyebrows.

"Away. Up toward Pell's."

"How many?" Neral said.

"All of them." The girl's voice cracked suddenly, wide open, and she pulled it back together the way a child pulls a coat shut. "Hundreds. All night. Every night."

A few dozen houses, a mill and an inn. There had never been hundreds of anything in Millhaven but hens.

"Every night," the mage said, "or one night?"

The girl did not know. She had slept and woken, and slept and woken, and the slot at the top of the wall had gone grey and gone black she did not know how many times. She had bitten turnips and licked the wet off the cellar wall, and there was dirt in the corners of her mouth in a ring.

"It was always dark," the girl said.

"That's all right," Neral said. She shifted the girl a little on her lap and waited before she asked the next thing. "Which way did they go?"

"Up the north road."

Sylvanas went still in the doorway.

The north road. She had ridden down it into this village at a walk, an hour before dusk, her bow in her hand, with every ranger she had in a line round the mage. Cyndia had gone down on her knees at the holly bend that afternoon and read the bank: deer and wolves and not one human foot. A village's worth of dead walk up a road, they leave it looking like that lane. That road had been clean. So the child was wrong, or had seen the wrong night, or her own mother's feet over and over with her eyes shut; or—

"Which is the north road?" Sylvanas said, in the common tongue, flat, and the girl's shoulders came up under the blanket at the sound of her. Too late to take it back.

Neral put her hand on the back of the girl's head. "Which way is it? You can point."

The girl took one arm out of the blanket and pointed, without turning, at the back wall of the house. Out at the lane. Up.

"Past Pell's," she said. "Up the cow way."

It was not the road down from the Pass, and Sylvanas had never been up it.

"Cyndia." Cyndia was still in the lane behind her. "Did you see anything leave the top of the lane? North, out of the field?"

"No, Ranger-General. The lane runs into the field at the top. I lost it there in the dark." Then: "There was a gate at the top. I didn't go through it."

The girl's arm pointed north; the toes in the slot pointed north. The noon stone with the cold under its warmth, which Sylvanas could feel in her palm still if she let herself, pointed north, because she wanted it to. She had a child's word, given through the bars of a cellar light, by a child who did not know one night from the next, about a road Sylvanas had never seen.

"That's enough tonight," she said.

Neral nodded. She had already started to rock the girl. Over the girl's head she spoke in Thalassian, in the brisk voice she used for rangers and tack.

"Give me the tube and the spare grey, Ranger-General. I'll change at the Pass and keep going."

"At first light."

Neral waited, the way she would wait on a horse that had refused a ditch before she put it at the ditch again.

"First light," Sylvanas said. "When I've seen the cow way."

They moved to the inn, which had a yard with a wall round it and a gallery over the door above all four roads at once. The girl was not going to sleep in the end house with its hole in the floor. Neral carried her up the lane wrapped in the blanket, the blue scrap still in her fist, and by the inn sign her head went loose on Neral's shoulder, all at once, between one step and the next.

In the yard the mage stopped by the horse lines.

"I said I'd look at them tonight."

Sylvanas had forgotten. That afternoon, with the doe still steaming in the ditch, the mage had said it, and Sylvanas had told her she would. She had been awake so long that the doe in the ditch felt like last week.

"Then look."

She held the lantern up so the light ran down the line. The mage went along it with the staff in the crook of her elbow, setting the heel of it between each horse's forefeet and leaning her cheek to the wood. Each horse put its ears back at the clicking and forward again before it stood. The grey, with the tooth-score on his near hock scabbed over black, she listened to longest. Sylvanas's mare last.

"Clean," the mage said. "All of them. Tired horses, nothing else."

"Mine."

"Clean." The mage straightened slowly, swaying, and put the staff down to steady herself. "Same as this afternoon. I don't understand it. They've walked through it all day and drunk out of the stream at the culvert, and it isn't in them. I'll be glad of it, and not understand it."

Sylvanas put her hand flat on her mare's neck. Warm and damp under the mane, the big slow pulse in it.

"Cyndia," she said. "Wrap the mage's hands."

She set the watch. Lyriel and the fourth ranger first, the gallery and the yard gate; Cyndia and herself after; Neral not at all. Neral lay down on the taproom settle with the girl against her chest and the blanket over both of them, asleep before her head was all the way down.

Sylvanas did not sleep. She went up on the gallery with Lyriel when Lyriel's watch started and stayed after it ended. Cyndia came up and stood at the other corner. She gave Lyriel the girl's words in a low voice, all of them, in order, and Lyriel heard them out with her face to the north road.

Nothing in the whole village had a light in it. She had seen dead villages before, cold hearths and the doors kicked in, but she had never sat in the middle of one in the dark and listened to it, and she had not known it would be so loud. The race. The water went on coming past the still wheel and under the bridge, white even now, the same pitch hour after hour.

What she had to send north was a child's word, through a grating, in the dark. *Feet. Up the north road. Hundreds, every night.* She thought of the chamber, and the magister's nephew with his figures, and Lor'themar on the bench being charming for her. They'd hold it up to the lamp. *And the Ranger-General saw this herself?* No. She saw a clog in the mud. She'd have had the hide off any ranger who sent a warning on that.

But if it was true, every soul who had lived in Millhaven was walking toward her line in the dark this minute while she sat on a gallery waiting for the sun. Neral could be on the hill road by now with the whole night in hand. And say what? *North.* They'd turn every bow on the wall to the Pass road, which was clean, while the thing came over the hills somewhere else, somewhere the child called the cow way.

Send it with the cow way in it. She had not seen the cow way. Go and see it in the dark, on ground trodden to soup? Cyndia couldn't read the lane with a lantern.

Below her in the yard the mage sat on the mounting block with her back to the wall, her hands in their strips of somebody's linen laid palm up in her lap, her head tipped back against the stones. Her eyes were open. Every so often the steam of her breath went up past her face and was gone.

She had been awake as long as Sylvanas had. Longer. She had crossed the rings on foot before dawn yesterday, sat in a cell all day with a damper in the wall, ridden all night on a borrowed mare she couldn't sit, and put her hands into the road and a dead deer and a storehouse floor. She sat there with her eyes open, waiting for the sun like Sylvanas, so she could go and look at something.

Go to bed, mage. Sylvanas didn't say it. She watched the north road, empty, and the east road, empty, the race going on below the bridge. Her hand was on the string again. She took it off; she had not felt it go there.

---

The gate at the top of the lane was a five-barred field gate, oak gone silver, standing open.

Somebody had swung it back against the bank and dropped the loop of rope over the post to hold it, neat, the way a drover holds a gate for cattle he means to bring through. Sylvanas stood beside it in the first grey light with her breath going up white and studied that loop of rope. A living man's hand, some morning before all this. It meant nothing. She went through.

Behind the gate the ground fell away into a cut, a hollow way worn down by carts and cattle for longer than the village had had a name, so deep in places that the banks stood higher than her head, hazel and thorn meeting over it. Under the hazel it was still nearly night. At the mouth, where the light came in, the floor was yellow clay, scored from one bank to the other.

Grooves. Long ones, side by side, running up the cut and on under the hazel into the dark, as if something had dragged a harrow up it with the tines set close. Dozens of them. Between them the clay was pocked with prints, the grooves running out of their toes.

Sylvanas pulled the glove off her right hand with her teeth and went down on her heels at the edge of the nearest one. Cyndia came through the gate behind her, and then the mage, with the staff in the crook of her elbow and her hands in their linen held up against her chest. Sylvanas drew two fingertips up the lip of the groove.

Rain had rounded the edge, but the bottom was smooth and glazed, a hard skin on the clay where a weight had gone over it and not let up. She measured the depth against her first knuckle, then moved up a pace and measured again. The same. A man drags his toe when he's tired, and the groove comes and goes as his foot lifts and drops. This foot had never lifted. It had come up the cut with the toe down in the clay and the weight on it the whole way, the next one beside it the same, a forearm's width off, and the next.

"It's a drove way, Ranger-General." Cyndia had come down on her heels at Sylvanas's shoulder, her voice level and careful. "They'd bring the beasts up here to the hill grazing in spring, and down again about now. Half of this will be cattle. And the carts after them, and the dogs." She put her own finger near a print, not in it. "I can't tell you how much is people. I'd be guessing."

"Then don't guess." Sylvanas turned to the mage. "Mage. Can you tell her which marks are which?"

The mage studied the cut. In the grey light her face had a greenish tinge round the mouth, and she swallowed, and swallowed again.

"It's loud here," she said. "Louder than the lane. The whole cut's soaked in it." She came down the bank on her heels and her backside and got her feet under her at the bottom. "But it was heavier in the prints than the ground, in the lane. If it's the same here—" She set the heel of the staff into the toe of the nearest print and bent her cheek to the wood. The crystal clicked. She lifted it and set it a hand's width off, in a cloven slot pressed deep and clean into the clay, and listened there.

"That's heavy," she said. "The first one. That one's light, as light as the bank. Whatever made that was alive when it made it."

"A cow," Cyndia said.

The mage moved the staff along, into a print, into a slot, into a wheel rut half full of water, into a long smear that might have been anything. "Heavy. Light. Light. Heavy." The rut was light. A dog's pad on the bank was light. The long smear was heavy, and so was every groove the staff went into. She said each one aloud in a flat scraped voice, and Cyndia turned her head to each mark as she said it.

"Keep on," Sylvanas said. "Cyndia, count the heavy."

Cyndia broke a hazel switch off the bank, stripped it with her thumbnail and laid it across the cut from bank to bank. She took one ordinary step up the cut and laid a second across it, so the two made a box on the clay as wide as the cut and as long as a man's step. Every one of them that had walked up here would have put a foot down inside it once. The mage went along inside the box with the staff, and Cyndia behind her on her knees.

Sylvanas stood up out of the way, on the bank above the cut.

It ran north and a little east, up out of the fold and onto the hillside beyond, where it went on as a dark seam in the brown bracken until it went over a shoulder of the hill. West, across two fields and a hedge, was the road down from the Pass, a pale line with the holly wood dark on it halfway up, where she had sat a horse yesterday afternoon and shot a doe through the heart. It bent away west while the cut went on east of north, toward the stones, the two of them further and further apart the higher they went. The child had called it the north road, and so it was, to a child at the top of this lane. They had passed each other with the whole hill between.

Toward the line. The stones, and the long empty miles of ring between one post and the next, where there was nobody but a warden going round once a fortnight with a lens.

Behind her Cyndia had gone very still on her knees inside the box, the way she did when she counted.

They went further up, into the dark under the hazel, where the cut narrowed between two old gateposts nobody had hung a gate on in a hundred years. Cyndia laid the two switches again, kneeling between them. The mage went along with the staff, slower now, leaning on it.

"Heavy. Heavy. Heavy. Light—that's a hare. Heavy."

"They've bunched here where it narrows," Cyndia said. "Walked on each other's heels. The clay's soft enough to keep them all."

"How long is it?"

Cyndia walked up past the stones and on under the hazel with her lips moving, and laid the switches once more where the hazel thinned and knelt there a long while. Where the cut came out onto the open hill she stood, then came back down and halted in front of Sylvanas with her weight even on both feet, the way she had stood to give a report at the Pass since she was a recruit.

"Sixty, Ranger-General. Sixty I'll put my name to. They walked close, closer than men would, four and five abreast, the whole width of the cut. There'll be more. I can't separate the rest where they've gone over each other."

"Sixty."

"At least." Cyndia's voice went lower, and more careful. "Nothing turned off, either. There's a gap in the bank back there, a track down to a farm. Not one of them went into it. And none came back down. Every mark in this cut goes the one way."

Sixty. A few dozen houses, and the farms round about, and whoever had been at the inn on the night it happened. Every one of them up this cut in the dark with their toes down in the clay, shoulder to shoulder, the way sheep go through a gap. Sheep follow a bellwether. What had they followed? Sylvanas went back down to the mouth of the cut and walked up it again along the edge, hunting the print of the one walker who had gone apart from the rest, up the bank to look ahead and back down, or turned round to face them. A drover's print; a horse's.

She found nothing on the bank, at the sides or ahead of the first of them. They had just gone, all together, uphill.

Who drives sixty dead up a cow way in the dark? Nobody she could find. She was not going to stand in a hole in the ground in Lordaeron and make up an answer because she wanted one.

The mage had sat down on the bank by the old stones all at once, with the staff across her knees and her wrapped hands lying on it, her face turned up the cut toward the grey light coming down under the hazel.

"They didn't go up our road," she said. "Did they?"

"No."

"I thought we'd have seen something. All day, I kept thinking, if it's in the deer and the wolves and the hedges, there'll be something on the road—" She stopped. "We were the whole hill away."

"Yes."

A child's word, and Cyndia's count, and the mage with her staff, putting each heavy mark apart from the light ones so that the sixty was a number Cyndia would put her name to.

She pulled the glove back on over her dirty fingers.

"Cyndia. The mage. Down." She was already on the way, out of the cut and through the open gate and down the lane between the garden walls toward the inn, the light coming up behind the hills on her left hand. She lifted her voice so it would carry the whole way down and in at the yard gate. "*Neral!*"

Neral was standing in the yard gate before Sylvanas was halfway down, the bay and the grey saddled behind her with their breath smoking, a blanket roll and two full skins strapped across the grey's cantle. She had been at it since before there was light to see the buckles by. Nobody had told her to, and Sylvanas would have had a word to say to any ranger who waited to be told.

"Sixty," Sylvanas said. "At the least. Up the cow way, north-northeast, toward the outer ring east of the Pass. Not our road. They have a day's start, and more."

Neral turned back to the grey's girth. "Tube's on the table, Ranger-General."

The taproom smelled of cold ash and old beer and the tallow of the lantern they had let burn down in the night. The girl was on the settle where Neral had left her, sitting up inside the blanket with her knees drawn up and the blue scrap in her fist, watching Sylvanas come in the way a hare watches a hawk go over, without moving anything but her eyes. Sylvanas sat down at the table under the window and opened the tube.

Oiled paper, rolled tight. A stick of red wax. Her own stub of lead out of her belt pouch, worn down to the length of her thumbnail; it skated on the oil and left a grey smear she could barely read herself. She tried again, harder, and the point went to powder.

Damn.

A knock on the table at her elbow. The mage had come in behind her and put down her ink tin and a steel pen out of its oilcloth. She bent, took the lid of the tin in her teeth, unscrewed it with a twist of her head and set it down on the board, wet, beside the pen, her two linen-wrapped hands held against her chest.

Violet at the rim, where a drop had dried on the lip of the tin. Kirin Tor ink.

"They'll know that in Silvermoon," the mage said.

"They know it already. It's in your file."

The mage's ears went red at the tips. She went and sat at the far end of the settle from the girl and laid her hands in her lap.

Sylvanas wrote. Thalassian, small, no greeting.

*Lor'themar. Millhaven, first village south on the Pass road, is dead. All of it. Five days at the most. No bodies. Sixty and more of its dead walked out north-northeast by a drove way east of the road, toward the outer ring between the Pass and the eastern posts, a day and more ahead of this. Double the wardens on that stretch of the ring and put eyes on it from the high ground. The road from the Pass south is clear. The courier brings you a living witness, a human child, out of a cellar. She saw them go. I am going on south.*

She stopped with the pen lifted over the last line, still wet.

The chamber would have this in its hands by the end of the week. Lor'themar would carry it in and read it to them in his pleasant voice, and they would like him for it, as they always did. Then the magister's nephew would turn over a leaf of his figures and ask, politely, whether the Ranger-General had leave to send two rangers and their horses back to the Pass with a human brat on the saddle-bow, at the patrol's expense. He would have it worked out to the copper by the time Lor'themar sat down.

Let him count it. She had sixty in a cow way.

She blew on the ink and rolled the paper, holding the stick of wax in the lantern flame until it dripped. It ran onto her thumb, hot. She pressed her ring into it before it had properly pooled, and the leaf-and-arrow came up blurred on one side where the wax had slid. It would have to do. She slid the roll into the tube and screwed the cap down.

Three rangers and a prisoner, then, south into human country, and the only one of the four who could say which way to ride was the prisoner. Was that why she had kept the mage, and not sent her north in Neral's charge? No. She had kept the mage because the mage was a prisoner, and you did not hand a Kirin Tor apprentice to a relay rider with a child on her saddle. That was true, and very convenient, which she would have pointed out to anyone else.

And the child. Was she sending the child north to safety, or north to be somebody else's problem? Both. The Pass was a squat tower on a saddle of rock full of rangers and saddlery and dried meat, and not one soul in it knew what a human girl ought to eat. Somebody else would think of something.

She stood up with the tube in her hand.

"Mage. Put your lid back on your ink."

Out in the yard Neral took the tube from her without a word and strapped it to the off side of the bay's saddle behind her own leg, where a hand could reach it at a gallop and a branch could not. She pulled the strap through the buckle hard enough to make the bay grunt, then tugged the tube twice, a girth-test, and only then let go.

The fourth ranger brought the girl out.

She came stiff-legged across the cobbles in somebody's cut-down cloak, the blue scrap still in her fist, and stopped short of the horses. The bay put his head round to look at her. She went white round the mouth. Neral went down on her heels on the stones, out of the bay's way, her hands on her knees where the child could see them.

"This is my horse," Neral said, in the common tongue, low and slow. "He's big and he's stupid, and he's never stepped on anyone in his life. Look. He's asleep already. *Stand, you.* We're going to ride him a long way, you and me, up the road and over the hill to where my people are, and you're going to sit in front of me. You can hold his mane. You can pull it if you want. He won't mind."

It was the voice she had used on the spare grey in the ditch. The bay's ears went back to her and stayed there, his lip dropping. The girl's shoulders came down a little way from her ears. After a while she took one step, and another, and then she was standing against the bay's side with her cheek on the skirt of Neral's saddle and her free hand flat on the warm hide under it, her breath going in and out in little jerks that grew longer and slower.

Sylvanas turned away from it.

"Mount," she said. "Same as the crest. Cyndia, the left. Lyriel, the right. Mage, in the middle. I have the front."

They walked the horses out of the yard and down to the well and turned them to face the south road, the mage up on her borrowed mare after a long struggle, Lyriel holding the far stirrup. Sylvanas sat at the head of the road, watching the bridge.

Iron tyres on stone, a long grinding rumble off the far end of the bridge, and under it the slow clop of one heavy horse and the snort it gave at the water. Lyriel brought her bow up on the right, Cyndia hers on the left. Sylvanas did not draw.

"Hold."

It came up over the hump of the bridge at a walk: a flat farm wagon with plank sides, one brown cob in the shafts with feathered legs caked in mud to the knee and a collar patched with sacking and twine, and an old man on the box with a sack over his knees against the cold. He saw the bows first. He hauled back on the reins, and the cob stopped as if it had been hoping to, blowing, dropping its head.

"Morning," the old man said, to the bows.

Then he looked past them.

It was a face she would not have known again in a crowd: weathered, stubbled grey, a nose broken once and set crooked. His mouth went slack first, and then the rest of him caught up. He took in the well, the inn, the sign hanging still on its bracket, the houses up the street with no smoke from any chimney and nobody at any door. He took his hat off.

"Where's everybody?"

"Dead," Sylvanas said. "Five days. Don't water your horse here, and don't touch the well."

"Dead." He turned the hat round in his hands. "Millhaven? I was through here on market day. They were well enough then." He turned toward the inn door again. "Well enough."

The wagon was empty. She could see down into it over the side: bare boards, a coil of rope, a folded sack, and old grain dust packed into the cracks between the planks and gone grey with weather. Empty going up, full coming down.

"Where have you come from this morning?"

"The ford." He waved the hat back over his shoulder, south. "Down the valley."

"What's it like down there? Who's still trading?"

"All of them, far as I know." He put the hat back on. "They had the market at the ford yesterday, busy as you like. Smoke in every chimney the whole way up. Barley's up a penny, and they're crying about it, but they're buying." He was looking past her again, up the street. "I'd not have thought it. Not Millhaven."

Busy as you like, and smoke in every chimney. It was what she had wanted from him, given plainly, and it was no use to her at all. Millhaven had been busy as you like on market day.

"I come up for the mill every fortnight," the old man said. "Nothing for me here, then." He gathered the reins up off the cob's back, then stopped. "You'll not have passed a young fellow on the road? Coming down from your way, with a grain cart. New man. I've a mind to see what he looks like."

It was Lyriel who answered.

"We passed nobody, friend. Not a living soul between here and the hills." She lowered her bow and tipped her head back toward the yard gate, where the girl stood leaning against Neral's saddle with her cheek on the leather. "The little one there spoke of a newer grain seller, though, so we'll keep an eye out for him going south, and if we see him we'll tell him you're looking. You'll get a better price going home full than going home empty, mind."

Cyndia's bow stayed up. Lyriel looked satisfied anyway.

The old man laughed, short, through his nose. "That's the truth."

He took in the line of horses once, front to back, the mage's borrowed mare sitting between them in the gap. Then he slapped the reins down on the cob's back. The cob leaned into the collar, the wagon lurching and coming on.

"May the stars keep your road," he said, going by.

The iron tyres ground off the bridge stones onto the packed road. The wagon went past Sylvanas's knee close enough to touch, rattling on past the well, north, through the crossing and up the empty street beyond.

Stars. Light keep you, the humans said, the few who came up the Pass road with a petition or a cartload of oats; or Safe road; or nothing at all, which was likeliest. Some hill saying, a carter's grandmother's. It had caught on her, and she had no room this morning for an old man's goodbye. On her left Cyndia had turned her head after the wagon and held it there a moment before she turned it back to the road.

Leave it.

Over by the yard gate Neral had the girl by the waist.

"I'm going to lift you up now," Neral said.

The girl went up onto the front of the saddle without a sound, stiff as a doll. Neral swung up behind her in one long movement, drew her back against her own chest and put the girl's hands into the bay's mane, one and then the other, the fist with the blue scrap in it too. The fourth ranger was up already, holding the spare grey's lead short round one hand.

"Ranger-General."

"Don't stop at the Pass longer than it takes to change saddles. Put it in Lor'themar's hand yourself."

"Yes, Ranger-General."

Neral put her heel to the bay and the bay stepped out. The girl sat bolt upright against Neral with her two hands in the mane and her eyes on nothing, and the bay walked her up past the inn sign into the north road, swinging wide round the wagon, the fourth ranger and the grey coming on behind. At the last of the houses the bay broke into a trot. The girl's head jolted once against Neral's collar and stayed there.

Sylvanas turned the mare's head toward the bridge.

"South," she said.

They crossed the bridge single file, the race roaring white under the arch, the great wheel standing still in the mill, dripping, green weed already furring the paddles. Then the road climbed out of the fold between two banks of hawthorn, and over the lip of it, and the country opened.

The hedges stood further apart here, and the fields between them were bigger, long strips of ploughland turned brown and shining with wet, and stubble with the rooks walking up and down it. The road went down the middle of it all toward a long blue valley, and at the bottom a line of willows that would be the river and the ford. Human country. Fat and flat and wide open, the wind coming across it with nothing to stop it, and nowhere at all for a ranger to stand that could not be seen from half a mile off.

The road was worse than the one down from the Pass. Carts had been on it all autumn and the rain had got into the ruts. In the low places the mud came up over the mare's fetlocks and sucked at her feet, and she hitched each time a hoof came free.

Four horses, where she had come down from the Pass with seven.

She kept her face to the road. She could hear it. Lyriel's mare on her right hand, Cyndia's on her left, the mage's borrowed mare behind and between, and behind that, nothing. No bay, with Neral talking to it under her breath. No grey on the short lead, blowing at the mud. No fourth ranger at the tail, the one she had never once had to think about all down the hill road.

She had put them there. Well, and what else should she have done? Sent the warning north with one rider and no spare, the child bouncing on the saddle-bow, and hoped nothing on the hill road had teeth? She would have flayed a ranger for that. Now she had three bows and a prisoner, in the middle of a human ploughland with nothing to put her back against but rooks.

By the middle of the morning the mage was asleep. Her head went down by little jerks, caught itself, went down again, until her chin was on her chest and her body swayed with the mare's walk, her wrapped hands slack on the pommel. Lyriel had moved up to her knee without being told and hooked two fingers through the mare's cheek-strap. Sylvanas let her sleep. She had held her eyes open on a mounting block half the night, and she would want them later.

Sylvanas was not far behind her. Her knees ached from the ditch. Every so often the road in front of her went very flat and very bright until she blinked and it came back. She had sent rangers to their blankets for less and stood over them till they went.

At noon they watered at a ford that was not the ford, a little gravel crossing where a stream came down under the road. The mage woke when the mare put her head down to drink and nearly went over it.

She came down out of the saddle badly and stood with the stirrup leather in her fist until she could trust her legs. Then she took the staff out of its loop, walked a few paces up onto the crown of the road, where it was drier, and set the heel of the staff on the ground between her boots, her cheek against the wood.

The crystal clicked faster than Sylvanas had heard it go in the cut, no gaps left in it now, tick on tick so close it nearly ran together into a hum. The mage stood listening with her eyes shut, her face going grey round the mouth, then greenish, as it had in the cut at dawn. She swallowed twice.

"Well?"

"It's thicker." The mage did not open her eyes. "Ahead. It's not a knot, the way Millhaven was. It's—" She stopped. She lifted the staff and set it down again a pace to the left, listening again. "Wider. Spread out. A stain in a cloth that's been wet through." She opened her eyes and turned toward the willows, where the road went. "It's ahead of us, Ranger-General. Down the road."

Behind them at the water, Lyriel's mare threw up her head with the water running from her muzzle and stood with her ears pricked hard, facing south down the road, and Cyndia's gelding beside her did the same. Then both put their heads down and drank again.

Sylvanas laid her hand on her own mare's neck. Warm. Wet with mud splash. Nothing in the hide or the bones of her hand. There never was.

The border was warned. Or it would be, by tomorrow night, with Neral changing saddles at the Pass and the tube strapped hard behind her leg: the wardens doubled on the eastern ring, somebody up on the high ground with a glass, and the magister's nephew counting the cost of it to the copper. Her duty was done, by any measure the chamber would put its seal to. A Ranger-General who turned round at this ford and rode home behind her warning would be thanked for it.

So why was she watering her horse in Lordaeron, facing south?

The trail went south. Which trail? The dead had gone north; she had their toes in the clay to prove it. What went south was a click in a crystal, a human mage's face turning green, and she was following it, four of them in a line with two holes in the back of it, deeper and deeper into somebody else's kingdom. On a human's read. On a prisoner's.

No. On the trail. The read was the trail; it was the only trail there was. That was not the same as following the mage.

It was not the same.

She took her hand off the mare's neck and gathered up the reins.

"Up," she said. "Mage. Up."

They rode the rest of the day down the long valley in the mud, flushing rooks off the stubble ahead that came down again behind them. Toward evening the road came round the shoulder of a low hill above a farm: a long low house, a barn, a midden steaming in the yard, smoke going up thin and blue into the still cold air. A dog came out from behind the barn and ran at the gate, barking, hackles up. A woman came to the door with a bucket in her hand and stared at the four of them going by on the road: the tall horses, the green and gold, the bows across the saddles, the young woman in the middle with her hands done up in white rags.

Then she went back in and shut the door.

The staff in the crook of the mage's elbow pointed past the farm, south.

The dog followed them along the inside of the hedge as far as the end of the field, barking.
