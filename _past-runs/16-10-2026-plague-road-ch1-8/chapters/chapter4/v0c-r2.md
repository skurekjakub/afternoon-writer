The taste came before the village did. It sat on the back of her tongue like an old copper she had been sucking. In the crook of her elbow the crystal began to tick against its claws, light and quick, tapping like a moth at a shutter.

Jaina swallowed. It came back.

Then the lane turned between two cottages and the village was all around them at once, loud with the business of a working morning. A hammer rang on iron somewhere ahead, quick and slow and quick again. A woman called a price for eggs, and another woman called it lower, and the first one laughed at her. Geese hissed from a yard. A man was backing a cart through a gate, shouting it back, back, back, and the hub knocked the gatepost anyway and he swore at the horse, which had done nothing. Bread smoke lay over all of it, low and warm and brown, as it lay over every village in Lordaeron at this hour. Two boys came pelting under the mare's nose after a hoop. The mare flung her head up, planting all four feet on the cobbles and blowing at them. Lyriel's hand came across from the right and took the cheek-strap, Lyriel not so much as turning her head.

"Steady," Lyriel said, to the horse or to her.

Jaina had the reins looped over her wrapped left hand, which was all that hand was good for this morning, throbbing inside the linen with every jolt. The right lay in her lap like something she had been asked to carry for someone else, the staff riding in the crook of that arm with its heel against her boot. She had slept. Two nights on the ground in her cloak with a saddle under her head; last night she had slept so hard she'd woken with the print of the stirrup buckle on her cheek and a clear head for the first time since Dalaran. She had been almost cheerful, saddling up. She had made Lyriel laugh.

And here was the cold coming up under her breastbone, exactly where the staff had said it would be. Two days of road since Millhaven and the dread arrived on time, like a coach.

Under the hammer and the geese and the eggs, the drag.

She shut her eyes to hear it. The currents ran under the cobbles as they ran everywhere, a slow pulse she had known so long she only heard it when it went wrong. Here it had gone wrong. Each beat came in and then something came in after it, late—a weight hung on the tail of the pulse. The same drag she had felt through the floor of the cell at the Pass and in the doe's blood and at the Millhaven well.

It was quieter here. That was the strange part. Millhaven had been a knot pulled so tight it rang, every line in the fold hauling the same dead weight into the crossing and nothing alive anywhere to cover it. Here it was thin and spread and moving, coming and going as the mare carried her past a doorway, a stall, a woman with a basket. Warm bodies all round her, dozens of them, and it was in them, moving with them, the whole loud warm noise of the place lying over it like a blanket over a cough.

Quieter because it was still moving. Because it still had somewhere warm to go.

Oh, Light. That was a horrible way to put it, and it was right.

She opened her eyes. Was it here, then? Yes. Rooted? She had followed the staff for two days down this valley like a dog after a cart, and the staff was ticking in her elbow now—so, yes. Where, exactly? Everywhere a little. In which houses, which people, which part of which people? She did not know. What would she show anyone, if anyone asked? A taste, and a crystal that clicked, and her own face going green. Brilliant, Jaina. Antonidas's prize student, sure as stone and with nothing in her hands to prove it—her hands not much use for anything else either.

The egg stall was on the left, a trestle under an awning with straw in the trays. The woman behind it was haggling with a broad-hipped woman in a blue apron who had a little girl against her skirts, five or six years old, in a knitted cap pulled down to her eyebrows. The child's cheeks were red as scrubbed brick, two hot patches high on the bones and the rest of her face pale round them. Her eyes were very bright. She was leaning her whole weight on her mother's hip, sagging by degrees, and every time she sagged her mother hitched her up again without looking down.

"Stand up, Mag, you're not a sack of turnips. Nearly done."

The child stood up, coughing into her mother's apron, a small wet cough, twice. Her mother went on haggling over the eggs.

The crystal ticked as the mare went past them, and again at the next stall, where there was no child at all, only a man selling salt herring out of a barrel. The brine smell came up off it sharp and clean and went straight through her, so that for a moment she was nine years old on the quay at Boralus with the gulls going over and her father's hand on her head. She had not smelled the sea since the spring. She breathed it in, and under it the bread smoke, and under that the copper on her tongue.

She could not read the child from the saddle. Not from here, not through the noise, not with the staff loose in a dead arm. The flush might be any fever. A child might cough for any reason; children did nothing else between harvest and spring.

But once she had heard the first cough she heard them everywhere. A man in a doorway, holding his fist against his mouth. Someone in an upstairs room behind a half-open shutter, on and on, a dry hacking that nobody went to. Two women at the pump in the middle of the street filling buckets, one of them turning her face aside to cough and then turning back to finish what she was saying. All of it under the hammer and the geese, so ordinary that nobody in the street turned to look.

*Everybody coughing at the well.*

The girl had said it into Neral's collar, in the dark of the inn at Millhaven. Missus Hale's baby first. Then Mister Pell. Then everybody coughing at the well. And then nobody: tables laid for supper and sixty sets of toes pressed into the clay of a cow way, all pointing uphill.

So this was the part before. This was Millhaven a fortnight ago or a week or however long it had been—Millhaven with smoke in its chimneys and a market in its street and a woman telling her daughter to stand up straight.

Jaina half turned in the saddle toward the back of the line, meaning to say it to Neral. There was nobody there. She turned back.

"Ranger-General," Cyndia said, on the left, her voice level and carrying no farther than it needed to. "Shutters up on three houses, this side, since the bend. Middle of the day."

"I saw them," Windrunner said, from the front.

Jaina had not seen them. She looked now, craning over Cyndia's shoulder: a cottage with its shutters closed and barred and its door shut, a stool on the step with a cup on it, as if someone had left it there for someone who would not come out. She had been listening to the ground. She had heard every pulse under the cobbles since the bend and missed three shut houses in plain daylight.

Then the horses stopped.

Windrunner's mare stopped first and the rest stopped behind it, bunching, iron scraping and sliding on the wet cobbles, Lyriel's mare swinging her quarters round until Lyriel sat her straight again. Across the lane, at the height of the horses' chests, lay the shaft of a spear.

It was a boar spear with a broad leaf of a head and a crossbar under it. The man who held it held it level, in both hands, across the whole width of the lane between a wall and a water butt. He was not pointing it at anyone. He was simply holding it there, as a farmer holds a gate shut against his own cattle.

He was big, heavy through the shoulders, in a quilted jack too warm for the day and a leather cap. Fair stubble going grey. His hands on the ash shaft were brown and broad and cracked across the knuckles—a farmer's hands, with a split thumbnail on the right one gone black underneath. Two men stood behind him, one with a pitchfork and one with a hunting bow, unstrung. The one with the bow looked about sixteen, holding it as if it might go off.

"That's far enough," the big man said. "We've sick here. Nobody comes in armed, and nobody comes in I don't know."

Behind them the egg woman had stopped calling her price.

Jaina had it all ready. It was right there, in good order, fit to hand to Antonidas: *Captain, what you have here is not a fever. It is a contamination of the currents, necromantic in character, and it is already in your people, and if you do not let us through—* She had leaned forward in the saddle to say it, her mouth already open.

She looked at the egg stall. The broad-hipped woman in the blue apron had turned round with the child against her skirts. The herring man had stopped with his scoop half out of the barrel, and the two women at the pump had set their buckets down. Every face in the lane was turned toward the spear.

*Necromantic.* In this lane. In front of that child.

She shut her mouth.

Windrunner swung down from her mare.

She did it without hurry and without looking round, lifting the bow off her shoulder and holding it out behind her, sideways, at arm's length. Cyndia leaned out of her saddle to take it. Then Windrunner walked forward to the spear, empty-handed, until the shaft lay across the front of her coat, and stopped there. She was taller than he was. She did not seem to be trying to be.

"You're right to stop us," she said.

She said it low. Jaina, a horse's length back, heard every word, but it would not have reached the egg stall. The man had to lean a little to hear it, and he did lean.

"Then you'll turn round, my lady."

"Ranger-General."

His thumb moved on the ash. "Ranger-General."

"We've come from Millhaven."

The thumb stopped.

"Millhaven," he said.

"We came into it at dusk. The tables were laid. Bowls on them and a spoon in every bowl and a loaf." Windrunner's voice did not change at all, going on at the same level, as if she were telling him how far it was to the next ford. "There was a sickbed made up with a basin and a cup of water beside it. A cradle in the end house with the blanket tucked in. No child in it. No one in any house. Living or dead."

The man said nothing. The boy with the bow had come up at his shoulder to listen, and the big man put out his elbow without looking, moving him back.

"They walked out of it," Windrunner said. "Up the cow way above the village, in the night. Sixty of them that we could count. We counted their prints."

"Walked."

"Walked." She let that sit. Then, in exactly the same voice: "There was a child left alive, shut in a cellar. She said the first thing she remembers is that everybody was coughing at the well."

She did not look round at the pump. She did not look at the egg stall or the child in the knitted cap. She kept her eyes on his face and let him do that himself, and he did. Jaina watched him do it. His head turned to the pump, where the two women stood with their buckets, and then, slower, to the stall. The child coughed again into the blue apron. He looked at that a long while.

When he turned back his face had gone a bad colour under the stubble.

"It's fever," he said. "It's the time of year for it. Tobin says it's fever."

"Then let my mage see your sick, and we'll know by dark whether I'm a liar."

His thumb moved on the wood, sliding up the shaft and back down. Up and back.

"Your people are watching you, Captain," Windrunner said, even lower. "Keep your spear. Walk with me. I'd like to see your village, and I'd like them to see me with you."

Up the shaft, and back.

Jaina had stopped breathing somewhere around the cradle, her left hand closed on the reins hard enough to make the raw fingers sing inside the linen. She made it open. What would she have done, if he said no? Argued. Explained. Explained louder, with diagrams, while the egg woman listened to every word and went home and told her sister. Light help her, she would have drawn him the currents in the air with a hand that didn't work.

The big man lifted the spear.

He brought it upright and grounded the butt on the cobbles beside his boot, holding it there like a staff, letting out a long breath through his nose.

"Willem Brask," he said. "I've the militia here, such as it is." He jerked his chin at the boy. "Tobin has them in the chapel. Dickon, take the mage to Tobin. Go on." Then, up at Jaina, as if he had only now seen her: "Miss."

"Thank you, Captain," Jaina said.

It came out higher than she meant it to.

Windrunner had already turned. "Lyriel. With the mage. Cyndia, with me." She took her bow back from Cyndia and slung it, looking up at Jaina for the first time since she had dismounted, her eyes very clear and giving nothing back. "Mage. What you find, I want before dark."

"Yes, Ranger-General."

And that was that. Windrunner walked off down the lane at Brask's shoulder, the big man still carrying his spear upright, Cyndia a pace behind and to the left of them with her hand resting near her quiver. The egg woman called her price again, a little too loudly. The herring man put his scoop back in the barrel, shaking the brine off it. Brask stopped at the pump to say something to the two women. One of them laughed, short and shaky, picking up her bucket.

Jaina sat on the mare in the middle of the lane, her ears going hot.

She had filed Windrunner under maps. Under ground, and ambush angles, and ears sharp enough to hear a wolf at the back of a holly wood; a woman who could read a map backward and be right, and had been, and Jaina had stopped talking to her in small words after that, she had, she had made a point of it. But people? Under people she had filed her as nothing much. A woman who said *Mage. Up.* and expected the mare and the mage to obey her in the same tone.

Refile it, then.

How had she known how hard to push? She had not raised her voice once. She had given her bow away, which had cost her nothing whatever with three more bows behind her. She had conceded the man his point before he had finished making it. Then she had told him about a cradle, and a spoon in a bowl, and a child coughing at the well—not one word about currents. She had let him look at his own street himself, never once pointing.

Jaina would have pointed. Jaina would have explained to him, at length, in a voice that carried, and the egg woman would have had the word *necromancer* by heart before dinner. The spear would have been the least of their troubles.

Could it be learned? Antonidas had never taught it. Antonidas had never needed to; men lowered their spears for Antonidas because of the hat.

"Lady Proudmoore." Lyriel had come round to her stirrup on foot, her own mare's reins over her arm. "Will you get down, or shall I fetch a ladder?"

"I'm getting down."

She got down, doing it badly, as she did it every time, the staff sliding in her elbow and her right leg not quite clearing the cantle; Lyriel took her weight at the waist without comment, set her on the cobbles and let go. The ground came up hard through her boot-soles. The copper taste was stronger down here.

The boy, Dickon, was waiting for them with his unstrung bow in his fist, looking at Lyriel's ears and trying not to.

"It's this way, miss," he said. "It's not far."

He took them up a side lane between two garden walls, glancing back every few steps at Lyriel's ears. Lyriel walked behind him, leading the two mares on her right hand, with Jaina on her left. Ahead of them the hammer rang on its iron, quick and slow and quick.

The chapel stood at the top of the lane, a squat grey house of the Light with a bell-cote on the gable and no bell in it. Someone had carried the benches out and stacked them in the yard under the yew, upside down, their legs in the air like dead beetles. The door was shut. Dickon knocked on it with the end of his bow and pushed it open without waiting. The smell came out to meet them.

Vinegar first, sharp enough to sting. Then sweat and tallow and the slop bucket nobody had emptied since morning. And under all of it, faint and thick, a sweetness.

Pears gone brown.

*They had no pears,* the girl had said, in the dark at Millhaven. Jaina had asked her whether the smell came before the fever or after. Before, the girl had said. Before she was hot. Jaina had tried to write it down that night by the inn lantern, the pen pushed through the linen between two fingers that would not close on it. She had blotted the page and given up.

Now she could smell it for herself, and she wished she could not.

"Light," Lyriel said under her breath, behind her. She tied the mares to the yard rail and came in after them.

It was dim inside and close and too warm. Every shutter was barred. A brazier smoked in the middle of the floor where the benches should have been. Round it, packed so close their sides touched, stood every bed in the village that could be carried. Two proper cots. A box bed with its doors taken off. A child's truckle bed with an old man on it, his feet hanging off the end in grey stockings. A door laid flat across two trestles with a straw pallet on top. Pallets on the floor between them. The blankets were every colour there was—a red one, a striped one, a sheepskin, a good blue wool with a darned corner—each family sending its own bedding with its own sick. Some of the beds were still empty, made up and waiting. She could not have walked straight between them if she had wanted to. She went sideways, holding the staff upright against her chest in the crook of her arm, and still her skirts brushed somebody's hand.

Someone was coughing. Several people were. Someone else was saying *Mam, Mam, Mam* in a flat voice, over and over, not loud.

A man came round the end of the box bed, carrying a basin in both hands. He was small and wiry and bald as an egg, sleeves rolled to the elbow and a leather apron gone black down the front, his hands and forearms red to the wrists—scrubbed red, the skin split across every knuckle and in the webs between the fingers. Something shiny was smeared in the cracks. Goose grease, from the smell of it. He stopped when he saw them, looking at Lyriel's bow and Lyriel's ears and at Jaina last.

"Willem sent you?"

"Captain Brask, yes. I'm Jaina Proudmoore. I'm a mage, from Dalaran."

"Dalaran." He said it as if Dalaran were a long way off and somehow to blame. "Tobin. I do teeth and bones, mostly. Births." He looked down at her hands, at the grubby linen bound round them to the wrists. "What happened to those?"

"Frost."

"Want me to look at them?"

It was so unexpected that she nearly laughed. "No. Thank you. They'll keep." She looked past him at the cots. "What have you been treating them for?"

"Fever." He set the basin down on the end of the door-bed and wiped his hands on the apron, which could not have made them cleaner. "What else would I treat? It comes on like fever. They burn like fever. Willow bark, vinegar cloths, keep them warm and keep the draught off. I bled old Garran yesterday and it did him no good at all, so I'll not do it again. Is it fever?"

"It's a fever," Jaina said. "There's something else in it too. I need to see who's been ill longest, and who came in last."

He looked at her a moment longer before jerking his head toward the truckle bed. "Garran's longest. Since before market. Elsie came in this morning." He pointed across the room to the door laid on the trestles, where a girl of fifteen or so lay on the pallet under the striped blanket with her eyes open, watching them. "She was milking yesterday. Fainted in the byre."

Jaina went to Elsie first.

The girl turned her head on the pallet as Jaina came round to her. Her face was flushed and damp, her hair stuck down on her forehead in dark points, her eyes clear enough, and frightened. When Jaina bent over her the sweetness came up off her breath, strong enough this close that Jaina had to swallow.

"I'm going to look at you," Jaina said. "With a light. It won't hurt you. You might feel warm."

"I'm warm now," Elsie said, plucking at the blanket.

"Warmer, then. I'm sorry."

She could not do this as she had done the doe. Her right hand would not have felt the girl if she had laid it on her. The left had nothing left on its fingertips to feel with—she had left that on the stones of the well at Millhaven. Windrunner had told her flatly that there would be no more hand-reading. For once she had not argued. The staff, then. She turned to Lyriel.

"Will you hold this for me?"

Lyriel took it, holding it as rangers held everything, as if she expected it to do something sudden: one hand under the crystal and one round the silver wire. "If it bites me, Lady Proudmoore, I'm letting go."

"It won't. Lower. The crystal against her, here, under the ribs. Gently. Through the blanket is fine."

Lyriel set it there. Jaina bent to lay her cheek against the ash where the wood was dark from her own hands, shutting her eyes, listening.

The girl's pulse came up through the wood, fast and light. Under it, faint and very close, the drag.

She said the word for light—the plain one, the first one every apprentice in the Violet Citadel learned and the last one she would ever forget—and let it go down the staff like water down a rope.

The crystal woke. A pale gold glow came out of it, going into the girl through the blanket and her shift and her skin. Jaina opened her eyes to watch. The skin of Elsie's belly lit from inside like a hand cupped round a candle, pink and gold, the dark shadows of the ribs standing out above. The light moved with the pulse, outward and back, outward and back, rising into the chest to beat easy and clean round the heart and fill the lungs like breath before it came back.

Under the ribs it did not come back clean.

There was a place just below the breastbone, about the size of a fist, where the light went in on the beat and came out after it late and dragging—and when it came out it was the wrong colour. Brown, like a drop of ink let fall into a glass of water, curling. Below it, along the gut, ran a thread of the same brown, thin and broken, following the coils down. And nowhere else: the heart clean, the lungs clean, the blood in her arms going pink to the fingertips when Jaina sent the light out along them, coming back pink.

"Does it hurt her?" Lyriel said quietly. "Does it hurt you?"

"No. A little."

The staff was going cold under her cheek. She could feel it creeping up out of the wood into the bones of her face, and into her left hand, which had no business feeling anything, aching now as if she had plunged it in snow. When she let the light go the girl's skin went dark and ordinary. Elsie let out a breath, staring up at her.

"What's in me?"

"I don't know yet." Jaina straightened, her back cracking. "I'm going to find out. Lie still."

"Garran," she said to Tobin.

The old man on the truckle bed did not open his eyes when they came. His face had gone the colour of a tallow candle, grey-yellow, the skin stretched across the bones of the nose. His breath went in and out of him, catching wet at the top every time. His belly under the sheepskin was round and hard. The sweetness had gone off his breath. There was something else there now, heavier, that she did not want to name.

Lyriel set the crystal against him without being told, angling it in under the sheepskin. Jaina laid her cheek to the wood.

The light went in, and almost none of it came back clean.

It was everywhere. It came up out of the old man's belly in brown threads, branching, out under the ribs and up into the liver—dark and swollen with it—and into the lungs, where the light went in on each breath and came out late and dirty and broken up. Along the great vessels of the neck. Down both arms, thin as hair, almost to the fingers. It had spread out from the middle of him and gone everywhere. Every thread of it ran back, if she followed it, to the same place under the breastbone where the girl's fist of brown had been.

She had seen this before. She had seen it in a doe in a ditch below the Pass, with her bare hand flat on the hide, running through the meat and the blood and the liver like roots in a pot. She had not seen it in a man who was still breathing.

The same. Light help her, it was the same—alive in him, and him still alive round it.

She let the light go and stood up, the cold already in her teeth.

"Who else came in in the last day or two?"

Tobin showed her, going from bed to bed with the basin still in his hands. A boy of ten on a pallet by the brazier, the cooper's youngest, hot since yesterday. A woman in the box bed, his mother, hot since the day before.

"The cooper's down to him and her, so far," Tobin said. "Three more of them in that house. I've a bed kept for each."

A young man in his shirt lay on the floor by the door with his boots still on, propped against the wall, opening one eye at them.

"I'm not sick," he said. "I'm tired. I've been on the hill all summer and I came down to a forge. Tobin's an old woman."

"Tobin's the one with the bed," said Tobin.

The young man's breath smelled of brown pears. Lyriel moved the staff from one to the next and Jaina followed it, bending to the wood each time, sending the light down. Each time it came back the same.

Under the ribs. A fist of brown, or half a fist. A thread along the gut. Nothing in the lungs, nothing in the blood, nothing in the arms or the head.

The young man by the door had the smallest knot of all, no bigger than a walnut. He watched the light go in and out of his own stomach through his open shirt, frowning down at it. "Well, that's not right," he said, in a tone of mild complaint, as if someone had sold him a bad horse.

Jaina knelt there beside him, keeping her cheek against the staff, not moving. She did not want to move, in case it went away.

The gut. Every one of them, at the start, in the gut and nowhere else. And the long cases went outward from there, out from the stomach into everything else, like water spreading from where it was spilled.

So it went in by the mouth.

They were swallowing it.

"Well?" Tobin had come up behind her with the basin, peering over her shoulder. "What is it?"

She got up off her knees. It took two tries, Lyriel putting a hand under her elbow for the second one and letting go once she was up.

"It starts in the stomach," she said, keeping her voice down for the young man's sake, and for Elsie's, who was watching them across the room. "In everyone who's newly ill, it's only there. In Garran it's everywhere now. It spread out from there. They're taking it in. Swallowing it."

"Swallowing what?"

"I don't know."

"It's the water, then." Tobin set the basin down on the cooper's boy's pallet, hard enough to slop it. "I said so to Willem. Bad water. There's the three wells and the brook, and the brook comes down past the tannery—"

"It might be the water."

It might. The well at Millhaven had read thin, thinner than the doe. Thin water didn't put a fist of it under a dairymaid's ribs between one evening's milking and the next morning. Unless she drank a great deal of it. Everybody drank a great deal of water, Jaina; that was rather the point of water. Food, then—whose food? Everybody's, because everybody ate. A cup passed hand to hand along a bench. A spoon licked and handed on. Fingers in a mouth. A mother kissing a child goodnight. Two down in the cooper's house already, Tobin said, and what did a household do together but eat from one pot and drink from one bucket and sleep in one bed and breathe each other's breath?

She couldn't strike out a single one of them. Not one. And every hour she stood here not knowing, every house in the village was sitting down to its dinner.

Tobin had gone back to Elsie and knelt beside the door-bed, sliding one red cracked hand under the back of her head to lift it while he held a cup to her mouth with the other. Jaina watched the girl's throat move as she drank—once and again and again—her own stomach turning over.

Water. Or the cup. Or the hand on the cup. Or nothing to do with any of it.

"Lyriel," she said, pointing with her chin. "Bring me that jug."

Lyriel brought it: a brown crock jug with a chip out of the lip and a wet ring under it where it had stood on the trestle, nearly full.

"From the pump?"

"From the pump," Tobin said. "Same as every bucket in this room. Same as every bucket in the street."

"Set the crystal against it, please. Low down, under the water line."

Lyriel tipped the staff, setting the crystal to the glaze as carefully as if the jug might go off. Jaina bent to put her cheek to the ash, the wood no warmer than the last time, and sent the light down.

Water did not glow like a body. The light went in and lay there, dull gold, holding no pulse but the slow pulse of the currents coming up through the floor and the clay of the jug. Each beat came in. Something came in after it, late.

Thin, though—a hair's weight on the tail of each beat, no more than that. She had felt worse in the ditch water below the Pass. She had felt the same at the Millhaven well, before she had put her hand to the stones and left half of it there.

She let it go. "There's some in the water."

Tobin let his breath out through his nose, shaking his head.

"A little," Jaina said. "Very little. Less than in Elsie, by a long way."

"So it's the water and other things besides."

"It might be."

It was in the water. It had been in the water at Millhaven and in the clay at Millhaven, and very likely it was in the clay under this floor; thin was everywhere south of the Pass by now, as damp is everywhere in a cellar. Did thin put a fist of it under a dairymaid's ribs between one milking and the next? She didn't think so. Did she know so? No. She knew it was in a jug.

The door banged open behind her before she could decide what that was worth.

Two men came in sideways with a sheep hurdle between them and a woman on the hurdle. She was big and fair and red-armed, in a brown wool dress with the hem soaked dark. She was fighting, drumming her heels on the wattles. One hand had hold of the hurdle's edge and the other clawed at her own collar as if it choked her. She was talking, fast and low and not to anyone in the room: the hens, somebody had to see to the hens, nobody ever saw to the hens but her.

"Dunmore's wife," Tobin said. "From the top farm. Put her down there, Hew, by the door-bed, mind the bucket—"

They put her down on the floor between Elsie's trestles and the brazier. The bigger of the two men stood over her with his hands hanging, muck to the knees from the yard and a face like a slapped child's. Behind him, in the doorway, a little girl in a man's coat that came down to her ankles was holding a shawl against her mouth. She came in after them because nobody told her not to and went into the corner by the font where the benches should have been. She sat down on the flags with the shawl, watching.

Jaina looked at the woman, and the cooper's wife coughing in the box bed, and old Garran with the wet catch at the top of every breath, and Elsie watching her across the room with her clear frightened eyes.

She could stand here and read jugs until dark. She could read the salt box and the bucket and the ladle and the cat, and every one of them would come back thin while every house in the village went on sitting down to its dinner. Or she could do the one thing she was sure she knew how to do.

"Lyriel. The staff, please."

Lyriel gave it to her. Jaina got it into the crook of her right arm, where the dead hand could not drop it because the dead hand was not being asked to hold anything, pinning it against her ribs. She knelt by the hurdle on the cracked flags, set the heel of the staff on the stone beside the woman's shoulder, and leaned the crystal out over her.

It was the first holding ward any apprentice learned in Dalaran. It was no healing at all. They taught it in the alchemy rooms in the first week, for the glass. You put it round a flask that had to go into the furnace so the flask would not crack in the heat; it did nothing to whatever was boiling inside, only kept the glass in one piece while it boiled. She had put it round a hundred flasks. She had never put it round a woman.

She said it. The light that came down out of the crystal was blue, not gold, a cold clean blue like the shadow side of snow, coming down over the woman in a fine mesh, settling on her, sinking in. Through it, under the ribs and spreading, the brown in her showed up murky and yellow, the colour of a fly in old honey.

The drag hit the ward on the next beat.

Jaina felt it in her arm. It came up the staff as a pull, a little late weight hung on the tail of the woman's pulse, and the ward took it and held. The pull sank back into the woman and got no further than it already was. Next beat, the same. And the next—each one a tug on a line, each one the line holding. The cold came up out of the ash into the crook of her elbow, spreading across her chest under the bodice like a wet shirt.

The woman's heels stopped drumming.

Her hand let go of the hurdle, the other sliding down off her collar to lie open on the brown wool, palm up. Her head rolled a little to the side, toward the brazier, and she was still.

Quite still.

Jaina's own breath stopped in her. No. No, she hadn't—

The woman's chest went up. Slow. It hung there a moment and came down, slow, and rose again. The lines round her mouth let go. She was asleep.

In the corner, the little girl in the man's coat took the shawl away from her face.

Jaina breathed. She stayed on her knees with the staff against her ribs until she was sure the ward would stand without her, then got up, badly, and moved on to the cooper's wife.

Then the cooper's boy. Then Garran, whose ward took twice the holding, giving her a cold so deep she felt it in the roots of her teeth. Then Elsie, who said, "It's like a wet sheet," and then, "It's nice," closing her eyes. The wards stood on their own once they were set, for a while, as a lamp burns on its own for a while, and then they thinned. She went round the room from cot to box bed to hurdle to door-bed and round again, trimming them before they guttered, like a girl in a house with too many lamps and one taper.

Most of them were early. That was the thing she kept coming back to, between wards, with the cold in her teeth. The cooper's boy, Elsie, the young man by the door with his walnut, the woman from the top farm: a fist or less in all of them, all in the gut, nothing in the blood. Not Garran—but most. Most of them were early enough to be worth something.

The bandage on Garran's arm had come loose.

She saw it on her second round, kneeling to feed his ward. Tobin had bled him yesterday at the inside of the elbow. The pad had slid, the cut weeping thin and pink into the sheepskin. Tobin was across the room with Hew Dunmore, who wanted to know if his wife was dead and would not believe that she wasn't. Lyriel was at the brazier, warming a bucket of water. Jaina had the staff pinned in her right arm, the ward half-trimmed, the pull coming up the ash in its slow late beats. She could not let go of it to call anybody.

So she picked up the strip of linen with her left hand.

She got the pad back over the cut easily enough. She got the strip round his arm once, and round again, the left hand doing it all—fingers that would only curl halfway, the rag on them grey now from the morning. Then she had to tie it, and she had one hand to tie it with.

She tried to tuck the end under, and it slid out. She tried to make a half hitch round her own fingers and pull them out of it, dragging the linen with them. She tried again, every pass of the strip rasping across fingertips that were raw under the wrapping. Garran breathed his wet breath. The ward thinned under the crystal and she fed it, the cold coming up, the strip of linen hanging off his elbow like a wet ribbon off a maypole.

She had tied a bowline behind her back at eight years old, with her father's bosun counting, for a bet. She knew a sheet bend and a clove hitch and a rolling hitch and the knot the Kul Tiran gunners used on the breeching ropes, and she could not tie a rag round an old man's arm.

"Here."

Lyriel was crouched on the other side of the truckle bed. She had come without a sound, as they did, reaching across Garran to put her hands over Jaina's on the linen. They were quick and dry and warm, moving Jaina's hand for her as if it were a hand they had borrowed.

"Loop over your thumb. Like that." The linen went round. "Turn your wrist under." Jaina's wrist turned under, Lyriel's fingers on the back of it. "Tail through." The end went through the loop. "Now take it in your teeth and set it."

Then she let go and sat back on her heels, saying nothing.

Jaina looked at the knot she had not tied. Then she pulled it out.

Lyriel's eyebrows went up.

"Again," Jaina said.

Loop over the thumb. That went. Wrist under; and the loop slid off the end of her thumb as she turned, the thumb refusing to bend enough to hold it. She said a word she had learned from the gunners, and Lyriel's mouth twitched. Loop over the thumb again, further down this time, against the root of it. Wrist under. Tail through, which needed the tips of two fingers she barely had and took three tries, and then it was through.

"Now set it," Lyriel said. "Go on, Jaina. Bite it. It won't bite you back."

She bent and took the tail of the linen in her teeth. It tasted of vinegar and old blood and the goose grease off Tobin's hands. She pulled her head back and her hand back, against each other, closing the knot down on itself flat and small on the inside of Garran's arm. It held.

She looked at it.

It was a bandage knot. She had read the inside of this man's liver by spell light an hour ago, and here she was, kneeling on a stone floor with her chin practically on his elbow, wanting that knot more than she had wanted anything since Dalaran. That was absurd. It was a small useful thing, and she was ridiculous, and she wanted it anyway. She was going to tie it again on the next one, on her own, if it took her all afternoon.

And Lyriel had said *Jaina*. Just like that, in the middle of telling her to bite something.

"Where do the Farstriders learn that?" she said. "Or is it only you?"

"All of us. In the first year. You do it with the left until you can, and then they make you do it with the right." Lyriel was already standing up, the bucket in her other hand. "We learn it so we can patch our own mistakes."

She went back to the brazier.

Jaina knelt there, holding the staff against her ribs. Was that a joke? It sounded like one of Lyriel's: the plain thing said flat, then a look round to see if it had landed. She hadn't looked round. Their own mistakes. A ranger who had put her own hand somewhere it shouldn't go, and had to tie it up herself, one-handed, with her teeth, because nobody else was going to stop and do it for her.

Her left hand throbbed in its linen. She didn't look at it.

She tied the next dressing herself. It was on the cooper's boy, who had picked a scab on his knee down to the blood while he lay there with nothing else to do. It took her two tries and the knot came out lopsided, but she set it with her teeth and it held. The boy stared at her, round-eyed. "Did you just *bite* my bandage?" he said, in a voice of deep respect.

Then the ward on the woman from the top farm was thinning. She went to feed it.

They held, all of them, all the way round. She went round again and they were still holding, and nobody in the room was any better.

That was the part she could not get past. The wards held as the glass held: Garran was exactly as sick as he had been when she knelt down, and so was Elsie, and so was everyone else. They were only slower about it. And whatever had put a fist in Elsie overnight was still out there in the village, in somebody's kitchen, these people taking it in until this morning and every house in the street taking it in still. She was bailing with the plug out. She could go round this room with her bucket until she dropped, and the sea would still be coming in.

What were they swallowing?

She could not ask the light. It only told her where it was, not how it had got there.

She would have to ask them.

"Tobin," she said. "Have you got a slate?"

He had one hanging on a nail inside the door, a cracked one in a wooden frame, with a row of names down the side and strokes after them that he said were teeth—and who owed him for them. He wiped the teeth off with his sleeve without a pang and fished the slate pencil out of his apron.

"I can't hold it," Jaina said, showing him her hands. "You'll have to write. Down the side, everyone in this room. Then how long they've been ill. Then how much of it I found in them."

"How much?"

"Small. Half a fist. A fist. Everywhere." She heard herself and nearly laughed again—Antonidas would have had a fit. "Those will do."

He wrote in big square capitals, slowly, the tip of his tongue showing. GARRAN. COOPER, WIFE. COOPER, BOY. ELSIE. COL—which was the young man by the door, who turned out to be the smith's striker and lodged over the forge. WENNA DUNMORE. Down the side and across. She stood at his elbow with the staff in the crook of her arm, reading it upside down as she read Antonidas's letters on his desk when he was pretending she couldn't.

"Now the water," she said. "Which well does each of them drink from?"

Tobin did not need to ask anybody. He knew which house drew from which well as he knew whose teeth were whose, writing it in as he said it. "Cooper's Lane well, the cooper's lot. Garran, the same; he's three doors down. Col at the forge, the same." The pencil squeaked. "Top farm has its own, in the yard. Elsie's at Ashby's. Ashby's draws from the brook below the farm, because the Ashbys were always too mean to dig."

Jaina looked at what he had written.

Cooper's Lane well. Garran, everywhere. The cooper's wife and the boy, a fist each and more. Col, a walnut. Three houses on one well, and it ran from the worst in the room to the best.

So it wasn't the water. No—it might still be the water; the jug had read thin, and thin was thin, and she would not pretend she hadn't felt it. But the water didn't sort them. A whole lane drew one bucket and it came out of them as a dying man and a boy who'd picked his scab and a striker who thought Tobin was an old woman. If it was the water, it was the water for everybody. It didn't explain Garran.

"Who's been nursing Garran?" she said.

"Nobody, till I brought him up here. His wife's been gone since the spring. He's the cottage on his own."

Contact, then. Two down out of the five in the cooper's house, and that was one pot and one bucket and one bed and five people breathing each other's breath, exactly as she'd thought. And Garran, the first in the village and the worst in the room, had been alone in his cottage with nobody to catch it off.

Contact got the cooper and lost Garran. The well got the lane and lost the lane. She had two answers that fitted half the room each, and she wanted very badly for one of them to fit the whole of it—and Antonidas would have told her that was exactly when to stop wanting.

*I'm ruling out the comfortable answers first, Master.* Very good. And then what?

A girl came to the chapel door, carrying a basket on her hip.

She was fifteen or sixteen, round-faced, out of breath from the lane, her sleeves rolled to the elbow and white to the elbow too. She set the basket down on the step and leaned on the doorframe a moment, catching her breath.

"Mam says how are they, and is there anything else," she said.

"Bless her, tell her the same as yesterday, Ellen," Tobin said.

The girl looked at the lit staff in Jaina's arm and the elf at the brazier and backed off the step. She went away down the lane fast.

Tobin brought the basket in. There were three round loaves in it under a cloth, still warm; Jaina smelled them across the room through the vinegar. He took one out and tore it in half with his red cracked hands, and broke the soft middle into a bowl, small, smaller. Then he poured milk over it from a can and pressed it down with a spoon and carried it to the truckle bed.

"What are you giving him?"

"His sops." Tobin knelt and got his arm under Garran's head. "He's no teeth to speak of. I had the last two out of him at Midsummer. He lives on these, morning and night, since his wife went. Ellen sends the loaf up, or did, to the cottage, and he soaks it."

Jaina watched the spoon go to the old man's mouth. He didn't open his eyes. He took it as a baby takes it, lips working, swallowing.

"Wait," she said. "Wait. Write that. What they eat."

Tobin looked round at her, holding the spoon in the air.

"What they eat," Jaina said. "All of them. What they've eaten this week. Garran, bread in milk, twice a day. Write it."

She went round the room again, asking them this time.

The cooper's wife first, propped on one elbow in the box bed, speaking in a voice like a door hinge.

"Two loaves a day," she said. "From Ellen's mam. There's five of us, and who's time to bake, with him behind on his orders?"

"And what else do you eat?"

"Else?" The woman coughed, wiping her mouth on the blanket. "Bread and dripping. Bread and pottage. Bread and whatever there is."

"And your boy?"

"Him? Eats like a wolf. Eats his and half of mine."

Elsie, from the door-bed, turning her head on the pallet to answer. "Porridge at Ashby's in the morning, before the milking. Then bread and cheese in the field at noon. The mistress buys it in for the hands from the village." She made a face. "Cheaper than feeding the oven, she says."

Hew Dunmore sat on the floor at his wife's side with her hand in both of his, answering without lifting his eyes from her.

"We had the wet in our barley at harvest," he said. "Whole field. Black in the ear." His thumb moved over her knuckles. "So she's been buying flour off the store since, and baking her own. She eats the heel standing up. Every loaf. Says it's the best of it."

And Col, by the door, with his shirt still open over his walnut.

"Me? I've only been down two days." He'd been up on the hill pasture all summer with the smith's two cows and everybody else's, coming down with the cattle. "Oatcake, mostly. My mam's. She sends me up with a sack of her own meal and I do it on a stone by the fire. And whatever I can snare."

"And since you came down?"

"Bread and dripping," he said, shrugging. "Last night and the night before. There's no griddle at the forge."

Jaina stood still.

The staff ticked in the crook of her arm. The brazier spat, somebody coughing behind her, and she stood very still with all of it at the edges, looking at the slate in Tobin's hand.

Garran. Bread in milk, morning and night, for weeks. Everywhere.

The cooper's house. Two loaves a day and bread with everything. A fist and more.

Wenna Dunmore. Store flour since harvest, and the heel of every loaf. A fist, and the worst of the new ones.

Elsie. Porridge in the morning, bread at noon. A fist.

Col. Oatcake all summer, his mother's own meal. Two nights of bread and dripping. A walnut.

Oh.

Oh, there it was.

It went through her like the first warm swallow of wine on a cold night, from the throat right down. She had it. She'd had it out of the bodies the right way round—the gut first, then who, then how much, then what—without jumping once, putting the well and the touching up against the room and letting the room knock them down. Antonidas could have stood at her shoulder and watched every step and found nothing to—

In a room full of fevers. With Garran swallowing his sops. Lovely, Jaina. Shall we have a prize-giving?

She made herself look at the slate again.

Bread. So who bakes? Ellen's mother. Who mills? Who sells the flour to the store, and the store to the—

No. Slower. She was doing it again, running ahead of herself with the answer in her teeth, as she'd nearly run at the storehouse in Millhaven. She'd refused the storehouse there. A store is where things end up, she'd told Windrunner—the right thing to say then, and right here too. So: the oat-eaters. Rule out the comfortable answer before you go and build a house on it.

Oats were grain. Col's mother's oats were grain the same as Ellen's mother's flour, and Col had been eating them every day all summer, and Col had a walnut—a walnut that had come in two nights of somebody else's bread.

"Who else in the village eats their own oats?" she said. "No bread from the bakehouse. No flour from the store."

Tobin thought, rubbing the slate pencil along his jaw. "Mother Haw," he said, "at the end of Cooper's Lane. Won't have shop bread in the house, says it's chalk. Does oatcake on the griddle for herself and the grandson."

"Is she sick?"

"Her? She stopped me this morning to tell me my apron was a disgrace."

Cooper's Lane well. Her own oats. Not sick.

Not grain, then. One flour. One stock, whatever was in the bakehouse loaves and the store sacks, and the harder you ate it, and the longer, the worse you were. Garran, morning and night for weeks, and it had gone from his gut into everything. Col, two nights, a walnut. It was every meal. Was it ever one bad cup, one swallow at the wrong moment? Tobin was still by the truckle bed, kneeling with the bowl in his hand. There was milk on Garran's chin.

"Don't give him any more of that," Jaina said.

"He's had nothing else in a week."

"I know. Don't. Not any of them. Not the bread, not anything baked with the store's flour. If somebody has oats they grew themselves, give them that."

He looked at the bowl, then at the basket of loaves on the end of the door-bed, with the cloth turned back. "Ellen's a good girl," he said.

"I know she is." She didn't know what else to say about Ellen. "Please."

Tobin got up slowly, knees cracking, and carried the bowl to the slop bucket to tip it in.

Jaina went to the door. She needed air that didn't smell of pears. She needed to stand somewhere that wasn't next to Garran.

The chapel stood at the top of the lane with the whole village lying below it, roofs and gardens and smoke, going down toward the brook. She took hold of the doorframe with her left hand to steady herself, the wood gritty under the linen. It was in the cracks of the wood and the grain of the linen, and it would be on Ellen's sleeves all the way down the lane.

Down there, three roofs and two gardens below, a chimney stood up out of a low stone building with a long brick oven built against its end, its smoke coming out brown and warm and lying along the lane as it had lain over the whole village this morning when she rode in under it. Next to the bakehouse stood a bigger building, long and stone and windowless, with a hoist beam over a door set high in the wall at the height of a cart bed. A woman came out of the bakehouse with a loaf under each arm and went off up the street.

Jaina held the doorframe, watching her go.

The bakehouse was where it ended up. A bakehouse was a store with an oven in it. The flour had come into it from that long stone building next door, and the long stone building had got it from somewhere, from somebody, on some day. That was the half that would tell her whether this was one bad harvest gone wrong in a damp barn or something else. It wasn't in the chapel or on Tobin's slate. It was down there in the lanes, in who sold what to whom and when it had started and what it had cost. Windrunner was out there with it, and Jaina was up here holding a doorframe.

She could go down. It was not far; three roofs and two gardens. She could take Lyriel, who would come whether she was asked or not, walking into that long stone building to put the staff to the sacks and—

And what? Read them thin, or thick, standing there with a crystal against a sack of flour in front of whoever owned it, frightening him half to death, while up here the wards went out one by one.

Behind her, Wenna Dunmore said something about the hens in her sleep.

"What does he look like?" she said. "The new man."

"I asked," Sylvanas said. "Young. Civil. Bought the keeper a jug at the alehouse the first time he came, paying in good coin, asking after his wife's leg. The keeper liked him." One corner of her mouth went in. "I asked him for a face. He gave me a young man with a cart. Brown hair, he thinks. Or fair. A good horse. He'd know him again, he says."

"He wouldn't."

"No."

*Cheaper than Mister Hollis.* The girl had said it into Neral's collar in the dark at Millhaven, about a man who had called at her house and set her mother and her father quarrelling. A new man. Nobody at Millhaven had been able to say anything about him either, except that he was new and he was cheaper.

Sylvanas had stood half in the doorway at the inn while the girl talked. Jaina looked at her—one new man, selling cheap, at Millhaven and here—and Sylvanas looked back, and neither of them said it.

A new man, three months ago, with cheap flour. Cheap, so the keeper bought it, so the baker bought it, so the cooper's wife with five mouths and no time bought two loaves a day of it, and the top farm with its barley rotted in the field. Cheap was how it got into every house. Cheap was how it got into the old man who lived on sops.

"Somebody is selling it to them," Jaina said. Her voice came out very quiet. "Somebody is selling it to them on—"

She couldn't get the rest of it out at once.

"On purpose," Sylvanas said.

Neither of them said anything after that.

The brazier ticked. Garran's breath caught at the top, and caught, and caught. In the corner the little girl in the man's coat had fallen asleep against the wall, her shawl in her fist. Behind Sylvanas's shoulder Cyndia stood without moving at all, and across the room Lyriel had put the bucket down. Jaina was holding the staff so hard against her ribs that the silver wire had printed itself through her bodice.

Sylvanas's hand had gone to the string of her bow, resting there. She was looking past Jaina, over the cots, at the shuttered wall, as if she could see through it and down the lane to the long stone building with the hoist beam.

"How much of his flour is left?" she said.

"Most of the last load," Cyndia said. "It's in the loft. The keeper showed me the sacks."

"Then nobody takes another sack out of there until we've seen it." Sylvanas looked at Jaina. "Can you leave them?"

Could she? Jaina looked round the room at the blue mesh over every bed, already beginning to thin over Garran's. "The wards will stand until full dark," she said. "Perhaps a little after. Then I have to be here."

"Then we're quick." Sylvanas was already turning to the door. "Lyriel. With the mage. Cyndia, with me."

Jaina went round the room once more, fast, feeding every ward as full as she could make it take, until the cold had climbed past her elbow and her teeth ached from it and the blue stood up thick over all of them. Tobin watched her do it, holding the slate against his apron.

"No bread," she told him at the door.

"No bread," Tobin said. "I heard you the first time."

Lyriel put Jaina's cloak round her shoulders without asking and fastened it at the throat, quick, because Jaina's left hand would not have managed the clasp. Then they went out into the lane after the other two. The sky over the roofs had gone the colour of a bruise. The air was cold, smelling of chimney smoke. Below them the bakehouse smoke still lay along the lane, low and brown and warm. Sylvanas was already two gardens down the hill, walking fast toward the granary. Jaina went down after her, gathering her skirts in her bad hand.

The granary had no windows. She had seen that from the chapel door. It was worse close to: a long blind wall of fieldstone going up into the dusk, the hoist beam sticking out over the high door like the arm of a gallows, the high door shut. The big doors at the yard end were shut too, a bar dropped across them into iron hasps. No lock on the bar. Who would steal a granary?

Sylvanas stopped at the corner of the bakehouse, putting her bare hand up. They stopped behind her.

The bakehouse was dark. Somebody had raked the oven out for the night. Heat still came off the brick end of it as Jaina came up, a soft dry warmth on the side of her face that smelled of scorched crust. Her stomach turned over at it. She'd eaten nothing since the saddle at noon. She would not, she found, be eating bread for a while.

Sylvanas was looking up.

Jaina looked up too. At first there was nothing, only the dark wall and the beam and the sky gone to bruise behind them—then she had it: a line of yellow under the high door, thin as a thread, where the door didn't quite meet its sill. It went out. It came back. Somebody was walking up and down between a lamp and that door.

"The keeper?" Lyriel said, so low it was hardly a sound.

"The keeper went home to his supper," Cyndia said. "I watched him drop the bar."

The bar was on the outside. Jaina looked at it and at the thread of light, the skin going tight across the back of her neck. Whoever was in there had not come in by the big doors. Whoever it was had not wanted a light where the street could see it.

Sylvanas touched Cyndia's arm and pointed, without a word, at a low door in the side wall half hidden behind a water butt. Cyndia went to it along the wall with her bow in her left hand and an arrow lying across the grip, and laid her palm on the planks. The door went inward a finger's width on leather hinges and stopped. Not barred. Not even latched.

Then Sylvanas turned round to look at Jaina, pointing at the ground beside the water butt. *There. Stay.*

Jaina shook her head. She lifted the staff an inch in the crook of her arm, which was all the argument she had room for. You won't know what you're looking at. I will. She had no idea what her face was doing. Sylvanas looked at her for as long as it took to breathe in and out once, her mouth going thin, and pointed again—at the ground behind her own heels.

*There, then.*

They went in.

Dark, and the smell of it hit her in the throat: grain dust and mouse and old sacking and, under all of it, faint, a sweetness she had been breathing all afternoon. The staff woke in her arm before her eyes had made out anything at all. It didn't tick here. It ground. The late drag that had come and gone in the lanes all day, thin and spread, came up through the ash in one long pull, so heavy and so slow that her back teeth ached with it. She had to stop, leaning her shoulder against the wall, letting it go through her.

Millhaven. The storehouse by the bridge, the loudest place in that whole dead village, and she had stood in the door of it and told Sylvanas Windrunner that a store is where things end up.

Well. Here was where it ended up.

Lyriel put a hand at the small of her back and kept it there. Jaina pushed off the wall.

Shapes came up out of the dark. Sacks, stacked on low trestles to keep them off the floor, row on row of them going back into black. A cart-bed's worth of empty sacking in a heap. A stair of bare planks going up the inner wall to the loft, with no rail on it. At the top of the stair, the light: no thread now but a soft yellow wash on the rafters, a shadow moving across it. And a sound. A small, steady, careful sound, like somebody prising a lid off a box.

Sylvanas went up the stair first, an arrow on the string and the bow held low, the planks making no sound under her. Cyndia went after her, keeping to the wall side. Jaina went third, because Lyriel's hand at her back pushed her third. The planks talked under her boots on every step, every single one, her skirts dragging on the treads behind her. They should have left her by the water butt.

Then her head came up over the edge of the loft.

There was a lamp on the boards. There were sacks stacked to the roof-tree behind it, new ones, the sacking pale and clean, one of them open at the mouth and slumped over with the flour run out of it in a white tongue. There was a young man on his knees between the lamp and the sacks, a floorboard lifted up beside him and his arm in the hole under it to the elbow, taking out a jar.

He was very young. Younger than Dickon—no. Not younger, only thinner; a long white neck and big red hands and hair cut close to the skull, flour in the creases of his knuckles and down the front of his smock. The jar was clay, round-bellied, the size of a baby's head, with a wad of waxed cloth tied over the mouth. He had it in both hands, lifting it out of the hole very carefully, as her father's cook lifted a full tureen.

He looked up.

He looked at the arrow first. Then at Cyndia's arrow, beside it. Then past them at Jaina's head, coming up out of the stairwell like a turnip, and the blue light of the staff. His face stayed blank a moment. It was only a boy's face, with a fluff of fair hair along the lip.

"Put it down," Sylvanas said.

He did. He put the jar down on the boards beside the hole, gently, both hands, and Jaina had half a heartbeat to think it—good, he was frightened, he'd talk—

And then his hand went past the jar for the lamp.

His knee caught the jar as he lunged. It went over and rolled, a hollow clay sound across the boards, fetching up against the edge of the lifted plank and breaking there, not loudly, a dull wet crack like an egg in a fist. He had the lamp by its bail. He was up on one knee with his arm going back to throw it, the flame streaming sideways in the glass, and behind him and all around him and right up under the rafters the air was moving: the dust off the sacks and the spilled flour, hanging, turning, lit gold by the lamp all through the loft like midges over a pond.

A mill. There had been a mill on the quay at Boralus that went up the winter she was six. She had been in bed. She remembered the windows of her room coming in.

She never saw the arrow go. She heard it: a hard flat smack like a shutter slammed in a wind. The boy's arm stopped in the air.

The arrow had gone through the loose sleeve of his smock above the wrist and on into the post behind him, nailing the sleeve there. His arm hung from it. The lamp hung from his fist on its bail, swinging, oil slopping out of the spout to run down the outside of the glass in a thin blue lick of fire and drip, burning, onto the boards.

Through the sleeve. She had watched Sylvanas put an arrow through a running doe's heart at the holly bend. This one had gone through cloth.

Sylvanas was already across the loft. Jaina hadn't seen her cross it either. Reaching up past the pinned arm, Sylvanas took the lamp out of the boy's fist by the bail, quite gently, as if he'd been holding it for her. She wound the wick down to a bead with her gloved fingers and set it behind her on a sack, out of his reach. Then she put her boot on the burning oil and ground it out.

The boy screamed. He tore at the pinned sleeve with his free hand, kicking out at the boards. The smock ripped, a long rending sound, the arrow-shaft bending, the post creaking. He was coming loose. He was going to come loose and get up and do something else, and nobody was near enough but Sylvanas, and Sylvanas had both hands full of lamp—

Jaina set the heel of the staff on the boards and pushed.

She did it as she would have done it to a bucket of water. There wasn't any other way she knew. The cold went out of her and down the ash in a white feathering, crackling across the planks and up over his boots and shins and knees, locking him there, kneeling, to the floor and to the foot of the post. His free hand had hold of his own sleeve. The frost went over that too, climbing his wrist, stiffening the cloth and his fingers into the cloth before it stopped.

He stopped, making a high thin sound through his nose.

She had done this once before, on the road at the holly bend, to a wolf's hind legs. Never to a person. A person made a different sound.

The cold came back up the staff as it always came back. It came into her left hand, turning the linen on it hard as card in an instant. The fingers inside it, which had been nothing much all afternoon, went to nothing at all; it ran up past the wrist and the elbow and stopped somewhere in the shoulder, a bar of ice laid along the bone. She kept the staff where it was, breathing through her teeth.

For a moment nobody moved.

The boy knelt in the frost with his arm nailed up behind him, his face turned away from them all. Sylvanas stood over him with the lamp at her back. Cyndia had come up off the stair and stood at the edge of the loft with an arrow still on her string, Lyriel beside Jaina, close. They had him. Alive, frightened, and nobody had told him yet what to say. They had the hole under the floor and the open sack and the jar.

The jar.

The smell came up off the boards and hit her all at once, her eyes running with it. Pears gone brown. It was Garran's breath and Wenna's breath and the whole of the chapel, but as if somebody had taken every sickroom in the village and boiled them down together to a spoonful, and the spoonful was here, on the floor, at her feet. Under the sweetness there was something else, thin and sharp and wrong at the back of the nose, like the smell of a struck flint.

It was dark in the lamplight, darker than oil. It had come out of the broken jar in a puddle no bigger than a dinner plate. It was not staying a puddle. It ran, going along the grain of the boards in thin dark fingers—toward the crack between two planks, toward the hole under the lifted board, toward the open sack with its tongue of flour; toward Sylvanas's boots.

Underneath this floor were the sacks on their trestles. Rows and rows of them.

"Back," Jaina said. "Get back from it. Don't breathe it. Ranger-General, your feet—"

Sylvanas stepped back without looking down.

There wasn't time to do it properly. The wards over the cots had taken her the whole of the afternoon to set, slow nets laid down thread by thread so they would hold their shape without her; there was no afternoon here. There was the next crack in the floor. She lifted the staff off the boy's frost, feeling the hold stand without her—just—and brought the heel down again on the edge of the spill, and threw it.

It came down like a bowl dropped over a candle. A shell of blue, hard, pressed flat to the boards, its edges biting into the wood with a hiss like a hot iron laid on wet cloth. The dark fingers ran to the edge of it and stopped. She watched one of them climb a hair's breadth up the inside of the curve and slide back. The fumes came up off the puddle to lie under the shell like smoke under glass, going no further.

The price came up the staff a breath later.

It went into the left hand, and something in the hand gave. She felt it go as a seam had once gone under her arm when she stretched for a top shelf in the Citadel library: a small tearing, low down in the palm, and then warm, and then the warm going cold inside the linen until she couldn't tell any more what the hand was doing. Her arm was ice from the fingers to the shoulder. She leaned on the staff because she needed to, her knees going down onto the boards of their own accord. She let them.

"Jaina." Lyriel, at her side.

"I'm all right. I'm reading it. Let me read it."

She put her face close to the shell. Through the blue the stuff lay black and still, a skin already forming on top like cooling jam. She put her whole attention down the ash and into it, because if she was going to pay for it she was going to have it.

It was the same. That was the first thing. It was the thing in Garran's gut and the cooper's wife's, in the doe's blood at the holly bend and the well at Millhaven—the same late drag, the same heaviness, like something hung on the back of every beat, the family resemblance so plain she could have picked it out of a crowd. And it was nothing like them. In the sick it was thin and smeared and everywhere. In here it was whole. It had a shape. It had been built.

She could feel where the parts of it met. One part to keep. One part to wait. One part that did something under the other two that she could not follow at all; she came at it from one side and slid off, coming at it from another and sliding off again, as her eye had slid off the Zandalari script on the cargo tags in her father's warehouse. It was no single spell. It was a stack of them, laid down one on another like the planking of a hull, every seam caulked. And it had gone into a sack of flour and through a baker's oven and into a man's milk, and kept. Kept its shape the whole way and sat down in him and waited.

Oh, Light.

She knew what this was. She knew it as she knew where the reefs lay off Tiragarde—from the charts, from the warnings, from the ships that hadn't come home: the death-currents, the work that ties a thing to the body past the body's end. Every apprentice in Dalaran knew that much. They walked you past the locked shelf in your first year, telling you what was on it and why the grille was there. They gave you the signs, so you'd know it if you met it. They never gave you the grammar. That was the whole point of the grille.

This was written in the grammar. She could read every letter of it and not one word.

And it was beautiful. That came up in her before she could stop it, clean and hot. Whoever had made this had made it as Antonidas built a ward, without one wasted turn. She wanted to take it apart. She wanted to sit down on these boards with a candle and a week and find out what the third part did.

With Garran swallowing it in his sops. Lovely. Write that in the report.

"It's made," she said. Her voice came out hoarse. "Somebody made this. Somebody very good."

Nobody answered her.

There was light coming up the stair, yellow and jumping, and boots on the planks—a lot of boots—and Brask's voice at the bottom calling something she didn't catch. Then his head came up over the edge of the loft with a torch held up beside it, Dickon's behind him with another, the light shaking all over the loft, over the rafters and the pale sacks and the blue shell on the floor and the boy's face.

The boy's face was wet. He must have been crying since the frost went over him, and she hadn't heard a sound.

"Tam?" Brask said.

He came up the rest of the way and stood there with the torch, looking. At the frost. At the arrow in the post. At the hole in the floor. His thumb was moving on the torch handle as it had moved on the spear.

"That's Tam," he said, to nobody. "He works the hoist for Joss. He came looking for work at the back end of summer. Joss took him on." And then, to the boy, in a different voice, a father's voice: "Tam. What's this? What've you done?"

Tam didn't look at him, keeping his face turned away.

"Cyndia," Sylvanas said. "Lyriel."

They went to him. Cyndia cut the sleeve away from the arrow with her belt knife, one stroke. Jaina let the hold go, and his legs went out from under him as soon as she did; they'd been kneeling in her frost all this while, with no more feeling in them than her hand. Cyndia and Lyriel took him under the arms and hauled him up between them, holding him there with his boots dragging and his head down.

Jaina got up too. It took the staff and the post and a while.

"Who made it?" she said.

He lifted his head. In the torchlight his eyes were very pale and very young, looking at her, then past her at the blue shell on the floor and the black stuff lying under it. He looked at it the way the little ones in the cathedral at Boralus used to look at the reliquary when the priest carried it down the aisle on a feast day—and she had been one of them, at the back, holding her nurse's hand: as if it might do something, and he would be glad, and he was terrified of it.

"I'm not to speak to you," he said.

"Who told you that?"

"I'm not to speak to you." His eyes came back to her. "You don't know what you're keeping them from."

Brask made a sound in his throat.

Jaina opened her mouth and found she had nothing to put in it. Who made this, how, from what, where did it come from, who gave it to you, who gives it to them, what does the third part *do*—and he didn't know. She could see it as plainly as she had seen the knots in the gut. He didn't know what was in the jar any more than she had known what was in the reliquary. He had been told to keep it, and to keep it dark, and he had kept it.

"He's a lookout," Sylvanas said, not moving from beside the lamp. "Someone gave him a jar and a floorboard and told him to watch. That's all he's for." She looked at Brask. "Captain. Is there somewhere here that locks?"

Brask was still looking at the boy, saying nothing.

"Captain."

"The cage," he said. "By the pound. At the bottom of Chapel Lane."

"Then we'll take him there. Cyndia, the stair. Nobody comes up. Nobody touches that." A tip of her head at the blue shell. "Mage. Will it hold?"

Jaina looked at it. At her hand, a shape in grey linen gone stiff and dark at the palm. "Until morning," she said. "If nobody puts a foot on it."

"Nobody will." That was to Cyndia, who had already gone to stand at the head of the stair with her bow. Sylvanas took the lamp up off the sack. "Down."

It was full dark in the lane.

Brask went first with the torch, the light going ahead of him over the cobbles and up the garden walls, showing her a cat on a gatepost, a bucket, a child's wooden horse lying on its side in a doorway, sliding off them again. Tam came behind him between Lyriel and Brask's man with the pitchfork, the man's free hand clamped round the boy's arm above the elbow; he kept looking at it as if he didn't know how it had got there. Tam's legs were coming back to him. He walked like a drunk, bow-kneed, his boots squelching where her frost had melted into them, keeping his chin down on his chest and his collar.

Crying again. She couldn't hear it. She didn't look.

She was looking at the sky. Full dark. She had told Sylvanas full dark, or a little after, and here it was—the last green gone out of the west and the stars come out sharp over the bakehouse roof—and up the hill behind her the chapel had a door and a window and six wards going thin in it one after another, Garran's first. It was all she could do not to turn round and go up the lane at a run. The cage was at the bottom of Chapel Lane. On the way. She told herself that, walking downhill, away from it.

Sylvanas walked behind the boy, near enough to touch him. She'd slung the bow. The lamp swung from her left hand, wick still down to a bead, its little light lying on the back of Tam's neck, on the fair fuzz there and the bones under it.

Jaina's own left arm hung at her side like something borrowed. When she tried to make the fingers move inside the stiff linen they did, a little, or seemed to. Then the palm woke and told her about it, a long deep burn as if she'd closed her hand on a poker, and she stopped trying.

The pound was a ring of drystone wall at the foot of the lane with a gate in it and a ewe inside, which got up as the torch came by and stood blinking. The cage stood against the pound wall, a little round house of fieldstone no bigger than a bread oven, with a stone dome for a roof and a door of iron bars set in it. Brask had the key on a ring at his belt. He fumbled it, swore, and fumbled it again. Jaina watched his big cracked fingers find the right one by feel in the end, without looking, as he must have done a hundred times for a drunk on a fair night. The door went open with a scream of iron.

They put Tam in. There was a stone bench along the back wall and straw on the floor; Lyriel set him down on the bench, not ungently, and came out. Brask swung the door to, turned the key, and stepped back.

Tam sat on the bench with his hands between his knees.

Sylvanas gave the lamp to Lyriel and went up to the bars. She didn't touch them. She stood a pace back from them with her arms at her sides, looking in at him for a while. He didn't look up.

"Who gives you the jar?" she said.

Nothing.

"Who brings the flour? The young man with the cart. Does he bring the jar too?"

Nothing. Tam's shoulders moved, once, as if he'd shivered.

Jaina came up beside her into the lamplight. Close to, the bars breathed cold into her face, the cold of iron that had stood in the night air since sundown. She leaned in so her cheek was nearly against them and held up the staff, sending the blue light into the cage over the straw and the bench and the boy.

"Tam," she said. "Look at me. Slowly. Nobody is going to hurt you. Start with who gave you the jar, and we'll leave everything else for—"

He looked up.

There was something on his lip. White. She thought it was flour, at first; he'd had flour all over him. Then it bubbled.

He looked at her with his pale eyes, his face perfectly calm, calmer than it had been all evening—and then it wasn't. His back arched. His heels went out from under him, scraping across the straw. He slid off the bench sideways onto the floor, his whole body drawing up tight as a bowstring, shaking, and shaking.

"Key," Sylvanas said.

Brask fumbled it. Sylvanas took the ring out of his hand and had the door open before he'd finished letting go of it, and was in, and down on her knees on the stone. She turned the boy on his side. She got her bare fingers into his mouth, between his teeth, and his teeth closed on them and she didn't take them out; she hooked whatever she could out of his cheek and threw it on the straw and went in again for more.

Jaina stood at the bars with her face against the iron. She couldn't have said when she'd put it there.

It didn't take long.

The shaking went out of him in waves, each one smaller, as the hum goes out of a struck bell. His heels stopped scraping. There was a long breath out of him through the foam—a sigh, almost—and then no breath after it. Sylvanas stayed where she was on her knees with her fingers in his mouth until there was nothing more to stay for. Then she took them out.

Nobody said anything. The ewe in the pound had gone back down in her corner. The torch hissed in Brask's fist. From somewhere up the lane a dog barked twice and gave it up. After that Jaina could hear the torch, and the blood in her own ears, and nothing else whatever.

She went in.

She knelt on the stone beside him because she didn't know what else to do and because the staff was something to do. He was warm. That was what she hadn't been ready for. He lay on the cold floor with his cheek in the straw, warm through the smock where her knee touched his back, warm as anybody, a little foam on his lip—only a little—and his eyes half open. She put the staff to him. There was nothing in him. No drag, no weight, no knot under the ribs; he had never eaten one mouthful of what he kept under that floor. There was just a boy, stopped.

"Here," Sylvanas said.

She had the collar of his smock turned over in her fingers. Its corner was chewed through, a ragged wet hole in the hem no bigger than a fingernail. Inside the hem, when she pulled it open, there was a fold of waxed paper, empty, and a smear of something brownish on the cloth.

"He had it in his collar," Sylvanas said. "Sewn in. He had his chin on it all the way down the lane."

Crying, she'd thought. She'd thought he was crying.

Sylvanas sat back on her heels, wiping her fingers on her coat. There were teeth marks in two of them, deep, and she didn't look at those either. She looked at the boy.

"Somebody taught him that," she said. "They sewed it in for him and told him when."

Brask was standing in the door of the cage with the torch, holding it low so the light came from below and made his face strange. "He ate at my table," he said. "At the harvest supper. He sat next to my youngest and cut her meat for her."

Jaina looked down at Tam. *I'm not to speak to you.* And he hadn't. He'd been told he wouldn't have to, and he'd believed it, and he'd been right.

They had had him. In the loft, with the frost to his waist and the arrow through his sleeve and his face wet, and nobody had told him yet what to say. And somebody had. Somebody had told him months ago, with a needle and thread.

She got up off her knees, which took a while again.

Brask was talking. She came back into it in the middle. "—Joss up first thing, and we'll go through the loft in daylight, and I'll want to hear it from Joss what he knows about this lad, and then we'll see. We'll see what's to be done." He was looking at Tam, not at them, his thumb going up and down the torch handle. "In the morning. I'll have the whole of it in the morning."

In the morning.

Jaina looked up the lane. Somewhere up there in the dark, behind the bakehouse with its brick oven raked out and cooling, the baker was asleep—the big soft man white to the eyebrows—and so was Ellen's mother. In the morning, before light, as they did it in every village in Lordaeron and every port in Kul Tiras, one of them would get up and light that oven. And put the loaves in. And Ellen would carry them round in her basket with the cloth over, warm, to the cooper's house and the top farm and everybody, and to the chapel, to Tobin, for his sops.

And Brask would have the whole of it in the morning. He was not a fool. He had a dead boy on the floor of his cage that he'd fed at his own table, and two strangers with bows, and a mage with a jar of something nobody could see. He wanted one night. Anybody would want one night.

She could tell him. She could explain it, all of it—the stack and the seams and the third part—in a voice that carried. He'd stand there with his thumb going on the torch, hearing one word in five and wanting his night more, not less.

She turned to Sylvanas instead.

"Ranger-General." She said it low, as Sylvanas had spoken to him at the spear. "The baker lights his oven before light. If the captain waits for the morning, they'll bake with it. The whole village will have it for breakfast. I can ward a room. I can't ward a village."

Sylvanas looked at her. Then past her, at Brask in the door with his torch held low and his face gone heavy and strange from underneath. She looked at him for a long moment, hands at her sides and nothing in them, as she had in the lane before noon.

"Captain," she said. "Your baker lights his oven before dawn?"

"Hob?" Brask lifted his head. "He does. He's at it by cockcrow, him and Nan."

"Then you have until cockcrow."

That was all she said. She didn't explain it. She stood there letting him have the rest of it himself, as she had let him look at his own street. Jaina watched him have it. His eyes went up the lane, toward the bakehouse. His thumb stopped.

"Tell me what to shut," he said.

Jaina told him. "The granary. The bakehouse. Tonight, now, before anyone's up. Nobody bakes, nobody sells, nobody carries a sack out of either. And every sack of that man's flour that's gone into any house, since the first load—"

"Every house?"

"I'm sorry, Captain. Every house."

He looked at her for the first time since the loft. Then at the boy on the floor. "Burn it, then," he said. "All of it. In Joss's yard, where they can see it. I'll answer for the flour." He turned round in the door of the cage, raising his voice. "Piers. Get your brother and the Aldon boys and go round the doors on this side of the brook, every one, and anyone that's bought flour off Joss since harvest, you bring it out to the granary yard. Sacks, crocks, the lot. If they give you any trouble you send them to me." The man with the pitchfork went off up the lane at a run. "Dickon. Joss sent a cart down to the ford this morning with flour for the Hallams and the alehouse. Take the two ponies and Wat and go after it and turn it round, and don't let anybody eat off it. Go on."

Dickon went.

Brask stood in the door a moment longer, looking at the boy. Then he said, quite quietly, "Cover his face, somebody," and went off down toward the granary with the torch, and the light went with him.

Lyriel had a spare cloak rolled behind her shoulders. She shook it out to lay it over Tam, over his face and his chewed collar, tucking the edge under before she stood up.

"I have to go up," Jaina said. "The wards."

"Lyriel. With her," Sylvanas said, and went after Brask without waiting to see them go.

Jaina went up Chapel Lane faster than she'd gone up anything in her life, her skirts in her dead hand and the staff clamped in her elbow and Lyriel's hand at her back the whole way. The lane was steeper going up than it had been coming down. Her breath sobbing in her chest by the yew, she shouldered the chapel door open.

Tobin was on a stool by Garran's bed, holding a candle. He looked round at her, his face grey. She saw the ward over the old man before she saw anything else. It was a rag—a cobweb with the sun through it, three threads and a hole. Under it, the old man's breath caught at the top, and caught, and caught.

She went down on her knees on the flags and put the staff to him, feeding it.

She did Garran, then all the others, round the room, cot and box bed and hurdle and door-bed. By the time she had been round once Garran's had thinned again, sending her back to him. She did the room again. Every one of them had thinned—not as far as his, though every one, while she had been down in the dark with a jar and a boy. Elsie's was holding by its corners. The cooper's wife's was going at the middle like a worn sheet. She mended them, going round again.

Somewhere in the rounds she noticed the smell.

It came in under the door and through the cracks of the shutters. Smoke, though not wood smoke; heavier, and sour, with a scorch in it, like bread left in the oven while somebody went to the door and stayed there talking. She went to the window over the altar and wiped the clear pane with the heel of her bad hand and looked down.

The granary yard was full of fire. Down there beyond the black roofs, beyond the bakehouse chimney, a heap of something burned in the middle of the yard with a smoky red flame. Men were going to it and away from it with sacks on their shoulders, black shapes against it, throwing the sacks in. Every sack went in with a puff of sparks that went straight up. Then the flame sank down and sulked over it and spread, and came up again. Somebody had a crock and was tipping it out. She thought she could see Brask by the shape of him, standing at the yard gate with a slate or a board or something, putting his head down over it every time a man came in with a sack.

There was no smoke from the bakehouse chimney. It stood up black and cold against the glow beside it, for the first time all day.

She went back to Garran.

The wards were time. That was all they were. She sat back on her heels between rounds with the staff across her knees, making herself say it plainly, because Antonidas would have, and because she didn't want to. They didn't take one grain of it out of anybody. They held the door, so that nothing more could come in and nothing that was in could get any further. What was in was in, and the body would do the rest or it wouldn't. And she didn't know which. She had never seen this before. Nobody had; or if somebody had, they'd written it on the far side of the grille.

The bread had stopped. The bread was burning down there in a yard. That was the door shut from the other side. It had taken Sylvanas and a man with a torch and a heap of burning sacks to shut it, and not one single thing she knew.

So she held the door from this side, which was all she had. It would have to do.

She went round again.

The fevers ticked. That was how she heard them, after a while: Garran's breath catching, and the cooper's wife's slow rasp, and the boy's quick light snore, and the brazier, and the staff in the crook of her arm ticking with every bed she passed—all of it going together like a room full of clocks that didn't agree. She couldn't feel a forehead with her hands. Her right hand had felt nothing since the holly bend and her left was a stiff bundle she didn't like to look at. So she did it as her nurse had done it, a long time ago, in a house on a hill over the sea, bending to lay her cheek against them. Elsie's was hot and dry. The boy's was hot. Wenna Dunmore's was hot and wet, and Jaina stayed bent over her longer than she meant to, trying to tell whether wet was better or worse, not knowing.

Then Garran's, which was like leaning against a stove.

The boy in the cage. She'd been keeping him off. He came anyway, between Wenna and Garran, with the foam on his lip.

If she'd been quicker. If she'd thrown the shell over that spill in one breath instead of two, and come back to him, and stood over him while they hauled him up, putting the staff on him—would she have seen it? Seen what? A fold of paper in a collar? She'd have seen a boy. She'd been looking straight at him in the loft and seen a boy, crying. If she'd frozen him to the neck instead of the waist—

His jaw. She could have frozen his jaw. She sat with that for a moment, very still. A boy's face, the frost going up over his mouth and his nose, and the noise he would have made. Would she have done it, if she'd known? She didn't know—she had hated doing it to a wolf.

And if she had, he'd have thawed in the end and sat in the cage with his chin on his collar, saying *I'm not to speak to you*, waiting. He'd been waiting for months.

Slower. That was no good either. She was doing the sums backward from the answer she wanted—that it was her fault—because if it was her fault there was something she could have done.

Garran's ward was thinning. She fed it.

Some time in the deep of the night Lyriel came and sat down on the flags beside her and took her left hand without asking. Jaina let her. She didn't want to look, and she did look. The linen across the palm had gone black-red and stiff. When Lyriel cut it off with her knife there was a split in the heel of the hand like a split in an overripe plum, gone shiny with the cold.

"Hold still," Lyriel said. "You've bled through." She washed it with water from Tobin's can, quick, Jaina hissing through her teeth, Lyriel not stopping. Clean linen, round and round. Over the thumb, wrist under, tail through, and Lyriel set it in her own teeth and bit it down, quick, and let go. "Don't let Tobin see that. He'll want to bleed you as well."

Jaina looked at the knot.

"I can tie that now," she said.

"Not on your own hand, you can't. Not yet." Lyriel got up. "Tomorrow."

Some time after that Cyndia came in with the cold of the night on her cloak and a bundle under her arm done up in a cloth. She stood just inside the door and reported to the room in general, standing straight, as she would have reported to Sylvanas.

"Brask's man came up from the loft and took my place on the stair. The woman at the end of Cooper's Lane was on her step watching the fire. I asked her for oats. She gave me these." She held out the bundle. "Oatcakes. Her own oats, from her own field. She wants the cloth back."

Mother Haw. Jaina could have laughed. "Thank you," she said.

Cyndia gave the bundle to Tobin and went back out without another word. Tobin broke the cakes up small into a bowl with his red hands, poured milk over them, and pressed them down with a spoon before he carried them to Garran.

Sylvanas came once, very late. Jaina didn't hear her come; she was simply in the doorway, in the dark, the glow of the yard behind her and a smell coming off her cloak of scorched flour and smoke. She didn't come in. She stood with her back to the door frame and her left hand on it, looking round the room—at the cots, the wards, Tobin asleep on his stool with his chin on his chest. At Jaina, on her knees by Elsie.

"It's burned," she said. "All of the granary. All of the bakehouse."

"The houses?"

"Most of them. Brask is still going round." A pause. "The cart's not back."

"And the loft?"

"Brask's man on the stair. Nobody's been up."

"Thank you, Ranger-General."

Sylvanas looked at her a moment longer. Then she went, and the doorway was dark again.

Jaina bent to lay her cheek against Elsie's forehead. Hot. She sat back, planning the round: Garran next, then the cooper's wife, then the boy. Then Elsie again. And somewhere in the grey before dawn the shell on the loft floor would thin and let go, with that black skin lying loose under it and one of Brask's men sitting on the stair below, and there was nothing she could do about that from here but put it in the round with the rest.

---

It rained a little before dawn. She heard it come, soft on the chapel roof and then steadier, the smell of the yard fire going out of the air under it. Good. That was the embers seen to. She fed the cooper's wife's ward.

When the window over the altar went grey she was on her knees by Elsie again.

The girl was wet through, her hair stuck flat to her forehead and the striped blanket dark at the neck with it. She was awake, blinking at the ceiling as if she had never seen one before.

"I'm all wet," she said, in a cross little voice. "Is there water?"

Jaina bent to lay her cheek against the girl's forehead and held it there.

Cool. Damp, and cool, like the outside of a jug.

She sat back on her heels. She didn't say anything. She looked at Elsie, and Elsie looked back at her, puzzled, saying, "Is there?" again, and Lyriel was already coming across from the brazier with Tobin's can.

The cooper's boy was awake too, sitting up on his pallet with his lopsided bandage, wanting to know if there was any more of the oat stuff and why couldn't he have bread, he always had bread. Col was on his feet by the door, holding his shirt shut. He was perfectly well, he said, and he was starving, and he was going home. Wenna Dunmore slept. Her husband slept on the floor beside her, keeping one hand on her blanket. When Jaina laid her cheek against Wenna's forehead it was warm, only warm, a sleeper's warmth.

Garran was the same.

She went round twice to be sure. His breath still caught at the top, and caught, and caught, his skin still dry and hot as a stove, and under the crystal the brown was still out along his arms and up his neck. It was no further than it had been in the deep of the night. That was all she could say for it. It was no further.

The rest were holding. The early ones were more than holding.

On time. Here, in this room, with these people, they had been on time, and it had held. She tried the words over. They sounded like something for the bottom of a report, underlined twice, and she didn't trust them. She went and put her cheek to Elsie's forehead again.

Still cool.

"Jaina." Lyriel was watching her from the brazier. "She's the same as she was the last time. And the time before."

"I know."

She let the wards stand. They would hold a while on their own now, and she had nothing left to put in them. After a moment she found that she was sitting on the bench under the window with the staff across her knees, unable to remember sitting down. The hum went out of her hands slowly. First out of the right, where she'd never felt it much, then out of the left, and as it went the left woke up behind it in pins and then in burning, the long split in the heel of the palm throbbing under Lyriel's linen with every beat of her heart.

Garran's bandage had slipped again. She saw it from the bench. The pad had come off the cut at his elbow in the night, the strip hanging loose round his arm.

She got up. She knelt by the truckle bed and put the staff in the crook of her dead arm and got the pad back on with her left hand, the fingers poking out of the new wrapping just far enough to do it. Round once. Round again. Over the thumb. Wrist under, and the loop held at the root of her thumb where she'd learned to put it. Tail through, first time. She bent and took it in her teeth and pulled her head back, and the knot went down flat and small on the inside of his arm, and held.

It was a silly thing to want. A rag on an old man's arm, after a night like that. She looked at it anyway, a long moment, while it went on holding, feeling better than she had any right to.

When she looked up, Lyriel was grinning at her from the brazier, saying nothing. She tipped her head at the knot, once, then looked across at the door where Cyndia had been standing in the night, as though Cyndia might have seen it too. Cyndia wasn't there. Lyriel seemed satisfied anyway.

Jaina went out into the yard.

The roofs below the chapel were wet and dark and shining, every one of them, the light coming up pale yellow behind the hill and catching the wet slates and the thatch and the puddles in the lane. There was smoke from the chimneys, thin, grey, hearth smoke going straight up in the still air. There was no smoke from the bakehouse. The long brick end of it stood there black and cold, and the village lay under the morning without the brown warm haze it had lain under yesterday when she rode in, looking smaller without it, and barer. She could see clear down to the brook.

In the granary yard there was a black heap, flattened by the rain, steaming a little. A cart stood beside it with its shafts down. The cart from the ford, then. Somebody was still forking sacks off it onto the heap, somebody else trying to light it again.

Millhaven had had smoke in its chimneys too, once. A cradle with the blanket tucked in. Sixty pairs of feet in a cow way. None of that was any different this morning because Elsie was cool. She tried to set the one against the other anyway, standing there in the wet—the cool forehead against the tucked blanket—and it wouldn't come out either way she ran it. She let it go.

The horses were saddled under the yew. Cyndia had them ready, all four, going round the girths without looking up.

Sylvanas was standing by her mare's head with Brask.

He had a book under his arm. A thick one, bound in calf gone dark at the corners, with a little horn of ink on a cord round his neck and a pen stuck behind his ear. His face was grey with the night, soot on it and on his jack and on the backs of his big hands.

"Dickon caught the cart at the ford," he said, as Jaina came up. "Turned it round. The Hallams hadn't had theirs off it yet. The alehouse had had two sacks." He breathed out through his nose. "I've sent down. They'll bring it up." He jerked his head at the heap. "And the boards from the loft. Your ice was going thin at first light, so we had them up with a bar and carried them down flat between two of us, like a door, faces turned away. They went on the heap with the rest, ice and all."

So that was the end of the third part, whatever it did. It was the right thing, and she would have told him to do it, and some small low part of her minded anyway.

"Thank you, Captain," Jaina said.

Sylvanas put her hand into her coat and took out something folded. She unfolded it and held it out past Brask, to Jaina.

It was a square of sacking, cut neat with a knife from the side of a sack. Pale and clean. There was a mark on it in black, stencilled: a sheaf of wheat in a ring, and under the ring, in square letters, a word.

ANDORHAL.

"From the loft," Sylvanas said. "His sacks. I cut it before they went in the fire."

"Andorhal," Brask said, looking at the square of sacking as if it had bitten him. "That's the big granaries. Down the grain road, south and west, past the ford. Three days, with a cart. Everything in the south goes through Andorhal, one way or another. My barley goes through Andorhal."

The young man with the cart had come from the west road. Cyndia had said so last night. Jaina took the sacking in her left hand and held it, the clean pale weave of it smelling of nothing but sacking.

"Then that's where his cart came from," she said. And then, because it was true: "Or where he wanted us to think it came from."

Sylvanas took the sacking back. She folded it and put it away in her coat. "Either way," she said, "it's the road he drove."

Brask had got the book open against his chest. He took the pen from behind his ear and pulled the stopper out of the horn with his teeth.

"I'll want your names," he said round the stopper. He spat it into his hand. "All of you. If anyone comes asking what happened here, I want to tell it right."

He turned to a clean page. He wrote slowly, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth and the book braced on his forearm, the pen scratching across the paper, saying each word aloud as he made it, as children say their letters.

"The mage," he said. "Jaina." Scratch. "Proudmoore. Spell that for me."

"P, R, O, U, D," Jaina said. "Moore. M, O, O, R, E."

"Proud-moore." He wrote it. He looked at it a moment, then up at Sylvanas, the pen hanging over the page.

"Sylvanas Windrunner," Sylvanas said. She spelled it for him, flat and clear, one letter at a time, as she might have given him the bearing of a ford. Brask wrote it letter by letter after her and said it back carefully, getting the middle wrong and then right.

Then Cyndia, from the far side of her horse, without looking up from the girth. Then Lyriel, who spelled hers and then said, "And you can put that one of us ties a good knot, Captain, if there's room on the line," glancing at Cyndia, who didn't look round.

Brask looked at her. He didn't write that.

He blew on the page and shut the book on it, standing there holding it shut against his chest with both hands, as he had held the spear in the lane.

"Right," he said.

He went off down the lane toward the granary yard, where the heap had begun to smoke again.

"Mage," Sylvanas said. "Up."

Lyriel was at her stirrup. Jaina got up badly, as she always got up, with a foot in Lyriel's cupped hands and her skirts caught under her; Lyriel took her weight and set her straight in the saddle, then let go and went to her own horse. The mare shifted under her. Jaina sat there and found she was shaking, very slightly, all over, as she'd shaken after her first night watch on her father's deck, and she put her bandaged hand down flat on the mare's warm neck until it stopped.

They went down through the village in the same order they had come up it, Sylvanas in front and Cyndia on the left and Lyriel on the right and Jaina in the middle, and two empty places at the back. People were at their doors. The egg woman was setting out her trestle. Nobody called a price. They watched the horses go by, and the woman in the blue apron put her hand up, flat, and held it there a moment before she took it down again. They went past the bakehouse with its oven cold and its door shut, and the baker standing in the yard next door with a fork in his hands and flour still white to his eyebrows, watching the heap smoke. They went past the pound, and the cage. Jaina didn't look.

At the bottom of the village, past the brook, the road split. One way went back north, toward Millhaven. The other went off south and west between its hedges, broad and flat and rutted deep, two long grooves worn into the mud by every loaded cart that had ever come up it.

The staff shifted in the crook of her arm, all on its own, leaning toward the ruts.

Sylvanas turned her mare onto the grain road without a word. They fell in behind her, the ruts going on ahead of them into the wet morning, south and west, toward wherever the young man with the cart had come from.
