The taste came before the village did. It sat on the back of her tongue like an old copper she had been sucking, and in the crook of her elbow the crystal began to tick against its claws, light and quick, the way a moth ticks at a shutter.

Jaina swallowed. It came back.

Then the lane turned between two cottages and the village was all around them at once, loud the way a working place is loud before noon. A hammer rang on iron somewhere ahead, quick and slow and quick again. A woman called a price for eggs, and another woman called it lower, and the first one laughed at her. Geese hissed from a yard. A man was backing a cart through a gate and shouting it back, back, back, and the hub knocked the gatepost anyway, and he swore at the horse, which had done nothing. Bread smoke lay over all of it, low and warm and brown, the way it lies over every village in Lordaeron at this hour. Two boys came pelting under the mare's nose after a hoop. The mare flung her head up and set all four feet on the cobbles and blew at them, and Lyriel's hand came across from the right and took the cheek-strap without Lyriel so much as turning her head.

"Steady," Lyriel said, to the horse or to her.

Jaina had the reins looped over her wrapped left hand, which was all that hand was good for this morning. It throbbed inside the linen with every jolt. The right lay in her lap like something she had been asked to carry for someone else, and the staff rode in the crook of that arm with its heel against her boot. She had slept. Two nights on the ground in her cloak with a saddle under her head, and last night she had slept so hard she had woken with the print of the stirrup buckle on her cheek and a clear head for the first time since Dalaran. She had been almost cheerful, saddling up. She had made Lyriel laugh.

And here was the cold coming up under her breastbone, exactly where the staff had said it would be. Two days of road since Millhaven, and the dread arrived on time, like a coach.

Under the hammer and the geese and the eggs, the drag.

She shut her eyes to hear it. The currents ran under the cobbles the way they ran everywhere, a slow pulse she had known so long she only heard it when it went wrong, and here it had gone wrong. Each beat came in and then something came in after it, late, a weight hung on the tail of the pulse. The same drag she had felt through the floor of the cell at the Pass, and in the doe's blood, and at the Millhaven well.

It was quieter here. That was the strange part. Millhaven had been a knot pulled so tight it rang, every line in the fold hauling the same dead weight into the crossing and nothing alive anywhere to cover it. Here it was thin, and spread, and it moved. It came and went as the mare carried her past a doorway, a stall, a woman with a basket. Warm bodies all round her, dozens of them, and it was in them, moving with them, and the whole loud warm noise of the place lay over it like a blanket over a cough.

Quieter because it was still moving. Because it still had somewhere warm to go.

Oh, Light. That was a horrible way to put it, and it was right.

She opened her eyes. Was it here, then? Yes. Rooted? She had followed the staff for two days down this valley like a dog after a cart, and the staff was ticking in her elbow now, so, yes. Where, exactly? Everywhere a little. In which houses, which people, which part of which people? She did not know. What would she show anyone, if anyone asked? A taste, and a crystal that clicked, and her own face going green. Brilliant, Jaina. Antonidas's prize student, sure as stone and with nothing in her hands to prove it, and her hands not much use for anything else either.

The egg stall was on the left, a trestle under an awning with straw in the trays. The woman behind it was haggling with a broad-hipped woman in a blue apron, and the broad-hipped woman had a little girl against her skirts, five or six years old, in a knitted cap pulled down to her eyebrows. The child's cheeks were red as scrubbed brick, two hot patches high on the bones and the rest of her face pale round them. Her eyes were very bright. She was leaning her whole weight on her mother's hip, and every time she sagged her mother hitched her up again without looking down.

"Stand up, Mag, you're not a sack of turnips. Nearly done."

The child stood up. She coughed into her mother's apron, a small wet cough, twice, and her mother went on haggling over the eggs.

The crystal ticked as the mare went past them. It ticked, too, as the mare went past the next stall, where there was no child at all, only a man selling salt herring out of a barrel. The brine smell came up off it sharp and clean and went straight through her, so that for a moment she was nine years old on the quay at Boralus with the gulls going over and her father's hand on her head. She had not smelled the sea since the spring. She breathed it in, and under it the bread smoke, and under that the copper on her tongue.

She could not read the child from the saddle. Not from here, not through the noise, not with the staff loose in a dead arm. The flush might be any fever. A child might cough for any reason; children did nothing else between harvest and spring.

But once she had heard the first cough she heard them everywhere. A man in a doorway with his fist against his mouth. Someone in an upstairs room behind a half-open shutter, on and on, a dry hacking that nobody went to. Two women at the pump in the middle of the street, filling buckets, one of them turning her face aside to cough and then turning back to finish what she was saying. All of it under the hammer and the geese, so ordinary that nobody in the street turned to look.

*Everybody coughing at the well.*

The girl had said it into Neral's collar, in the dark of the inn at Millhaven. Missus Hale's baby first. Then Mister Pell. Then everybody coughing at the well. And then nobody, and tables laid for supper, and sixty sets of toes pressed into the clay of a cow way, all pointing uphill.

So this was the part before. This was Millhaven a fortnight ago, or a week, or however long it had been. Millhaven with smoke in its chimneys and a market in its street and a woman telling her daughter to stand up straight.

Jaina half turned in the saddle, toward the back of the line, to say it to Neral. There was nobody there. She turned back.

"Ranger-General," Cyndia said, on the left. Her voice was level and carried no farther than it needed to. "Shutters up on three houses, this side, since the bend. Middle of the day."

"I saw them," Windrunner said, from the front.

Jaina had not seen them. She looked now, over Cyndia's shoulder: a cottage with its shutters closed and barred, and its door shut, and a stool on the step with a cup on it, as if someone had left it there for someone who would not come out. She had been listening to the ground. She had heard every pulse under the cobbles since the bend and missed three shut houses in plain daylight.

Then the horses stopped.

Windrunner's mare stopped first, and the rest stopped behind it, bunching, iron scraping and sliding on the wet cobbles, Lyriel's mare swinging her quarters round until Lyriel sat her straight again. Across the lane, at the height of the horses' chests, lay the shaft of a spear.

It was a boar spear, with a broad leaf of a head and a crossbar under it, and the man who held it held it level, in both hands, across the whole width of the lane between a wall and a water butt. He was not pointing it at anyone. He was simply holding it there, the way you would hold a gate shut.

He was big, heavy through the shoulders, in a quilted jack too warm for the day and a leather cap. Fair stubble going grey. His hands on the ash shaft were brown and broad and cracked across the knuckles, a farmer's hands, with a split thumbnail on the right one gone black underneath. Two men stood behind him, one with a pitchfork and one with a hunting bow, unstrung. The one with the bow looked about sixteen.

"That's far enough," the big man said. "We've sick here. Nobody comes in armed, and nobody comes in I don't know."

Behind them the egg woman had stopped calling her price.

Jaina had it all ready. It was right there, in good order, the way she would have laid it out for Antonidas: *Captain, what you have here is not a fever. It is a contamination of the currents, necromantic in character, and it is already in your people, and if you do not let us through—* She had leaned forward in the saddle to say it. Her mouth was open.

She looked at the egg stall. The broad-hipped woman in the blue apron had turned round with the child against her skirts, and the herring man had stopped with his scoop half out of the barrel, and the two women at the pump had set their buckets down. Every face in the lane was turned toward the spear.

*Necromantic.* In this lane. In front of that child.

She shut her mouth.

Windrunner swung down from her mare.

She did it without hurry and without looking round, and she lifted the bow off her shoulder and held it out behind her, sideways, at arm's length. Cyndia leaned out of her saddle and took it. Then Windrunner walked forward to the spear, empty-handed, until the shaft lay across the front of her coat, and stopped there. She was taller than he was. She did not seem to be trying to be.

"You're right to stop us," she said.

She said it low. Jaina, a horse's length back, heard every word, but it would not have reached the egg stall, and the man had to lean a little to hear it, and he did lean.

"Then you'll turn round, my lady."

"Ranger-General."

His thumb moved on the ash. "Ranger-General."

"We've come from Millhaven."

The thumb stopped.

"Millhaven," he said.

"We came into it at dusk. The tables were laid. Bowls on them, and a spoon in every bowl, and a loaf." Windrunner's voice did not change at all. It went on at the same level, as if she were telling him how far it was to the next ford. "There was a sickbed made up with a basin and a cup of water beside it. A cradle in the end house with the blanket tucked in. No child in it. No one in any house. Living or dead."

The man said nothing. The boy with the bow had come up at his shoulder to listen, and the big man put out his elbow without looking and moved him back.

"They walked out of it," Windrunner said. "Up the cow way above the village, in the night. Sixty of them that we could count. We counted their prints."

"Walked."

"Walked." She let that sit. Then, in exactly the same voice: "There was a child left alive, shut in a cellar. She said the first thing she remembers is that everybody was coughing at the well."

She did not look round at the pump. She did not look at the egg stall, or the child in the knitted cap. She kept her eyes on his face and let him do that himself, and he did. Jaina watched him do it. His head turned to the pump, where the two women stood with their buckets, and then, slower, to the stall. The child coughed again, into the blue apron. He looked at that a long while.

When he turned back his face had gone a bad colour under the stubble.

"It's fever," he said. "It's the time of year for it. Tobin says it's fever."

"Then let my mage see your sick, and we'll know by dark whether I'm a liar."

His thumb moved on the wood. Up the shaft and back down. Up and back.

"Your people are watching you, Captain," Windrunner said, even lower. "Keep your spear. Walk with me. I'd like to see your village, and I'd like them to see me with you."

Up the shaft, and back.

Jaina found that she was holding her breath, and that her left hand had closed on the reins hard enough to make the raw fingers sing inside the linen. She made it open. What would she have done, if he said no? Argued. Explained. Explained louder, with diagrams, while the egg woman listened to every word and went home and told her sister. Light help her, she would have drawn him the currents in the air with a hand that didn't work.

The big man lifted the spear.

He brought it upright and grounded the butt on the cobbles beside his boot, and stood holding it like a staff, and let out a long breath through his nose.

"Willem Brask," he said. "I've the militia here, such as it is." He jerked his chin at the boy. "Tobin has them in the chapel. Dickon, take the mage to Tobin. Go on." Then, up at Jaina, as if he had only now seen her: "Miss."

"Thank you, Captain," Jaina said.

It came out higher than she meant it to.

Windrunner had already turned. "Lyriel. With the mage. Cyndia, with me." She took her bow back from Cyndia and slung it, and looked up at Jaina for the first time since she had dismounted. Her eyes were very clear and gave nothing back at all. "Mage. What you find, I want before dark."

"Yes, Ranger-General."

And that was that. Windrunner walked off down the lane at Brask's shoulder, the big man still carrying his spear upright, Cyndia a pace behind and to the left of them with her hand resting near her quiver. The egg woman called her price again, a little too loudly. The herring man put his scoop back in the barrel. Brask stopped at the pump and said something to the two women, and one of them laughed, short and shaky, and picked up her bucket.

Jaina sat on the mare in the middle of the lane, and her ears went hot.

She had filed Windrunner under maps. Under ground, and ambush angles, and ears sharp enough to hear a wolf at the back of a holly wood; a woman who could read a map backward and be right, and had been, and Jaina had stopped talking to her in small words after that, she had, she had made a point of it. But people? Under people she had filed her as nothing much. A woman who said *Mage. Up.* and expected the mare and the mage to obey her in the same tone.

Refile it, then.

How had she known how hard? She had not raised her voice once. She had given her bow away, which had cost her nothing whatever with three more bows behind her, and she had conceded the man his point before he had finished making it, and then she had told him about a cradle, and a spoon in a bowl, and a child coughing at the well, and not one word about currents. And she had let him look at his own street himself, and never pointed.

Jaina would have pointed. Jaina would have explained to him, at length, in a voice that carried, and the egg woman would have had the word *necromancer* by heart before dinner, and the spear would have been the least of their troubles.

Could it be learned? Antonidas had never taught it. Antonidas had never needed to; men lowered their spears for Antonidas because of the hat.

"Lady Proudmoore." Lyriel had come round to her stirrup on foot, her own mare's reins over her arm. "Will you get down, or shall I fetch a ladder?"

"I'm getting down."

She got down. She did it badly, as she did it every time, the staff sliding in her elbow and her right leg not quite clearing the cantle, and Lyriel took her weight at the waist without comment and set her on the cobbles and let go. The ground came up hard through her boot-soles. The copper taste was stronger down here.

The boy, Dickon, was waiting for them with his unstrung bow in his fist, looking at Lyriel's ears and trying not to.

"It's this way, miss," he said. "It's not far."

He took them up a side lane between two garden walls, and Lyriel walked with the two mares on her right hand and Jaina on her left, and the hammer ahead of them rang on its iron, quick and slow and quick.

The chapel stood at the top of the lane, a squat grey house of the Light with a bell-cote on the gable and no bell in it. Someone had carried the benches out and stacked them in the yard under the yew, upside down, their legs in the air like dead beetles. The door was shut. Dickon knocked on it with the end of his bow and then pushed it open without waiting, and the smell came out to meet them.

Vinegar first, sharp enough to sting. Then sweat, and tallow, and the slop bucket nobody had emptied since morning. And under all of it, faint and thick, a sweetness.

Pears gone brown.

*They had no pears,* the girl had said, in the dark at Millhaven, and Jaina had asked her, before or after, and the girl had said before. Before she was hot. Jaina had tried to write it down that night by the inn lantern, with the pen pushed through the linen between two fingers that would not close on it, and had blotted the page and given up.

Now she could smell it for herself, and she wished she could not.

"Light," Lyriel said under her breath, behind her. She tied the mares to the yard rail and came in.

It was dim inside and close and too warm. Every shutter was barred, and a brazier smoked in the middle of the floor where the benches should have been. Round it, packed so close their sides touched, stood every bed in the village that could be carried. Two proper cots. A box bed with its doors taken off. A child's truckle bed with an old man on it, his feet hanging off the end in grey stockings. A door laid flat across two trestles with a straw pallet on top. Pallets on the floor between them. The blankets were every colour there was, a red one, a striped one, a sheepskin, a good blue wool with a darned corner, each family sending its own bedding with its own sick. She could not have walked straight between the cots if she had wanted to. She went sideways, the staff held upright against her chest in the crook of her arm, and still her skirts brushed somebody's hand.

Someone was coughing. Several people were. Someone else was saying *Mam, Mam, Mam* in a flat voice, over and over, not loud.

A man came round the end of the box bed with a basin in his hands. He was small and wiry and bald as an egg, with his sleeves rolled to the elbow and a leather apron gone black down the front, and his hands and forearms were red to the wrists, scrubbed red, the skin split across every knuckle and in the webs between the fingers. Something shiny was smeared in the cracks. Goose grease, from the smell of it. He stopped when he saw them and looked at Lyriel's bow, and Lyriel's ears, and at Jaina last.

"Willem sent you?"

"Captain Brask, yes. I'm Jaina Proudmoore. I'm a mage, from Dalaran."

"Dalaran." He said it as if Dalaran were a long way off and somehow to blame. "Tobin. I do teeth and bones, mostly. Births." He looked down at her hands, at the grubby linen bound round them to the wrists. "What happened to those?"

"Frost."

"Want me to look at them?"

It was so unexpected that she nearly laughed. "No. Thank you. They'll keep." She looked past him, at the cots. "What have you been treating them for?"

"Fever." He set the basin down on the end of the door-bed. "What else would I treat? It comes on like fever. They burn like fever. Willow bark, vinegar cloths, keep them warm and keep the draught off. I bled old Garran yesterday and it did him no good at all, so I'll not do it again." He wiped his hands on the apron, which could not have made them cleaner. "Is it fever?"

"It's a fever," Jaina said. "There's something else in it too. I need to see who's been ill longest, and who came in last."

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he jerked his head toward the truckle bed. "Garran's longest. Since before market. Elsie came in this morning." He pointed across the room to the door laid on the trestles, where a girl of fifteen or so lay on the pallet under the striped blanket with her eyes open, watching them. "She was milking yesterday. Fainted in the byre."

Jaina went to Elsie first.

The girl turned her head on the pallet as Jaina came round to her. Her face was flushed and damp, her hair stuck down on her forehead in dark points, but her eyes were clear enough, and frightened. When Jaina bent over her the sweetness came up off her breath, so strong this close that Jaina had to swallow.

"I'm going to look at you," Jaina said. "With a light. It won't hurt you. You might feel warm."

"I'm warm now," Elsie said.

"Warmer, then. I'm sorry."

She could not do this the way she had done the doe. Her right hand would not have felt the girl if she had laid it on her, and the left had nothing left on its fingertips to feel with; she had left that on the stones of the well at Millhaven, and Windrunner had told her flatly that there would be no more hand-reading, and for once she had not argued. The staff, then. She turned to Lyriel.

"Will you hold this for me?"

Lyriel took it. She held it the way rangers held everything, as if she expected it to do something sudden, one hand under the crystal and one round the silver wire. "If it bites me, Lady Proudmoore, I'm letting go."

"It won't. Lower. The crystal against her, here, under the ribs. Gently. Through the blanket is fine."

Lyriel set it there. Jaina bent and laid her cheek against the ash where the wood was dark from her own hands, and shut her eyes, and listened.

The girl's pulse came up through the wood, fast and light. Under it, faint and very close, the drag.

She said the word for light, the plain one, the first one every apprentice in the Violet Citadel learned and the last one she would ever forget, and let it go down the staff the way water goes down a rope.

The crystal woke. A pale gold glow came out of it, and went into the girl through the blanket and her shift and her skin, and Jaina opened her eyes to watch. The skin of Elsie's belly lit from inside the way a hand lights when you cup it round a candle, pink and gold, with the dark shadows of the ribs standing out above. The light moved. It went with the pulse, outward and back, outward and back, up into the chest, where it beat easy and clean round the heart and filled the lungs like breath and came back.

Under the ribs it did not come back clean.

There was a place, just below the breastbone, about the size of a fist, where the light went in on the beat and came out after it, late, dragging, and when it came out it was the wrong colour. Brown, like a drop of ink let fall into a glass of water. It curled. Below it, along the gut, a thread of the same brown, thin and broken, following the coils down. And nowhere else. The heart was clean. The lungs were clean. The blood in her arms, when Jaina sent the light out along them, went pink to the fingertips and came back pink.

"Does it hurt her?" Lyriel said quietly. "Does it hurt you?"

"No. A little."

The staff was going cold under her cheek. She could feel it creeping up out of the wood into the bones of her face, and into her left hand, which had no business feeling anything, and which ached now as if she had plunged it in snow. She let the light go. The girl's skin went dark and ordinary, and Elsie let out a breath and stared up at her.

"What's in me?"

"I don't know yet." Jaina straightened, and her back cracked. "I'm going to find out. Lie still."

"Garran," she said to Tobin.

The old man on the truckle bed did not open his eyes when they came. His face had gone the colour of a tallow candle, grey-yellow, the skin stretched across the bones of the nose, and his breath went in and out of him with a wet catch at the top of it every time. His belly under the sheepskin was round and hard. The sweetness had gone off his breath. There was something else there now, heavier, that she did not want to name.

Lyriel set the crystal against him without being told. Jaina laid her cheek to the wood.

The light went in, and almost none of it came back clean.

It was everywhere. It came up out of the old man's belly in brown threads, branching, out under the ribs and up into the liver, dark and swollen with it, and into the lungs, where the light went in on each breath and came out late and dirty and broken up. Along the great vessels of the neck. Down both arms, thin as hair, almost to the fingers. It had spread out from the middle of him and gone everywhere, and every thread of it ran back, if she followed it, to the same place under the breastbone where the girl's fist of brown had been.

She had seen this before. She had seen it in a doe in a ditch below the Pass, with her bare hand flat on the hide, running through the meat and the blood and the liver like roots in a pot. She had not seen it in a man who was still breathing.

The same. Light help her, it was the same, and it was alive in him, and he was still alive round it.

She let the light go and stood up. The cold had got into her teeth.

"Who else came in in the last day or two?"

Tobin showed her. A boy of ten on a pallet by the brazier, the cooper's youngest, hot since yesterday. A woman in the box bed, his mother, hot since the day before. A young man in his shirt on the floor by the door with his boots still on, who said he was not sick at all and only tired and Tobin was an old woman, and whose breath smelled of brown pears. Lyriel moved the staff from one to the next and Jaina followed it, bending to the wood each time, and each time she sent the light down, and each time it came back the same.

Under the ribs. A fist of brown, or half a fist. A thread along the gut. Nothing in the lungs, nothing in the blood, nothing in the arms or the head.

The young man by the door had the smallest knot of all, no bigger than a walnut. He watched the light go in and out of his own stomach through his open shirt and said, "Well, that's not right," in a tone of mild complaint, as if someone had sold him a bad horse.

Jaina knelt there beside him with her cheek still against the staff. She did not move. She did not want to move, in case it went away.

The gut. Every one of them, at the start, in the gut and nowhere else. And the long cases went outward from there, out from the stomach into everything else, the way water spreads out from where you spill it.

So it went in by the mouth.

They were swallowing it.

"Well?" Tobin had come up behind her with the basin. "What is it?"

She got up off her knees. It took two tries; Lyriel put a hand under her elbow for the second one, and let go.

"It starts in the stomach," she said. She kept her voice down, for the young man's sake, and for Elsie's, who was watching them across the room. "In everyone who's newly ill, it's only there. In Garran it's everywhere now, and it spread out from there. They're taking it in. Swallowing it."

"Swallowing what?"

"I don't know."

"It's the water, then." Tobin set the basin down on the cooper's boy's pallet, hard enough to slop it. "I said so to Willem. Bad water. There's the three wells and the brook, and the brook comes down past the tannery—"

"It might be the water."

It might. The well at Millhaven had read thin, thinner than the doe, and thin water didn't put a fist of it under a dairymaid's ribs between one evening's milking and the next morning. Unless she drank a great deal of it. Everybody drank a great deal of water, Jaina; that was rather the point of water. Food, then. Whose food? Everybody's, everybody ate. A cup passed hand to hand along a bench. A spoon licked and handed on. Fingers in a mouth. A mother kissing a child goodnight. The whole cooper's household was down, Tobin had said, all five of them, and what did a household do together but eat from one pot and drink from one bucket and sleep in one bed and breathe each other's breath?

She couldn't strike out a single one of them. Not one. And every hour she stood here not knowing, every house in the village was sitting down to its dinner.

Tobin had gone back to Elsie. He knelt beside the door-bed and slid one red cracked hand under the back of her head and lifted it, and held a cup to her mouth with the other. She drank. Jaina watched the girl's throat move as she swallowed, once and again and again, and her own stomach turned over.

Water. Or the cup. Or the hand on the cup. Or nothing to do with any of it.

"Lyriel," she said. "Bring me that jug."

Lyriel brought it. It was a brown crock jug with a chip out of the lip and a wet ring under it where it had stood on the trestle, and it was nearly full.

"From the pump?"

"From the pump," Tobin said. "Same as every bucket in this room. Same as every bucket in the street."

"Set the crystal against it, please. Low down, under the water line."

Lyriel tipped the staff and set the crystal to the glaze as carefully as if the jug might go off, and Jaina bent and put her cheek to the ash. The wood had not warmed since the last time. She sent the light down.

Water did not glow the way a body glowed. The light went in and lay there, dull gold, and the only pulse in it was the slow pulse of the currents coming up through the floor and the clay of the jug. Each beat came in. Something came in after it, late.

Thin, though. A hair's weight on the tail of each beat, no more than that. She had felt worse in the ditch water below the Pass. She had felt the same at the Millhaven well, before she had put her hand to the stones and left half of it there.

She let it go. "There's some in the water."

Tobin let his breath out through his nose.

"A little," Jaina said. "Very little. Less than in Elsie, by a long way."

"So it's the water and other things besides."

"It might be."

It was in the water. It had been in the water at Millhaven, and the clay at Millhaven, and very likely it was in the clay under this floor; thin was everywhere south of the Pass by now, the way damp is everywhere in a cellar. Did thin put a fist of it under a dairymaid's ribs between one milking and the next? She didn't think so. Did she know so? No. She knew it was in a jug.

The door banged open behind her before she could decide what that was worth.

Two men came in sideways with a sheep hurdle between them, and a woman on the hurdle. She was big and fair and red-armed, in a brown wool dress with the hem soaked dark, and she was fighting. Her heels drummed on the wattles. One hand had hold of the hurdle's edge and the other was clawing at her own collar as if it choked her, and she was talking, fast and low and not to anyone in the room: the hens, somebody had to see to the hens, nobody ever saw to the hens but her.

"Dunmore's wife," Tobin said. "From the top farm. Put her down there, Hew, by the door-bed, mind the bucket—"

They put her down on the floor between Elsie's trestles and the brazier, and the bigger of the two men stood over her with his hands hanging. He had muck to the knees from the yard and a face like a slapped child's. Behind him, in the doorway, a little girl in a man's coat that came down to her ankles was holding a shawl against her mouth. She came in after them because nobody told her not to. She went into the corner where the benches should have been, by the font, and sat down on the flags with the shawl, and watched.

Jaina looked at the woman, and the cooper's wife coughing in the box bed, and old Garran with the wet catch at the top of every breath, and Elsie watching her across the room with her clear frightened eyes.

She could stand here and read jugs until dark. She could read the bread bin and the bucket and the ladle and the cat, and every one of them would come back thin, and every house in the village would go on sitting down to its dinner. Or she could do the one thing she was sure she knew how to do.

"Lyriel. The staff, please."

Lyriel gave it to her. Jaina got it into the crook of her right arm, where the dead hand could not drop it because the dead hand was not being asked to hold anything, and pinned it against her ribs, and knelt down by the hurdle on the cracked flags. She set the heel of the staff on the stone beside the woman's shoulder and leaned the crystal out over her.

It was the first holding ward any apprentice learned in Dalaran, and it was not a healing at all. They taught it in the alchemy rooms in the first week, for the glass. You put it round a flask that had to go into the furnace so the flask would not crack in the heat; it did nothing to whatever was boiling inside, it only kept the glass in one piece while it boiled. She had put it round a hundred flasks. She had never put it round a woman.

She said it. The light that came down out of the crystal was blue, not gold, a cold clean blue like the shadow side of snow, and it came down over the woman in a fine mesh and settled on her and sank in. Through it, under the ribs and spreading, the brown in her showed up murky and yellow, the colour of a fly in old honey.

The drag hit the ward on the next beat.

Jaina felt it in her arm. It came up the staff as a pull, a little late weight hung on the tail of the woman's pulse, and the ward took it and held, and the pull went back down into the woman and did not get any further than it already was. Next beat, the same. And the next. Each one, a tug on a line, and each one, the line holding. The cold came up out of the ash into the crook of her elbow and spread across her chest under the bodice like a wet shirt.

The woman's heels stopped drumming.

Her hand let go of the hurdle. Her other hand slid down off her collar and lay open on the brown wool, palm up, and her head rolled a little to the side, toward the brazier, and she was still.

Quite still.

Jaina's own breath stopped in her. *No. No, I didn't—*

The woman's chest went up. Slow. It hung there a moment and came down, slow, and went up again, and the lines round her mouth let go, and she was asleep.

In the corner, the little girl in the man's coat took the shawl away from her face.

Jaina breathed. She stayed where she was on her knees with the staff against her ribs until she was sure the ward would stand without her, and then she got up, badly, and did the cooper's wife.

Then the cooper's boy. Then Garran, whose ward took twice the holding and gave her a cold so deep she felt it in the roots of her teeth. Then Elsie, who said, "It's like a wet sheet," and then, "It's nice," and closed her eyes. The wards stood on their own once they were set, for a while, the way a lamp burns on its own for a while, and then they thinned; and she went round the room, from cot to box bed to hurdle to door-bed and round again, trimming them before they guttered, like a girl in a house with too many lamps and one taper.

Most of them were early. That was the thing she kept coming back to, between wards, with the cold in her teeth. The cooper's boy, Elsie, the young man by the door with his walnut, the woman from the top farm: a fist or less in all of them, all in the gut, nothing in the blood. Not Garran, but most. Most of them were early enough to be worth something.

The bandage on Garran's arm had come loose.

She saw it on her second round. Tobin had bled him yesterday at the inside of the elbow, and the pad had slid, and the cut was weeping thin and pink into the sheepskin. Tobin was across the room with Hew Dunmore, who wanted to know if his wife was dead and would not believe that she wasn't. Lyriel was at the brazier with a bucket. Jaina had the staff pinned in her right arm, the ward half-trimmed, the pull coming up the ash in its slow late beats, and she could not let go of it to call anybody.

So she picked up the strip of linen with her left hand.

She got the pad back over the cut. That was easy. She got the strip round his arm once, and round again, the left hand doing it all, fingers that would only curl halfway, the rag on them grey now from the morning. Then she had to tie it, and she had one hand to tie it with.

She tried to tuck the end under. It slid out. She tried to make a half hitch round her own fingers and pull them out of it, and the linen came with them. She tried again. Her fingertips were raw under the wrapping and every pass of the strip across them rasped. Garran breathed his wet breath. The ward thinned under the crystal and she fed it, and the cold came up, and the strip of linen hung off his elbow like a wet ribbon off a maypole.

She had tied a bowline behind her back at eight years old, with her father's bosun counting, for a bet. She knew a sheet bend and a clove hitch and a rolling hitch and the knot the Kul Tiran gunners used on the breeching ropes, and she could not tie a rag round an old man's arm.

"Here."

Lyriel was crouched on the other side of the truckle bed. She had come without a sound, as they did, and she reached across Garran and put her hands over Jaina's on the linen. They were quick and dry and warm, and they moved Jaina's hand for her as if it were a hand they had borrowed.

"Loop over your thumb. Like that." The linen went round. "Turn your wrist under." Jaina's wrist turned under, Lyriel's fingers on the back of it. "Tail through." The end went through the loop. "Now take it in your teeth and set it."

Then she let go and sat back on her heels and said nothing.

Jaina looked at the knot she had not tied. Then she pulled it out.

Lyriel's eyebrows went up.

"Again," Jaina said.

Loop over the thumb. That went. Wrist under; and the loop slid off the end of her thumb as she turned, because the thumb would not bend enough to hold it, and she said a word she had learned from the gunners, and Lyriel's mouth twitched. Loop over the thumb again, further down this time, against the root of it. Wrist under. Tail through, which needed the tips of two fingers she barely had, and took three tries, and then it was through.

"Now set it," Lyriel said. "Go on, Jaina. Bite it. It won't bite you back."

She bent and took the tail of the linen in her teeth. It tasted of vinegar and old blood and the goose grease off Tobin's hands. She pulled her head back, and her hand back, against each other, and the knot closed down on itself flat and small on the inside of Garran's arm, and held.

She looked at it.

It was a bandage knot. She had read the inside of this man's liver by spell light an hour ago, and here she was, kneeling on a stone floor with her chin practically on his elbow, wanting that knot more than she had wanted anything since Dalaran. That was absurd. It was a small useful thing, and she was ridiculous, and she wanted it anyway, and she was going to tie it again on the next one, on her own, if it took her all afternoon.

And Lyriel had said *Jaina*. Just like that, in the middle of telling her to bite something.

"Where do the Farstriders learn that?" she said. "Or is it only you?"

"All of us. In the first year. You do it with the left until you can, and then they make you do it with the right." Lyriel was already standing up, the bucket in her other hand. "We learn it so we can patch our own mistakes."

She went back to the brazier.

Jaina knelt there with the staff against her ribs. Was that a joke? It sounded like one of Lyriel's, the plain thing said flat, and then she'd look round to see if it had landed. She hadn't looked round. Their own mistakes. A ranger who had put her own hand somewhere it shouldn't go, and had to tie it up herself, one-handed, with her teeth, because nobody else was going to stop and do it for her.

Her left hand throbbed in its linen. She didn't look at it.

She tied the next dressing herself. It was on the cooper's boy, who had picked a scab on his knee down to the blood while he lay there with nothing else to do, and it took her two tries and the knot came out lopsided, but she set it with her teeth and it held, and the boy said, "Did you just *bite* my bandage?" in a voice of deep respect.

Then the woman from the top farm's ward was thinning, and she went and fed it.

They held. All of them, all the way round. She went round again and they were still holding, and nobody in the room was any better.

That was the part she could not get past. The wards held the way the glass held: Garran was exactly as sick as he had been when she knelt down, and so was Elsie, and so was everyone else. They were only slower about it. And whatever had put a fist in Elsie overnight was still out there in the village, in somebody's kitchen, and these people were still taking it in, or had been until this morning, and so was every house in the street. She was bailing with the plug out. She could go round this room with her bucket until she dropped, and the sea would still be coming in.

What were they swallowing?

She could not ask the light. The light only told her where it was, not how it had got there.

She would have to ask them.

"Tobin," she said. "Have you got a slate?"

He had. It hung on a nail inside the door, a cracked one in a wooden frame, with a row of names down the side and strokes after them that he said were teeth, and who owed him for them. He wiped the teeth off with his sleeve without a pang and found the slate pencil in his apron.

"I can't hold it," Jaina said, and showed him her hands. "You'll have to write. Down the side, everyone in this room. Then how long they've been ill. Then how much of it I found in them."

"How much?"

"Small. Half a fist. A fist. Everywhere." She heard herself and nearly laughed again. Antonidas would have had a fit. "Those will do."

He wrote in big square capitals, slowly, with the tip of his tongue showing. GARRAN. COOPER, WIFE. COOPER, BOY. ELSIE. COL, which was the young man by the door, who turned out to be the smith's striker and lodged over the forge. W. DUNMORE. Down the side, and across, and she stood at his elbow with the staff in the crook of her arm and read it upside down, the way she read Antonidas's letters on his desk when he was pretending she couldn't.

"Now the water," she said. "Which well does each of them drink from?"

Tobin did not need to ask anybody. He knew which house drew from which well the way he knew whose teeth were whose. The cooper's household, Cooper's Lane well. Garran, the same; he lived three doors down. Col at the forge, the same. The top farm had its own well in the yard. Elsie, at Ashby's, from the brook below the farm, because the Ashbys had always been too mean to dig.

Jaina looked at what he had written.

Cooper's Lane well. Garran, everywhere. The cooper's wife and the boy, a fist each and more. Col, a walnut. Three houses on one well, and it ran from the worst in the room to the best.

So it wasn't the water. No, it might still be the water; the jug had read thin, and thin was thin, and she would not pretend she hadn't felt it. But the water didn't sort them. A whole lane drew one bucket and it came out of them as a dying man and a boy who'd picked his scab and a striker who thought Tobin was an old woman. If it was the water, it was the water for everybody. It didn't explain Garran.

"Who's been nursing Garran?" she said.

"Nobody, till I brought him up here. His wife's been gone since the spring. He's the cottage on his own."

Contact, then. The cooper's house was down to the last child; that was one pot and one bucket and one bed and five people breathing each other's breath, exactly as she'd thought. And Garran was the first in the village and the worst in the room, and he had been alone in his cottage with nobody to catch it off.

Contact got the cooper and lost Garran. The well got the lane and lost the lane. She had two answers that fitted half the room each, and she wanted very badly for one of them to fit the whole of it, and that was exactly when you were meant to stop wanting.

*I'm ruling out the comfortable answers first, Master.* Very good. And then what?

A girl came to the chapel door with a basket on her hip.

She was fifteen or sixteen, round-faced, out of breath from the lane, and her sleeves were rolled to the elbow and white to the elbow too. She set the basket down on the step and leaned on the doorframe a moment, and said, "Mam says how are they, and is there anything else," and Tobin said, "Bless her, tell her the same as yesterday, Ellen," and the girl looked at the lit staff in Jaina's arm and the elf at the brazier and went away down the lane fast.

Tobin brought the basket in. There were three round loaves in it under a cloth, still warm; Jaina smelled them across the room through the vinegar. He took one out and tore it in half with his red cracked hands and began to break the soft middle into a bowl, small, smaller, and then he poured milk over it from a can, and pressed it down with a spoon, and carried it to the truckle bed.

"What are you giving him?"

"His sops." Tobin knelt and got his arm under Garran's head. "He's no teeth to speak of. I had the last two out of him at Midsummer. He lives on these, morning and night, since his wife went. Ellen sends the loaf up, or did, to the cottage, and he soaks it."

Jaina watched the spoon go to the old man's mouth. He didn't open his eyes. He took it the way a baby takes it, his lips working, and swallowed.

"Wait," she said. "Wait. Write that. What they eat."

Tobin looked round at her with the spoon in his hand.

"What they eat," Jaina said. "All of them. What they've eaten this week. Garran, bread in milk, twice a day. Write it."

She went round the room again, and this time she asked them.

The cooper's wife, from the box bed, in a voice like a door hinge: two loaves a day from Ellen's mother, there were five of them and who had the time to bake, with the cooper behind on his orders. And what else? Bread and dripping, bread and pottage, bread and whatever there was. And the boy? The boy ate like a wolf. Elsie, from the door-bed: porridge at Ashby's in the morning, before the milking, and her bread and cheese in the field at noon, which the mistress bought for the hands from the village because it was cheaper than feeding the oven. Hew Dunmore, at his wife's side on the floor, without lifting his eyes from her: they'd had the wet in their barley at harvest, the whole field, and Wenna had been buying flour from the store since, and baking her own, and she ate the heel of every loaf standing up because she said the heel was the best of it.

And Col, by the door, with his shirt still open over his walnut.

"Me? I've only been down two days." He'd been up on the hill pasture all summer with the smith's two cows and everybody else's, and come down with the cattle. "Oatcake, mostly. My mam's. She sends me up with a sack of her own meal and I do it on a stone by the fire. And whatever I can snare."

"And since you came down?"

"Bread and dripping." He shrugged. "Last night and the night before. There's no griddle at the forge."

Jaina stood still.

The staff ticked in the crook of her arm, and the brazier spat, and somebody coughed behind her, and she stood very still with all of it at the edges and looked at the slate in Tobin's hand.

Garran. Bread in milk, morning and night, for weeks. Everywhere.

The cooper's house. Two loaves a day and bread with everything. A fist and more.

Wenna Dunmore. Store flour since harvest, and the heel of every loaf. A fist, and the worst of the new ones.

Elsie. Porridge in the morning, bread at noon. A fist.

Col. Oatcake all summer, his mother's own meal. Two nights of bread and dripping. A walnut.

Oh.

Oh, there it was.

It went through her like the first warm swallow of wine on a cold night, from the throat right down. She had it. She'd had it out of the bodies the right way round, the gut first, then who, then how much, then what, and she hadn't jumped once; she had put the well and the touching up against the room and let the room knock them down. Antonidas could have stood at her shoulder and watched every step and found nothing to—

In a room full of fevers. With Garran swallowing his sops. Lovely, Jaina. Shall we have a prize-giving?

She made herself look at the slate again.

Bread. So who bakes? Ellen's mother. Who mills? Who sells the flour to the store, and the store to the—

No. Slower. She was doing it again, running ahead of herself with the answer in her teeth, the way she'd nearly run at the storehouse in Millhaven. She'd refused the storehouse there. A store is where things end up, she'd told Windrunner, and it had been the right thing to say, and it was right here too. So: the oat-eaters. Rule out the comfortable answer before you go and build a house on it.

Oats were grain. Col's mother's oats were grain the same as Ellen's mother's flour, and Col had been eating them every day all summer, and Col had a walnut, and the walnut had come in two nights of somebody else's bread.

"Who else in the village eats their own oats?" she said. "No bread from the bakehouse. No flour from the store."

Tobin thought. "Mother Haw," he said, "at the end of Cooper's Lane. Won't have shop bread in the house, says it's chalk. Does oatcake on the griddle for herself and the grandson."

"Is she sick?"

"Her? She stopped me this morning to tell me my apron was a disgrace."

Cooper's Lane well. Her own oats. Not sick.

Jaina let out a breath she had not known she was keeping.

Not grain, then. One flour. One stock, whatever was in the bakehouse loaves and the store sacks, and the harder you ate it, and the longer, the worse you were. Garran, morning and night for weeks, and it had gone from his gut into everything. Col, two nights, a walnut. It wasn't a bad cup or a bad swallow, once, at the wrong moment. It was every meal. It went in with every mouthful and stayed, and the next meal laid more on top of it, like silt in a harbour.

Tobin was still kneeling by the truckle bed with the bowl in his hand. There was milk on Garran's chin.

"Don't give him any more of that," Jaina said.

"He's had nothing else in a week."

"I know. Don't. Not any of them. Not the bread, not anything baked with the store's flour. If somebody has oats they grew themselves, give them that."

He looked at the bowl. Then at the basket of loaves on the end of the door-bed, with the cloth turned back. "Ellen's a good girl," he said.

"I know she is." She didn't know what else to say about Ellen. "Please."

Tobin got up, slowly, his knees cracking, and carried the bowl to the slop bucket and tipped it in.

Jaina went to the door. She needed air that didn't smell of pears, and she needed to stand somewhere that wasn't next to Garran.

The chapel stood at the top of the lane and the whole village lay below it, roofs and gardens and smoke, going down toward the brook. She took hold of the doorframe with her left hand to steady herself. The wood was gritty under the linen. When she took the hand away there was a pale smear across the grey wrapping, and a pale print on the frame beside it where Ellen had leaned to get her breath. Flour. It was in the cracks of the wood and the grain of the linen and it would be on Ellen's sleeves all the way down the lane.

Down there, three roofs and two gardens below, a chimney stood up out of a low stone building with a long brick oven built against its end, and the smoke came out of it brown and warm and lay along the lane the way it had lain over the whole village this morning when she rode in under it. Next to the bakehouse stood a bigger building, long and stone and windowless, with a hoist beam over a door set high in the wall at the height of a cart bed. A woman came out of the bakehouse with a loaf under each arm and went off up the street.

Jaina held the doorframe and watched her go.

The bakehouse was where it ended up. A bakehouse was a store with an oven in it. The flour had come into it from that long stone building next door, and the long stone building had got it from somewhere, from somebody, on some day, and that was the half that would tell her whether this was one bad harvest gone wrong in a damp barn or something else. And it wasn't in the chapel. It wasn't on Tobin's slate. It was down there in the lanes, in who sold what to whom and when it had started and what it had cost, and Sylvanas was out there with it, and Jaina was up here holding a doorframe.

She could go down. It was not far; three roofs and two gardens. She could take Lyriel, who would come whether she was asked or not, and walk into that long stone building and put the staff to the sacks and—

And what? Read them thin, or thick, and stand there with a crystal against a sack of flour in front of whoever owned it, frightening him half to death, while up here the wards went out one by one.

Behind her, Wenna Dunmore said something about the hens in her sleep.

Jaina let go of the doorframe and went back in.

She did the round. Garran's ward was the thinnest and she fed it first, kneeling on the flags with the staff against her ribs while the cold came up the ash and the old man breathed his wet breath and the milk dried on his chin. Then the cooper's wife. Then the boy, who was asleep now with his lopsided bandage and his mouth open. Then Wenna, then Col, who didn't need it and got it anyway because he was there. Then Elsie, last, on the door-bed under the window.

There was only the one window in the chapel that wasn't shuttered. Someone had wiped one pane clear of soot at about the height of a tall man's eye. Jaina knelt by Elsie with the blue light coming down out of the crystal over the striped blanket, and she looked through the pane.

Sylvanas was down at the long stone building.

The glass bent her. She went thin and tall as she crossed one pane, and short in the next, and broke apart at the leading and came together again, but there was no mistaking her: the bow on her back, the green cloak, the bare head. She was asking about the flour.

She had come into the village before noon with nothing in her hands but Brask's spear across her coat, and she had not heard one word from this room since, and she had gone straight to the long stone building anyway, by some road of her own.

Jaina had been in here all afternoon with the bodies. Sylvanas had been out there with the money.

Herself. Obviously. *Refile it,* she'd said, in the lane, before noon, and then she had come in here and filed her straight back under maps.

She stayed where she was. She went round again.

The light was going by the time they came up the lane.

"Mage," she said.

Jaina had it ready. She had had it ready since the door. It was in small words, all laid out, the way she would give it to Brask: *It's the bread. Stop them eating the bread.* It was true, and it would do, and Sylvanas would nod once and go and do it.

She looked at the grey glove, and didn't say it.

"It starts in the gut," she said instead. "In everybody who's newly ill it's only there, under the ribs, and in the old man it's in everything now and you can follow it back to the same place. So they're swallowing it. I thought it was the water at first, and there's some in the water, I read a jug of it, but very little, and it doesn't sort them: three houses on one well, and it runs from the worst in the room to the least. Contact doesn't sort them either. The worst case in the village lives alone. What sorts them is bread." She heard herself going faster and let herself. The farmer's wife has been baking with store flour since harvest. The striker by the door ate his mother's oats all summer up on the hill, and he came down two days ago and ate bread, and he has the least of anybody. There's a woman at the end of the lane who eats her own oats and nothing from the store, and she drinks from the same well as the old man, and she's well. So it isn't grain. Oats are grain. It's one flour, and the more of it you eat, and the longer, the worse it goes. It builds, meal on meal." She stopped. "That's the body. I can't get further than that from in here."

Then she waited.

"How sure?"

"Sure enough that I've stopped Tobin feeding it to them."

Sylvanas nodded, once. "Cyndia."

So do most of the houses on this side of the brook, and the top farm since the harvest." She paused. "The keeper is pleased with the price. He said so twice."

Three months. Ellen's loaf had been going up the lane to Garran's cottage every morning for longer than that. Three months of it, then, torn up small and soaked in milk.

"What does he look like?" she said. "The new man."

"I asked," Sylvanas said. "Young. Civil. Bought the keeper a jug at the alehouse the first time he came, and paid in good coin, and asked after his wife's leg. The keeper liked him." "He wouldn't."

"No."

*Cheaper than Mister Hollis.* The girl had said it into Neral's collar in the dark at Millhaven, about a man who had called at her house and caused a quarrel between her mother and her father. And the carter on the bridge had asked them, so friendly, if they'd passed a young fellow with a grain cart. A new man. Nobody at Millhaven had been able to say anything about him either, except that he was new and he was cheaper.

Sylvanas had stood on that bridge. She had stood half in the doorway at the inn while the girl talked. Jaina looked at her, and Sylvanas looked back, and neither of them said it.

A new man, three months ago, with cheap flour. Cheap, so the keeper bought it, so the baker bought it, so the cooper's wife with five mouths and no time bought two loaves a day of it, and the top farm with its barley rotted in the field. Cheap was how you got into every house. Cheap was how you got into the old man who lived on sops.

"Somebody is selling it to them," Jaina said. Her voice came out very quiet. "Somebody is selling it to them on—"

She couldn't get the rest of it out at once.

"On purpose," Sylvanas said.

Neither of them said anything after that.

The brazier ticked. Garran's breath caught at the top, and caught, and caught. In the corner the little girl in the man's coat had fallen asleep against the wall, her shawl in her fist. Behind Sylvanas's shoulder Cyndia stood without moving at all, and across the room Lyriel had put the bucket down. Jaina was holding the staff so hard against her ribs that the silver wire had printed itself through her bodice.

Sylvanas's hand had gone to the string of her bow. It rested there. She was looking past Jaina, over the cots, at the shuttered wall, as if she could see through it and down the lane to the long stone building with the hoist beam.

"How much of his flour is left?" she said.

"Most of the last load," Cyndia said. "It's in the loft. The keeper showed me the sacks."

"Then nobody takes another sack out of there until we've seen it." Sylvanas looked at Jaina. "Can you leave them?"

Could she? Jaina looked round the room, at the blue mesh over every bed, already beginning to thin over Garran's. "The wards will stand until full dark," she said. "Perhaps a little after. Then I have to be here."

"Then we're quick." Sylvanas was already turning to the door. "Lyriel. With the mage. Cyndia, with me."

Jaina went round the room once more, fast, and fed every ward as full as she could make it take, until the cold had climbed past her elbow and her teeth ached from it and the blue stood up thick over all of them. Tobin watched her do it with the slate held against his apron.

"No bread," she told him at the door.

"No bread," Tobin said. "I heard you the first time."

Lyriel put Jaina's cloak round her shoulders without asking and fastened it at the throat, quick, because Jaina's left hand would not have managed the clasp. Then they went out into the lane after the other two. The sky over the roofs had gone the colour of a bruise. The air was cold and smelled of chimney smoke, and below them the bakehouse smoke still lay along the lane, low and brown and warm, the way it had lain over everything since noon. Sylvanas was already two gardens down the hill and walking fast toward the granary, and Jaina gathered her skirts in her bad hand and went down after her.

The granary had no windows. She had seen that from the chapel door, and it was worse close to: a long blind wall of fieldstone going up into the dusk, the hoist beam sticking out over the high door like the arm of a gallows, and the high door shut. The big doors at the yard end were shut too, with a bar dropped across them into iron hasps. No lock on the bar. Who would steal a granary?

Sylvanas stopped at the corner of the bakehouse with her bare hand up, and they stopped behind her.

The bakehouse was dark. Somebody had raked the oven out for the night, and heat still came off the brick end of it as Jaina came up, a soft dry warmth on the side of her face that smelled of scorched crust. Her stomach turned over at it. She'd eaten nothing since the saddle at noon. She would not, she found, be eating bread for a while.

Sylvanas was looking up.

Jaina looked up too, and at first there was nothing, only the dark wall and the beam and the sky gone to bruise behind them. Then she had it: a line of yellow under the high door, thin as a thread, where the door didn't quite meet its sill. It went out. It came back. Somebody was walking up and down between a lamp and that door.

"The keeper?" Lyriel said, so low it was hardly a sound.

"The keeper went home to his supper," Cyndia said. "I watched him drop the bar."

The bar was on the outside. Jaina looked at it, and at the thread of light, and the skin went tight across the back of her neck. Whoever was in there had not come in by the big doors, and had not wanted a light where the street could see it.

Sylvanas touched Cyndia's arm and pointed, without a word, at a low door in the side wall half hidden behind a water butt. Cyndia went to it along the wall with her bow in her left hand and an arrow lying across the grip, and laid her palm on the planks, and the door went inward a finger's width on leather hinges and stopped. Not barred. Not even latched.

Then Sylvanas turned round and looked at Jaina, and pointed at the ground beside the water butt. *There. Stay.*

Jaina shook her head. She lifted the staff an inch in the crook of her arm, which was all the argument she had room for; *you won't know what you're looking at, and I will.* She had no idea what her face was doing. Sylvanas looked at her for as long as it took to breathe in and out once, and her mouth went thin, and she pointed again, at the ground behind her own heels.

*There, then.*

They went in.

Dark, and the smell of it hit her in the throat: grain dust and mouse and old sacking and, under all of it, faint, a sweetness she had been breathing all afternoon. The staff woke in her arm before her eyes had made out anything at all. It didn't tick here. It ground. The late drag that had come and gone in the lanes all day, thin and spread, came up through the ash in one long pull, so heavy and so slow that her back teeth ached with it, and she had to stop with her shoulder against the wall and let it go through her.

Millhaven. The storehouse by the bridge, the loudest place in that whole dead village, and she had stood in the door of it and told Sylvanas Windrunner that a store is where things end up.

Well. Here was where it ended up.

Lyriel's hand found the small of her back and stayed there. Jaina pushed off the wall.

Shapes came up out of the dark. Sacks, stacked on low trestles to keep them off the floor, row on row of them going back into black. A cart-bed's worth of empty sacking in a heap. A stair of bare planks going up the inner wall to the loft, with no rail on it, and at the top of the stair the light: not the thread now but a soft yellow wash on the rafters, and a shadow moving across it. And a sound. A small, steady, careful sound, like somebody prising a lid off a box.

Sylvanas went up the stair first, an arrow on the string and the bow held low, and the planks didn't make a sound under her. Cyndia went after her. Jaina went third, because Lyriel's hand at her back pushed her third, and the planks talked under her boots on every step, every single one, and her skirts dragged on the treads behind her, and she thought, very clearly, *they should have left me by the water butt.*

Then her head came up over the edge of the loft.

There was a lamp on the boards. There were sacks stacked to the roof-tree behind it, new ones, the sacking pale and clean, and one of them open at the mouth and slumped over with the flour run out of it in a white tongue. There was a young man on his knees between the lamp and the sacks, with a floorboard lifted up beside him and his arm in the hole under it to the elbow, and he was taking out a jar.

He was very young. Younger than Dickon, she thought for a moment, and then no, not younger, only thinner; a long white neck and big red hands and hair cut close to the skull, flour in the creases of his knuckles and down the front of his smock. The jar was clay, round-bellied, the size of a baby's head, with a wad of waxed cloth tied over the mouth. He had it in both hands and he was lifting it out of the hole very carefully, the way you lift a full bowl.

He looked up.

He looked at the arrow first. Then at Cyndia's arrow, beside it. Then past them at Jaina's head, coming up out of the stairwell like a turnip, and at the blue light of the staff. His face did nothing at all for a moment. It was only a boy's face, with a fluff of fair hair along the lip.

"Put it down," Sylvanas said.

He did. He put the jar down on the boards beside the hole, gently, both hands, and Jaina had half a heartbeat to think *good, he's frightened, he'll talk*—

And then his hand went past the jar for the lamp.

His knee caught the jar as he lunged. It went over and rolled, a hollow clay sound across the boards, and fetched up against the edge of the lifted plank and broke there, not loudly, a dull wet crack like an egg in a fist. He had the lamp by its bail. He was up on one knee with his arm going back to throw it, the flame streaming sideways in the glass, and behind him and all around him and right up under the rafters the air was moving: the dust off the sacks and the spilled flour, hanging, turning, lit gold by the lamp all through the loft like midges over a pond.

A mill. There had been a mill on the quay at Boralus that went up the winter she was six. She had been in bed. She remembered the windows of her room coming in.

She never saw the arrow go. She heard it, a hard flat smack like a shutter slammed in a wind, and the boy's arm stopped in the air.

The arrow had gone through the loose sleeve of his smock above the wrist and on into the post behind him, and nailed the sleeve there. His arm hung from it. The lamp hung from his fist on its bail and swung, and oil slopped out of the spout and ran down the outside of the glass in a thin blue lick of fire and dripped, burning, onto the boards.

Through the sleeve. She had watched Sylvanas put an arrow through a running doe's heart at the holly bend. This one had gone through cloth.

Sylvanas was already across the loft. Jaina hadn't seen her cross it either. She took the lamp out of the boy's fist by the bail, quite gently, as if he'd been holding it for her, and wound the wick down to a bead with her gloved fingers, and set it behind her on a sack, out of his reach. Then she put her boot on the burning oil and ground it out.

The boy screamed. He tore at the pinned sleeve with his free hand and kicked out at the boards, and the smock ripped, a long rending sound, the arrow-shaft bending, the post creaking; he was coming loose, he was going to come loose and get up and do something else, and nobody was near enough but Sylvanas, and Sylvanas had both hands full of lamp—

Jaina set the heel of the staff on the boards and pushed.

She did it the way she would have done it to a bucket of water. There wasn't any other way she knew. The cold went out of her and down the ash and across the planks in a white feathering, fast, crackling, and went up over his boots and his shins and his knees, and locked him there, kneeling, to the floor and to the foot of the post. His free hand had hold of his own sleeve. The frost went over that too, up his wrist, and stiffened the cloth and his fingers into the cloth, and stopped.

He stopped. He made a high thin sound through his nose.

She had done this once before, on the road at the holly bend, to a wolf's hind legs. Never to a person. A person made a different sound.

The cold came back up the staff the way it always came back. It came into her left hand and the linen on it went hard as card in an instant, and the fingers inside it, which had been nothing much all afternoon, went to nothing at all; it ran up past the wrist and the elbow and stopped somewhere in the shoulder, a bar of ice laid along the bone. She heard herself breathing through her teeth. She kept the staff where it was.

For a moment nobody moved.

The boy knelt in the frost with his arm nailed up behind him and his face turned away from them all. Sylvanas stood over him with the lamp at her back. Cyndia had come up off the stair and stood at the edge of the loft with an arrow still on her string, and Lyriel was beside Jaina, close. They had him. Alive, frightened, and nobody had told him yet what to say. They had the hole under the floor and the open sack and the jar.

The jar.

The smell came up off the boards and hit her all at once, and her eyes ran with it. Pears gone brown. It was Garran's breath and Wenna's breath and the whole of the chapel, but as if somebody had taken every sickroom in the village and boiled them down together to a spoonful, and the spoonful was here, on the floor, at her feet. Under the sweetness there was something else, thin and sharp and wrong at the back of the nose, like the smell of a struck flint.

It was dark in the lamplight, darker than oil. It had come out of the broken jar in a puddle no bigger than a dinner plate and it was not staying a puddle. It ran. It went along the grain of the boards in thin dark fingers, toward the crack between two planks, toward the hole under the lifted board, toward the open sack with its tongue of flour; toward Sylvanas's boots.

Underneath this floor were the sacks on their trestles. Rows and rows of them.

"Back," Jaina said. "Get back from it. Don't breathe it. Ranger-General, your feet—"

Sylvanas stepped back without looking down.

There wasn't time to do it properly. The wards over the cots had taken her the whole of the afternoon to set, slow nets laid down thread by thread so they would hold their shape without her; there was no hour here. There was the next crack in the floor. She lifted the staff off the boy's frost, and felt the hold stand without her, just, and brought the heel down again on the edge of the spill, and threw it.

It came down like a bowl dropped over a candle. A shell of blue, hard, pressed flat to the boards, and the edges of it bit into the wood with a hiss like a hot iron laid on wet cloth. The dark fingers ran to the edge of it and stopped. She watched one of them climb a hair's breadth up the inside of the curve and slide back. The fumes came up off the puddle and lay under the shell like smoke under glass, and went no further.

The price came up the staff a breath later.

It went into the left hand, and something in the hand gave. She felt it go, the way you feel a seam go under your arm when you reach too far: a small tearing, low down in the palm, and then warm, and then the warm went cold inside the linen and she couldn't tell any more what the hand was doing. Her arm was ice from the fingers to the shoulder. She leaned on the staff, because she needed to, and her knees went down onto the boards of their own accord, and she let them.

"Jaina." Lyriel, at her side.

"I'm all right. I'm reading it. Let me read it."

She put her face close to the shell. Through the blue the stuff lay black and still, gone to a skin on top already like cooling jam, and she put her whole attention down the ash and into it, because if she was going to pay for it she was going to have it.

It was the same. That was the first thing. It was the thing in Garran's gut and the cooper's wife's, in the doe's blood at the holly bend and the well at Millhaven, the same late drag, the same heaviness, like something hung on the back of every beat, the family resemblance so plain she could have picked it out of a crowd. And it was nothing like them. In the sick it was thin and smeared and everywhere. In here it was whole. It had a shape. It had been built.

She could feel where the parts of it met. One part to keep. One part to wait. One part that did something under the other two that she could not follow at all; she came at it from one side and slid off, and came at it from another and slid off again, the way your eye slides off a word in a language you've never learned. It was not one spell. It was a stack of them, laid down one on another like the planking of a hull, every seam caulked. And it had gone into a sack of flour and through a baker's oven and into a man's milk, and kept. Kept its shape the whole way and sat down in him and waited.

Oh, Light.

She knew what this was. She knew it the way she knew where the reefs lay off Tiragarde, from the charts, from the warnings, from the ships that hadn't come home: the death-currents, the work that ties a thing to the body past the body's end. Every apprentice in Dalaran knew that much. They walked you past the locked shelf in your first year and told you what was on it and why the grille was there. They gave you the signs, so you'd know it if you met it. They never gave you the grammar. That was the whole point of the grille.

This was written in the grammar. She could read every letter of it and not one word.

And it was beautiful. That came up in her before she could stop it, clean and hot. Whoever had made this had made it the way Antonidas built a ward, without one wasted turn, and she wanted to take it apart. She wanted to sit down on these boards with a candle and a week and find out what the third part did.

With Garran swallowing it in his sops. Lovely. Write that in the report.

"It's made," she said. Her voice came out hoarse. "This isn't a fever, and it isn't something gone bad in a wet barn. Somebody made this. Somebody very good."

Nobody answered her.

There was light coming up the stair, yellow and jumping, and boots on the planks, a lot of boots, and Brask's voice at the bottom calling something she didn't catch. Then his head came up over the edge of the loft with a torch held up beside it, and Dickon's behind him with another, and the light shook all over the loft, over the rafters and the pale sacks and the blue shell on the floor and the boy's face.

The boy's face was wet. He'd been crying, silently, the whole time.

"Tam?" Brask said.

He came up the rest of the way and stood there with the torch, looking. At the frost. At the arrow in the post. At the hole in the floor. His thumb was moving on the torch handle the way it had moved on the spear.

"That's Tam," he said, to nobody. "He works the hoist for Joss. He came looking for work at the back end of summer, and Joss took him on." And then, to the boy, in a different voice, the voice you would use to a son: "Tam. What's this? What've you done?"

Tam didn't look at him.

"Cyndia," Sylvanas said. "Lyriel."

They went to him. Cyndia cut the sleeve away from the arrow with her belt knife, one stroke, and Jaina let the hold go, and his legs went out from under him as soon as she did; they'd been kneeling in her frost all this while and they had no more feeling in them than her hand. Cyndia and Lyriel took him under the arms and hauled him up between them, and he hung there with his boots dragging and his head down.

Jaina got up too. It took the staff and the post and a while.

"Who made it?" she said.

He lifted his head. In the torchlight his eyes were very pale and very young, and he looked at her, and then past her at the blue shell on the floor and the black stuff lying under it. He looked at it the way the little ones in the cathedral at Boralus used to look at the reliquary when the priest carried it down the aisle on a feast day, and she had been one of them, at the back, holding her nurse's hand: as if it might do something, and he would be glad, and he was terrified of it.

"I'm not to speak to you," he said.

"Who told you that?"

"I'm not to speak to you." His eyes came back to her. "You don't know what you're keeping them from."

Brask made a sound in his throat.

Jaina opened her mouth and found she had nothing to put in it. Who made this, how, from what, where did it come from, who gave it to you, who gives it to them, what does the third part *do*—and he didn't know. She could see it as plainly as she had seen the knots in the gut. He didn't know what was in the jar any more than she had known what was in the reliquary. He had been told to keep it, and to keep it dark, and he had kept it.

"He's a lookout," Sylvanas said. She hadn't moved from beside the lamp. "Someone gave him a jar and a floorboard and told him to watch. That's all he's for." She looked at Brask. "Captain. Is there somewhere here that locks?"

Brask was still looking at the boy.

"Captain."

"The cage," he said. "By the pound. At the bottom of Chapel Lane."

"Then we'll take him there. Cyndia, the stair. Nobody comes up. Nobody touches that." A tip of her head at the blue shell. "Mage. Will it hold?"

Jaina looked at it. At her hand, which was a shape in grey linen gone stiff and dark at the palm. "Until morning," she said. "If nobody puts a foot on it."

"Nobody will." That was to Cyndia, who had already gone to stand at the head of the stair with her bow, and who said nothing at all. Sylvanas took the lamp up off the sack. "Down."

It was full dark in the lane.

Brask went first with the torch, and the light went ahead of him over the cobbles and up the garden walls and showed her a cat on a gatepost, a bucket, a child's wooden horse lying on its side in a doorway, and slid off them again. Tam came behind him between Lyriel and Brask's man with the pitchfork, who had his free hand clamped round the boy's arm above the elbow and kept looking at it as if he didn't know how it had got there. Tam's legs were coming back to him. He walked like a drunk, bow-kneed, and his boots squelched where her frost had melted into them, and he kept his chin down on his chest and his collar.

Crying again, she thought. She couldn't hear it. She didn't look.

She was looking at the sky. Full dark. She had told Sylvanas full dark, or a little after, and here it was, the last green gone out of the west and the stars come out sharp over the bakehouse roof, and up the hill behind her the chapel had a door and a window and seven wards going thin in it one after another, and Garran's first. It was all she could do not to turn round and go up the lane at a run. The cage was at the bottom of Chapel Lane. On the way. She told herself that, and kept walking downhill, away from it.

Sylvanas walked behind the boy, near enough to touch him. She'd slung the bow. The lamp swung from her left hand, wick still down to a bead, and its little light lay on the back of Tam's neck, on the fair fuzz there and the bones under it.

Her own left arm hung at her side like something borrowed. When she tried to make the fingers move inside the stiff linen they did, a little, she thought, and then the palm woke and told her about it, a long deep burn as if she'd closed her hand on a poker, and she stopped trying.

The pound was a ring of drystone wall at the foot of the lane with a gate in it and a ewe inside, which got up as the torch came by and stood blinking. The cage stood against the pound wall, a little round house of fieldstone no bigger than a bread oven, with a stone dome for a roof and a door of iron bars set in it. Brask had the key on a ring at his belt. He fumbled it, and swore, and fumbled it again, and Jaina watched his big cracked fingers find the right one by feel in the end, without looking, as he must have done a hundred times for a drunk on a fair night. The door went open with a scream of iron.

They put Tam in. There was a stone bench along the back wall and straw on the floor, and Lyriel set him down on the bench, not ungently, and came out, and Brask swung the door to and turned the key, and stood back.

Tam sat on the bench with his hands between his knees.

Sylvanas gave the lamp to Lyriel and went up to the bars. She didn't touch them. She stood a pace back from them with her arms at her sides, and looked in at him for a while, and he didn't look up.

"Who gives you the jar?" she said.

Nothing.

"Who brings the flour? The young man with the cart. Does he bring the jar too?"

Nothing. Tam's shoulders moved, once, as if he'd shivered.

Jaina came up beside her. Close to, the bars breathed cold off them into her face, the cold of iron that had stood in the night air since sundown. She leaned in so her cheek was nearly against them, and held up the staff, and the blue light went into the cage over the straw and the bench and the boy.

"Tam," she said. "Look at me. Slowly. Nobody is going to hurt you. Start with who gave you the jar, and we'll leave everything else for—"

He looked up.

There was something on his lip. White. She thought it was flour, at first; he'd had flour all over him. Then it bubbled.

He looked at her with his pale eyes, and his face was perfectly calm, calmer than it had been all evening, and then it wasn't. His back arched. His heels went out from under him and scraped across the straw, and he slid off the bench sideways onto the floor, and his whole body drew up tight as a bowstring and shook, and shook.

"Key," Sylvanas said.

Brask fumbled it. Sylvanas took the ring out of his hand and had the door open before he'd finished letting go of it, and was in, and down on her knees on the stone. She turned the boy on his side. She got her bare fingers into his mouth, between his teeth, and his teeth closed on them and she didn't take them out; she hooked whatever she could out of his cheek and threw it on the straw, and got in again for more.

Jaina stood at the bars with her face against the iron. She couldn't have said when she'd put it there.

It didn't take long.

The shaking went out of him in waves, each one smaller, the way the shaking goes out of a struck bell, and his heels stopped scraping, and there was a long breath out of him through the foam, a sigh, almost, and then no breath after it. Sylvanas stayed where she was on her knees with her fingers in his mouth until there was nothing more to stay for. Then she took them out.

Nobody said anything. The ewe in the pound had gone back down in her corner. The torch hissed in Brask's fist. From somewhere up the lane a dog barked twice and gave it up, and after that Jaina could hear the torch, and the blood in her own ears, and nothing else whatever.

She went in.

She knelt on the stone beside him because she didn't know what else to do and because the staff was something to do. He was warm. That was what she hadn't been ready for. He lay on the cold floor with his cheek in the straw and he was warm through the smock where her knee touched his back, warm as anybody, and there was a little foam on his lip, only a little, and his eyes were half open. She put the staff to him. There was nothing in him. No drag, no weight, no knot under the ribs; he had never eaten one mouthful of what he kept under that floor. There was just a boy, stopped.

"Here," Sylvanas said.

She had the collar of his smock turned over in her fingers. Its corner was chewed through, a ragged wet hole in the hem of it no bigger than a fingernail, and inside the hem, when she pulled it open, there was a fold of waxed paper, empty, and a smear of something brownish on the cloth.

"He had it in his collar," Sylvanas said. "Sewn in. He had his chin on it all the way down the lane."

Crying, she'd thought. She'd thought he was crying.

Sylvanas sat back on her heels and wiped her fingers on her coat. There were teeth marks in two of them, deep, and she didn't look at those either. She looked at the boy.

"Somebody taught him that," she said. "They sewed it in for him and told him when."

Brask was standing in the door of the cage with the torch. He'd lowered it, so the light came from below and made his face strange. "He ate at my table," he said. "At the harvest supper. He sat next to my youngest and cut her meat for her."

Jaina looked down at Tam. *I'm not to speak to you.* And he hadn't. He'd been told he wouldn't have to, and he'd believed it, and he'd been right.

They had had him. In the loft, with the frost to his waist and the arrow through his sleeve and his face wet, and nobody had told him yet what to say. And somebody had. Somebody had told him months ago, with a needle and thread.

She got up off her knees. It took a while again.

Brask was talking. She came back into it in the middle. "—Joss up first thing, and we'll go through the loft in daylight, and I'll want to hear it from Joss what he knows about this lad, and then we'll see. We'll see what's to be done." He was looking at Tam, not at them. His thumb went up and down the torch handle. "In the morning. I'll have the whole of it in the morning."

In the morning.

Jaina looked up the lane. Somewhere up there in the dark, behind the bakehouse with its brick oven raked out and cooling, the baker was asleep, the big soft man white to the eyebrows, and so was Ellen's mother, and in the morning, before light, the way they did it in every village in Lordaeron and every port in Kul Tiras, one of them would get up and light that oven. And put the loaves in. And Ellen would carry them round in her basket with the cloth over, warm, to the cooper's house and the top farm and everybody, and to the chapel, to Tobin, for his sops.

And Brask would have the whole of it in the morning. He was not a fool. He had a dead boy on the floor of his cage that he'd fed at his own table, and two strangers with bows, and a mage with a jar of something nobody could see, and he wanted one night. Anybody would want one night.

She could tell him. She could explain it, all of it, the stack and the seams and the third part, in a voice that carried, and he'd stand there with his thumb going on the torch and hear one word in five and want his night more, not less.

She turned to Sylvanas instead.

"Ranger-General." She said it low, the way Sylvanas had spoken to him at the spear. "The baker lights his oven before light. If the captain waits for the morning, they'll bake with it. The whole village will have it for breakfast. I can ward a room. I can't ward a village."

Sylvanas looked at her. Then past her, at Brask in the door with his torch held low and his face gone heavy and strange from underneath. She looked at him for a long moment, the way she'd looked at him in the lane before noon, with her hands at her sides and nothing in them.

"Captain," she said. "Your baker lights his oven before dawn?"

"Hob?" Brask lifted his head. "He does. He's at it by cockcrow, him and Nan."

"Then you have until cockcrow."

That was all she said. She didn't explain it. She stood there and let him have the rest of it himself, the way she had let him look at his own street, and Jaina watched him have it. His eyes went up the lane, toward the bakehouse. His thumb stopped.

"Tell me what to shut," he said.

Jaina told him. "The granary. The bakehouse. Tonight, now, before anyone's up. Nobody bakes, nobody sells, nobody carries a sack out of either. And every sack of that man's flour that's gone into any house, since the first load—"

"Every house?"

"I'm sorry, Captain. Every house."

He looked at her for the first time since the loft. Then at the boy on the floor. "Burn it, then," he said. "All of it. In Joss's yard, where they can see it. I'll answer for the flour." He turned round in the door of the cage with the torch and raised his voice. "Piers. Get your brother and the Aldon boys and go round the doors on this side of the brook, every one, and anyone that's bought flour off Joss since harvest, you bring it out to the granary yard. Sacks, crocks, the lot. If they give you any trouble you send them to me." The man with the pitchfork went off up the lane at a run. "Dickon. Joss sent a cart down to the ford this morning with flour for the Hallams and the alehouse. Take the two ponies and Wat and go after it, and turn it round, and don't let anybody eat off it. Go on."

Dickon went.

Brask stood in the door a moment longer, looking at the boy. Then he said, quite quietly, "Cover his face, somebody," and went off down toward the granary with the torch, and the light went with him.

Lyriel had a spare cloak rolled behind her shoulders. She shook it out and laid it over Tam, over his face and his chewed collar, and tucked the edge under, and stood up.

"I have to go up," Jaina said. "The wards."

"Lyriel. With her," Sylvanas said, and went after Brask without waiting to see them go.

She went up Chapel Lane faster than she'd gone up anything in her life, with her skirts in her dead hand and the staff clamped in her elbow and Lyriel's hand at her back the whole way. The lane was steeper going up than it had been coming down. Her breath was sobbing in her chest by the yew. She shouldered the chapel door open.

Tobin was on a stool by Garran's bed with a candle. He looked round at her with his face grey, and she saw the ward over the old man before she saw anything else, and it was a rag. It was a cobweb with the sun through it, three threads and a hole. Under it, the old man's breath caught at the top, and caught, and caught.

She went down on her knees on the flags and put the staff to him and fed it.

She did Garran, and then she did all the others, round the room, cot and box bed and hurdle and door-bed, and by the time she had been round once Garran's had thinned again and she went back to him. She did the room again. Every one of them had thinned. Not as far as his, but every one, while she had been down in the dark with a jar and a boy; Elsie's was holding by its corners, and the cooper's wife's was going at the middle like a worn sheet. She mended them. She went round again.

Somewhere in the rounds she noticed the smell.

It came in under the door and through the cracks of the shutters. Smoke, but not wood smoke; heavier, and sour, with a scorch in it, like bread left in the oven while you went to the door and stayed there talking. She went to the window over the altar and wiped the clear pane with the heel of her bad hand and looked down.

The granary yard was full of fire. Down there beyond the black roofs, beyond the bakehouse chimney, a heap of something burned in the middle of the yard with a smoky red flame, and men were going to it and away from it with sacks on their shoulders, black shapes against it, and throwing the sacks in. Every sack went in with a puff of sparks that went straight up, and then the flame sank down and sulked over it, and spread, and came up again. Somebody had a crock and was tipping it out. She thought she could see Brask by the shape of him, standing at the yard gate with a slate, or a board, or something, and every time a man came in with a sack he put his head down over it.

There was no smoke from the bakehouse chimney. It stood up black and cold against the glow beside it, for the first time all day.

She went back to Garran.

The wards were time. That was all they were. She sat back on her heels between rounds with the staff across her knees and made herself say it plainly, because Antonidas would have, and because she didn't want to. They didn't take one grain of it out of anybody. They held the door, so that nothing more could come in and nothing that was in could get any further, and what was in was in, and the body would do the rest or it wouldn't. And she didn't know which. She had never seen this before. Nobody had; or if somebody had, they'd written it on the far side of the grille.

The bread had stopped. The bread was burning down there in a yard. That was the door shut from the other side, and it had taken Sylvanas and a man with a torch and a heap of burning sacks to shut it, and not one single thing she knew.

So she held the door from this side, and that was all she had, and it would have to do.

She went round again.

The fevers ticked. That was how she heard them, after a while: Garran's breath catching, and the cooper's wife's slow rasp, and the boy's quick light snore, and the brazier, and the staff in the crook of her arm ticking with every bed she passed, all of it going together like a room full of clocks that didn't agree. She couldn't feel a forehead with her hands. Her right hand had felt nothing since the holly bend and her left was a stiff bundle she didn't like to look at, so she did it the way her nurse had done it, a long time ago, in a house on a hill over the sea: she bent and laid her cheek against them. Elsie's was hot and dry. The boy's was hot. Wenna Dunmore's was hot and wet, and Jaina stayed bent over her longer than she meant to, trying to tell whether wet was better, or worse, and not knowing.

Then Garran's, which was like leaning against a stove.

The boy in the cage. She'd been keeping him off, and he came anyway, between Wenna and Garran, with the foam on his lip.

If she'd been quicker. If she'd thrown the shell over that spill in one breath instead of two, and come back to him, and stood over him while they hauled him up, and put the staff on him, would she have seen it? Seen what? A fold of paper in a collar? She'd have seen a boy. She'd been looking straight at him in the loft and seen a boy, crying. If she'd frozen him to the neck instead of the waist—

His jaw. She could have frozen his jaw. She sat with that for a moment, very still. A boy's face, the frost going up over his mouth and his nose, and the noise he would have made. Would she have done it, if she'd known? She didn't know. She had hated doing it to a wolf.

And if she had, he'd have thawed in the end, and sat in the cage with his chin on his collar, and said *I'm not to speak to you*, and waited. He'd been waiting for months.

Slower. That was no good either. She was doing the sums backward from the answer she wanted, which was that it was her fault, because if it was her fault there was something she could have done.

Garran's ward was thinning. She fed it.

Some time in the deep of the night Lyriel came and sat down on the flags beside her and took her left hand without asking. Jaina let her. She didn't want to look, and she did look, and the linen across the palm had gone black-red and stiff, and when Lyriel cut it off with her knife there was a split in the heel of the hand like a split in an overripe plum, gone shiny with the cold.

"Hold still," Lyriel said. "You've bled through." She washed it with water from Tobin's can, quick, and Jaina hissed through her teeth, and Lyriel didn't stop. Clean linen, round and round. Over the thumb, wrist under, tail through, and Lyriel set it in her own teeth and bit it down, quick, and let go. "Don't let Tobin see that. He'll want to bleed you as well."

Jaina looked at the knot.

"I can tie that now," she said.

"Not on your own hand, you can't. Not yet." Lyriel got up. "Tomorrow."

Some time after that Cyndia came in, with the cold of the night on her cloak and a bundle under her arm done up in a cloth. She stood just inside the door and reported to the room in general, standing straight, the way she would have reported to Sylvanas.

"Brask's man came up from the loft and took my place on the stair. The woman at the end of Cooper's Lane was on her step watching the fire. I asked her for oats. She gave me these." She held out the bundle. "Oatcakes. Her own oats, from her own field. She wants the cloth back."

Mother Haw. Jaina could have laughed. "Thank you," she said.

Cyndia gave the bundle to Tobin and went back out without another word, and Tobin broke the cakes up small into a bowl with his red hands and poured milk over them and pressed them down with a spoon, and carried them to Garran.

Sylvanas came once, very late. Jaina didn't hear her come; she was simply in the doorway, in the dark, with the glow of the yard behind her and a smell coming off her cloak of scorched flour and smoke. She didn't come in. She stood with her back to the door frame and her left hand on it, and looked round the room, at the cots, the wards, Tobin asleep on his stool with his chin on his chest. Jaina, on her knees by Elsie.

"It's burned," she said. "All of the granary. All of the bakehouse."

"The houses?"

"Most of them. Brask is still going round." A pause. "The cart's not back."

"Thank you, Ranger-General."

Sylvanas looked at her a moment longer. Then she went, and the doorway was dark again.

Jaina bent and laid her cheek against Elsie's forehead. Hot. She sat back. She'd do Garran next, and then the cooper's wife, and then the boy. Then Elsie again.

---

It rained a little before dawn. She heard it come, soft on the chapel roof and then steadier, and the smell of the yard fire went out of the air under it, and she thought, from a long way off, *good, that's the embers seen to,* and fed the cooper's wife's ward.

When the window over the altar went grey she was on her knees by Elsie again.

The girl was wet through. Her hair was stuck flat to her forehead and the striped blanket was dark at the neck with it, and she was awake, blinking at the ceiling as if she had never seen one before.

"I'm all wet," she said, in a cross little voice. "Is there water?"

Jaina bent and laid her cheek against the girl's forehead, the way she had done it all night, and held it there.

Cool. Damp, and cool, like the outside of a jug.

She sat back on her heels. She didn't say anything. She looked at Elsie, and Elsie looked back at her, puzzled, and said, "Is there?" again, and Lyriel was already coming across from the brazier with Tobin's can.

The cooper's boy was awake too, sitting up in the box bed with his lopsided bandage, wanting to know if there was any more of the oat stuff and why couldn't he have bread, he always had bread. Col was on his feet by the door with his shirt pulled shut, saying he was perfectly well and he was starving and he was going home. Wenna Dunmore slept. Her husband slept on the floor beside her with his hand on her blanket, and when Jaina laid her cheek against Wenna's forehead it was warm, only warm, the way a sleeping person is warm.

Garran was the same.

She went round twice to be sure. His breath still caught at the top, and caught, and caught, and his skin was still dry and hot as a stove, and under the crystal the brown was still out along his arms and up his neck. It was no further than it had been in the deep of the night. That was all she could say for it. It was no further.

The rest were holding. The early ones were more than holding.

On time. Here, in this room, with these people, they had been on time, and it had held. She tried the words over and they sounded like something you'd write at the bottom of a report and underline twice, and she didn't trust them, and she went and put her cheek to Elsie's forehead again.

Still cool.

"Jaina." Lyriel was watching her from the brazier. "She's the same as she was the last time. And the time before."

"I know."

She let the wards stand. They would hold a while on their own now, and she had nothing left to put in them, and after a moment she found that she was sitting on the bench under the window with the staff across her knees, and couldn't remember sitting down. The hum went out of her hands slowly. First out of the right, where she'd never felt it much, then out of the left, and as it went the left woke up behind it in pins and then in burning, the long split in the heel of the palm throbbing under Lyriel's linen with every beat of her heart.

Garran's bandage had slipped again. She saw it from the bench. The pad had come off the cut at his elbow in the night and the strip hung loose round his arm.

She got up. She knelt by the truckle bed and put the staff in the crook of her dead arm and got the pad back on with her left hand, the fingers poking out of the new wrapping just far enough to do it. Round once. Round again. Over the thumb. Wrist under, and the loop held at the root of her thumb where she'd learned to put it. Tail through, first time. She bent and took it in her teeth and pulled her head back, and the knot went down flat and small on the inside of his arm, and held.

It was a silly thing to want. A rag on an old man's arm, after a night like that. She looked at it anyway, a long moment, and it went on holding, and she felt better than she had any right to.

When she looked up, Lyriel was grinning at her from the brazier. She didn't say anything. She tipped her head at the knot, once, and then looked across at the door where Cyndia had been standing in the night, as though Cyndia might have seen it too, and Cyndia wasn't there, and Lyriel seemed satisfied anyway.

Jaina went out into the yard.

The roofs below the chapel were wet and dark and shining, every one of them, and the light was coming up pale yellow behind the hill and catching the wet slates and the thatch and the puddles in the lane. There was smoke from the chimneys, thin, grey, hearth smoke going straight up in the still air. There was no smoke from the bakehouse. The long brick end of it stood there black and cold, and the village lay under the morning without the brown warm haze it had lain under yesterday when she rode in, and it looked smaller without it, and barer, and she could see clear down to the brook.

In the granary yard there was a black heap, flattened by the rain, steaming a little. A cart stood beside it with its shafts down. The cart from the ford, then. Somebody was still forking sacks off it onto the heap, and somebody else was trying to light it again.

Millhaven had had smoke in its chimneys too, once. A cradle with the blanket tucked in. Sixty pairs of feet in a cow way. None of that was any different this morning because Elsie was cool. She tried to set the one against the other anyway, standing there in the wet, the cool forehead against the tucked blanket, and it wouldn't come out either way she ran it, and she let it go.

The horses were saddled under the yew. Cyndia had them ready, all four, and was going round the girths, and didn't look up.

Sylvanas was standing by her mare's head with Brask.

He had a book under his arm. A thick one, bound in calf gone dark at the corners, and a little horn of ink on a cord round his neck, and a pen stuck behind his ear. His face was grey with the night and there was soot on it, and on his jack, and on the backs of his big hands.

"Dickon caught the cart at the ford," he said, as Jaina came up. "Turned it round. The Hallams hadn't had theirs off it yet. The alehouse had had two sacks." He breathed out through his nose. "I've sent down. They'll bring it up."

"Thank you, Captain," Jaina said.

Sylvanas put her hand into her coat and took out something folded. She unfolded it and held it out, not to Brask, but to Jaina.

It was a square of sacking, cut neat with a knife from the side of a sack. Pale and clean. There was a mark on it in black, stencilled: a sheaf of wheat in a ring, and under the ring, in square letters, a word.

ANDORHAL.

"From the loft," Sylvanas said. "His sacks. I cut it before they went in the fire."

"Andorhal," Brask said. He looked at the square of sacking as if it had bitten him. "That's the big granaries. Down the grain road, south and west, past the ford. Three days, with a cart. Everything in the south goes through Andorhal, one way or another. My barley goes through Andorhal."

The young man with the cart had come from the west road. Cyndia had said so last night. Jaina took the sacking in her left hand and held it, and the clean pale weave of it smelled of nothing at all.

"Then that's where his cart came from," she said. And then, because it was true: "Or where he wanted us to think it came from."

Sylvanas took the sacking back and folded it and put it away in her coat. "Either way," she said, "it's the road he drove."

Brask had got the book open against his chest. He took the pen from behind his ear and pulled the stopper out of the horn with his teeth.

"I'll want your names," he said round the stopper. He spat it into his hand. "All of you. If anyone comes asking what happened here, I want to tell it right."

He turned to a clean page. He wrote slowly, with his tongue in the corner of his mouth and the book braced on his forearm, and the pen scratched across the paper, and he said each word aloud as he made it, the way children say their letters.

"The mage," he said. "Jaina." Scratch. "Proudmoore. Spell that for me."

"P, R, O, U, D," Jaina said. "Moore. M, O, O, R, E."

"Proud-moore." He wrote it. He looked at it a moment, and then up at Sylvanas, and the pen hung over the page.

"Sylvanas Windrunner," Sylvanas said. She spelled it for him, flat and clear, one letter at a time, as she might have given him the bearing of a ford, and Brask wrote it letter by letter after her and said it back, carefully, getting the middle wrong and then right.

Then Cyndia, from the far side of her horse, without looking up from the girth. Then Lyriel, who spelled hers and then said, "And you can put that one of us ties a good knot, Captain, if there's room on the line," and glanced at Cyndia, who didn't look round.

Brask looked at her. He didn't write that.

He blew on the page and shut the book on it, and stood there holding it shut against his chest with both hands, the way he had held the spear in the lane.

"Right," he said.

He went off down the lane toward the granary yard, where the heap had begun to smoke again.

"Mage," Sylvanas said. "Up."

Lyriel was at her stirrup. Jaina got up badly, the way she always got up, with a foot in Lyriel's cupped hands and her skirts caught under her, and Lyriel took her weight and set her straight in the saddle and let go and went to her own horse. The mare shifted under her. Jaina sat there and found she was shaking, very slightly, all over, the way you shake after a long night on deck, and she put her bandaged hand down flat on the mare's warm neck until it stopped.

They went down through the village in the same order they had come up it, Sylvanas in front and Cyndia on the left and Lyriel on the right and Jaina in the middle, and two empty places at the back. People were at their doors. The egg woman was setting out her trestle. Nobody called a price. They watched the horses go by, and the woman in the blue apron put her hand up, not a wave, just a hand, and took it down again. They went past the bakehouse with its oven cold and its door shut, and the baker standing in the yard next door with a fork in his hands and flour still white to his eyebrows, watching the heap smoke. They went past the pound, and the cage. Jaina didn't look.

At the bottom of the village, past the brook, the road split. One way went back north, toward Millhaven. The other went off south and west between its hedges, broad and flat and rutted deep, two long grooves worn into the mud by every loaded cart that had ever come up it.

The staff shifted in the crook of her arm, all on its own, and leaned toward the ruts.

Sylvanas turned her mare onto the grain road without a word. They fell in behind her, and the ruts went on ahead of them into the wet morning, south and west, toward wherever the young man with the cart had come from.
