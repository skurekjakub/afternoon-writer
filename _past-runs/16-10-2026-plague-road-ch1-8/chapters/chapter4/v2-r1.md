The taste came before she saw the village. Old copper sat on the back of her tongue. In the crook of her elbow the crystal began to tick against its claws, light and quick, tapping like a moth at a shutter.

Jaina swallowed. It came back.

Then the lane turned between two cottages and the village was all around them, loud with the business of a working morning. A hammer rang on iron somewhere ahead, quick and slow and quick again. A woman called a price for eggs, another woman called it lower, and the first one laughed at her. Geese hissed from a yard. A man was backing a cart through a gate, shouting it back, back, back, and the hub knocked the gatepost anyway and he swore at the horse. Bread smoke lay over all of it, low and warm and brown. Two boys came pelting under the mare's nose after a hoop. The mare flung her head up and blew at them, and Lyriel reached across from the right and took the cheek-strap without turning her head.

"Steady," Lyriel said, to the horse or to her.

Jaina had the reins looped over her wrapped left hand, which was all that hand was good for this morning, throbbing inside the linen with every jolt. The right lay in her lap, something she had been asked to carry for someone else, the staff riding in the crook of that arm. She had slept, and woken with the print of the stirrup buckle on her cheek and a clear head for the first time since Dalaran. She had been cheerful, saddling up. She had made Lyriel laugh.

Now the cold sat under her breastbone, exactly where the staff had said it would be, and under the hammer and the geese and the eggs, the drag.

She shut her eyes to hear it. The currents ran under the cobbles as they ran everywhere, a slow pulse she had known so long she only heard it when it went wrong. Here it had gone wrong. Each beat came in and something came in after it, late, a weight hung on the tail. The same drag she had felt through the floor of the cell at the Pass and in the doe's blood and at the Millhaven well.

It was quieter here. Millhaven had been a knot pulled so tight it rang, every line hauling the same dead weight into the crossing with nothing alive to cover it. Here it was thin and spread and moving, coming and going as the mare carried her past a doorway, a stall, a woman with a basket. Warm bodies all round her, dozens of them, and it was in them, moving with them, the loud warm noise of the place laid over it, a blanket over a cough.

Quieter because it was still moving. Because it still had somewhere warm to go.

Oh, Light. That was a horrible way to put it, and it was right.

She opened her eyes. What would she show anyone, if anyone asked? A taste, a crystal that clicked, and her own face going green. Brilliant, Jaina. Antonidas's prize student, sure as stone with nothing in her hands to prove it, her hands not much use for anything else either.

The egg stall was on the left, a trestle under an awning. The woman behind it was haggling with a broad-hipped woman in a blue apron who had a little girl against her skirts, five or six years old, in a knitted cap pulled down to her eyebrows. The child's cheeks were red as scrubbed brick, two hot patches high on the bones, the rest of her face pale. Her eyes were very bright. She leaned her whole weight on her mother's hip, sagging by degrees, and every time she sagged her mother hitched her up again without looking down.

"Stand up, Mag, you're not a sack of turnips. Nearly done."

The child stood up, coughing into her mother's apron, a small wet cough. Her mother went on haggling over the eggs.

The crystal ticked as the mare went past, and again at the next stall, where a man was selling salt herring out of a barrel. Jaina could not read the child from the saddle, not through the noise, not with the staff loose in a dead arm. The flush might be any fever. Children coughed for any reason; they did nothing else between harvest and spring.

But once she had heard the first cough she heard them everywhere. A man in a doorway, fist against his mouth. Someone behind a half-open shutter upstairs, a dry hacking that nobody went to. Two women at the pump, one turning aside to cough and then back to finish what she was saying. Nobody in the street turned to look.

*Everybody coughing at the well.*

The girl had said it into Neral's collar, in the end house at Millhaven, with the hatch still open beside her. Missus Hale's baby first. Then Mister Pell. Then everybody coughing at the well. And then nobody: tables laid for supper and sixty sets of toes pressed into the clay of a cow way, all pointing uphill.

So this was Millhaven a fortnight ago or a week or however long it had been, Millhaven with smoke in its chimneys and a market in its street and a woman telling her daughter to stand up straight.

Jaina half turned in the saddle toward the back of the line, meaning to say it to Neral. There was nobody there. She turned back.

"Ranger-General," Cyndia said, on the left, her voice level and carrying no farther than it needed to. "Shutters up on three houses, this side, since the bend. Middle of the day."

"I saw them," Windrunner said, from the front.

Jaina had not seen them. She looked now, craning over Cyndia's shoulder: a cottage with its shutters barred and its door shut, a stool on the step with a cup on it, left there for someone who would not come out.
Then the horses stopped.

Windrunner's mare stopped first and the rest bunched behind it, iron scraping on the cobbles. Across the lane, at the height of the horses' chests, lay the shaft of a spear.

A boar spear, with a broad leaf of a head and a crossbar under it. The man who held it held it level, in both hands, across the whole width of the lane between a wall and a water butt, the way a farmer holds a gate shut against his own cattle. He was big, heavy through the shoulders, in a quilted jack too warm for the day and a leather cap, fair stubble going grey. His hands on the ash shaft were brown and cracked across the knuckles, a split thumbnail on the right gone black underneath. Behind him stood two men, one with a pitchfork and one with an unstrung hunting bow. The one with the bow looked about sixteen and held it as if it might go off.

"That's far enough," the big man said. "We've sick here. Nobody comes in armed, and nobody comes in I don't know."

Behind them the egg woman had stopped calling her price.

Jaina had it all ready, in good order, fit to hand to Antonidas: *Captain, what you have here is not a fever. It is a contamination of the currents, necromantic in character, and it is already in your people, and if you do not let us through—* She had leaned forward in the saddle to say it, mouth already open.

She looked at the egg stall. The woman in the blue apron had turned round with the child against her skirts. The herring man had stopped with his scoop half out of the barrel, and the two women at the pump had set their buckets down. Every face in the lane was turned toward the spear.

*Necromantic.* In this lane. In front of that child.

She shut her mouth.

Windrunner swung down from her mare without hurry and without looking round. She lifted the bow off her shoulder and handed it up to Cyndia, then walked forward to the spear, empty-handed, until the shaft lay across the front of her coat. She was taller than he was and made nothing of it.

"You're right to stop us," she said.

She said it low, for him alone. The man had to lean in to hear it, and he did lean.

"Then you'll turn round, my lady."

"Ranger-General."

His thumb moved on the ash. "Ranger-General."

"We've come from Millhaven."

The thumb stopped.

"Millhaven," he said.

"We came into it at dusk. The tables were laid. Bowls on them and a spoon in every bowl and a loaf." Windrunner's voice went on at the same level, a woman telling him how far it was to the next ford. "There was a sickbed made up with a basin and a cup of water beside it. A cradle in the house by the smithy with the blanket tucked in. No child in it. No one in any house. Living or dead."

The man said nothing. The boy with the bow had come up at his shoulder to listen, and the big man put out his elbow without looking and moved him back.

"They walked out of it," Windrunner said. "Up the cow way above the village, in the night. Sixty of them that we could count. We counted their prints."

"Walked."

She waited. Then, in exactly the same voice: "There was a child left alive, shut in a cellar. She said it started with everybody coughing at the well."

She kept her eyes on his face, and he looked at his own street himself. He turned his head to the pump, where the two women stood with their buckets, and then, slower, to the stall. The child coughed again into the blue apron. He watched that a long while.

When he turned back his face had gone a bad colour under the stubble.

"It's fever," he said. "It's the time of year for it. Tobin says it's fever."

"Then let my mage see your sick, and we'll know by dark whether I'm a liar."

His thumb slid up the shaft and back down.

"Your people are watching you, Captain," Windrunner said, even lower. "Keep your spear. Walk with me. I'd like to see your village, and I'd like them to see me with you."

Jaina had stopped breathing somewhere around the cradle, her left hand closed on the reins hard enough to make the raw fingers sing inside the linen. What would she have done, if he said no? Argued. Explained louder, with diagrams, while the egg woman listened to every word and went home and told her sister. Light help her, she would have drawn him the currents in the air with a hand that didn't work.

The big man lifted the spear. He brought it upright and grounded the butt beside his boot, and his shoulders came down.

"Willem Brask," he said. "I've the militia here, such as it is." He jerked his chin at the boy. "Tobin has them in the chapel. Dickon, take the mage to Tobin. Go on." Then, up at Jaina, as an afterthought: "Miss."

"Thank you, Captain," Jaina said.

It came out higher than she meant it to.

Windrunner had already turned. "Lyriel. With the mage. Cyndia, with me." She took her bow back from Cyndia and slung it. "Mage. What you find, I want before dark."

"Yes, Ranger-General."

Windrunner walked off down the lane at Brask's shoulder, Cyndia a step behind. The egg woman called her price again, too loudly. Brask stopped at the pump to say something to the two women, and one of them laughed, short and shaky, and picked up her bucket.

Jaina sat on the mare in the middle of the lane, her ears going hot.

She had Windrunner down as maps. Ground, and ambush angles, and ears sharp enough to hear a wolf at the back of a holly wood; a woman who could read a map backward and be right, and had been, and after that Jaina had stopped talking to her in small words, she had, she had made a point of it. But people? On people she had written her off. A woman who said *Mage. Up.* and expected the mare and the mage to obey in the same tone.

Wrong, then. She had not raised her voice once. She had given her bow away, which cost her nothing with two more bows behind her. She had conceded the man his point before he finished making it, and told him about a cradle and a spoon in a bowl and a child coughing at the well, not one word about currents, and never once pointed. Jaina would have explained, at length, in a voice that carried, and the egg woman would have had the word *necromancer* by heart before dinner.

Could it be learned? Antonidas had never taught it, and had never needed to; men lowered their spears for Antonidas because of the hat.

"Lady Proudmoore." Lyriel had come round to her stirrup on foot, her own mare's reins over her arm. "Will you get down, or shall I fetch a ladder?"

"I'm getting down."

She got down badly, as she did every time, the staff sliding in her elbow and her right leg not quite clearing the cantle; Lyriel took her weight at the waist without comment, set her on the cobbles and let go. The copper taste was stronger down here.

The boy, Dickon, waited with his unstrung bow in his fist, looking at Lyriel's ears and trying not to. "It's this way, miss," he said, and took them up a side lane between two garden walls, the hammer ringing ahead on its iron.

The chapel stood at the top of the lane, a squat grey house of the Light with a bell-cote on the gable and no bell in it. Someone had carried the benches out and stacked them in the yard under the yew, upside down, legs in the air, dead beetles. Dickon knocked with the end of his bow and pushed the door open without waiting. The smell came out through the open door.

Vinegar first, sharp enough to sting. Then sweat and tallow and the slop bucket nobody had emptied since morning. And under all of it, faint and thick, a sweetness.

Pears gone brown.

*They had no pears,* the girl had said, in the dark at Millhaven, when Jaina asked whether the smell came before the fever or after. Before she was hot.

"Light," Lyriel said under her breath, behind her. She tied the mares to the yard rail and came in after them.

It was dim inside and close and too warm. Every shutter was barred. A brazier smoked in the middle of the floor where the benches should have been. Round it, packed so close their sides touched, stood every bed in the village that could be carried: cots, a box bed with its doors taken off, a child's truckle bed with an old man on it, his feet hanging off the end in grey stockings, a door laid across two trestles with a straw pallet on top. The blankets were every colour there was, each family sending its own bedding with its own sick. Some beds were still empty, made up for whoever came in next. She went between them sideways, the staff upright against her chest, and still her skirts brushed somebody's hand.

Someone was coughing. Several people were. Someone else said *Mam, Mam, Mam* in a small flat voice, over and over.

A man came round the end of the box bed carrying a basin in both hands, small and bald as an egg, sleeves rolled to the elbow, leather apron gone black down the front, his hands and forearms scrubbed red to the wrists and split across every knuckle. Goose grease in the cracks, from the smell. He stopped when he saw them, taking in Lyriel's bow and Lyriel's ears and Jaina last.

"Willem sent you?"

"Captain Brask, yes. I'm Jaina Proudmoore. I'm a mage, from Dalaran."

"Dalaran." He made it sound a long way off, and to blame. "Tobin. I do teeth and bones, mostly. Births." He looked down at the grubby linen bound round her hands. "What happened to those?"

"Frost."

"Want me to look at them?"

It was so unexpected that she nearly laughed. "No. Thank you. They'll keep." She looked past him at the cots. "What have you been treating them for?"

"Fever." He set the basin down on the end of the door-bed and wiped his hands on the apron. "What else would I treat? It comes on like fever. They burn like fever. Willow bark, vinegar cloths, keep them warm and keep the draught off. I bled old Garran yesterday and it did him no good at all, so I'll not do it again. Is it fever?"

"It's a fever," Jaina said. "There's something else in it too. I need to see who's been ill longest, and who came in last."

"Garran's longest." He jerked his head toward the truckle bed. "Since before market. Elsie came in this morning." He pointed across the room to the door laid on the trestles, where a girl of fifteen or so lay under the striped blanket with her eyes open, watching them. "She was milking yesterday. Fainted in the byre."

Jaina went to Elsie first.

The girl turned her head on the pallet. Her face was flushed and damp, hair stuck to her forehead in dark points. When Jaina bent over her the sweetness came up off her breath, strong enough that Jaina had to swallow.

"I'm going to look at you," Jaina said. "With a light. It won't hurt you. You might feel warm."

"I'm warm now," Elsie said, plucking at the blanket.

"Warmer, then. I'm sorry."

She could not do this as she had done the doe. Her right hand would not have felt the girl if she had laid it on her, and the left had nothing on its fingertips; she had left that on the stones of the well at Millhaven. Windrunner had told her flatly there would be no more hand-reading, and for once she had not argued. The staff, then. She turned to Lyriel.

"Will you hold this for me?"

Lyriel took it braced for it to do something sudden: one hand under the crystal and one round the silver wire. "If it bites me, Lady Proudmoore, I'm letting go."

"It won't. Lower. The crystal against her, here, under the ribs. Gently. Through the blanket is fine."

Lyriel set it there. Jaina bent and laid her cheek against the ash where the wood was dark from her own hands, and shut her eyes.

The girl's pulse came up through the wood, fast and light. Under it, faint and very close, the drag.

She said the word for light, the plain one, the first every apprentice in the Violet Citadel learned and the last she would forget, and sent it down the staff, water down a rope.

The crystal woke. Pale gold went out of it into the girl through the blanket and her shift and her skin, and when Jaina opened her eyes Elsie's belly was lit from inside, pink and gold, the dark shadows of the ribs standing out above. The light moved with the pulse, outward and back, rising into the chest to beat easy and clean round the heart and fill the lungs before it came back.

Under the ribs it did not come back clean.

There was a place just below the breastbone, about the size of a fist, where the light went in on the beat and came out after it late and dragging, and the wrong colour. Brown, a drop of ink let fall into a glass of water, curling. Below it, along the gut, ran a thread of the same brown, thin and broken, following the coils down. And nowhere else: the heart clean, the lungs clean, the blood in her arms going pink to the fingertips when Jaina sent the light out along them, and coming back pink.

"Does it hurt her?" Lyriel said quietly. "Does it hurt you?"

"No. A little."

The staff was going cold under her cheek. The cold crept up out of the wood into the bones of her face, and into her left hand, which had no business feeling anything, aching now, plunged in snow. When she let the light go the girl's skin went dark and ordinary. Elsie stared up at her.

"What's in me?"

"I don't know yet." Jaina straightened, her back cracking. "I'm going to find out. Lie still."

"Garran," she said to Tobin.

The old man on the truckle bed did not open his eyes when they came. His face had gone the colour of a tallow candle, the skin stretched across the bones of the nose. His breath caught wet at the top every time. His belly under the sheepskin was round and hard. The sweetness had gone off his breath, and in its place was something heavier, meat kept too long.

Lyriel set the crystal against him without being told, angling it in under the sheepskin. Jaina laid her cheek to the wood.

The light went in, and almost none of it came back clean.

It was everywhere. It came up out of the old man's belly in brown threads, branching out under the ribs and up into the liver, dark and swollen with it, and into the lungs, where the light went in on each breath and came out late and dirty and broken up. Along the great vessels of the neck. Down both arms, thin as hair, almost to the fingers. Every thread of it ran back, if she followed it, to the same place under the breastbone where the girl's fist of brown had been.

She had seen this before, in a doe in a ditch below the Pass, with her bare hand flat on the hide, running through the meat and the blood and the liver, roots in a pot. She had not seen it in a man who was still breathing.

The same. Light help her, it was the same, alive in him, and him still alive round it.

She let the light go and stood up, the cold already in her teeth.

"Who else came in in the last day or two?"

Tobin showed her, going from bed to bed with the basin still in his hands. A boy of ten on a pallet by the brazier, the cooper's youngest, hot since yesterday. His mother in the box bed, hot since the day before. "The cooper's down to him and her, so far," Tobin said. "Three more of them in that house. I've a bed kept for each."

A young man in his shirt lay on the floor by the door with his boots still on, propped against the wall, opening one eye at them.

"I'm not sick," he said. "I'm tired. I've been on the hill all summer and I came down to a forge. Tobin's an old woman."

"Tobin's the one with the bed," said Tobin.

The young man's breath smelled of brown pears. Lyriel moved the staff from one to the next and Jaina followed it, bending to the wood each time, sending the light down. Each time it came back the same. Under the ribs, a fist of brown, or half a fist. A thread along the gut. Nothing in the lungs, nothing in the blood, nothing in the arms or the head.

By the door, the young man had the smallest knot of all, no bigger than a walnut. He frowned down through his open shirt at the light going in and out of his own stomach. "Well, that's not right," he said, in the tone of a man sold a bad horse.

The gut. Every one of them, at the start, in the gut. And the long cases went outward from there, out from the stomach into everything else, water spreading from where it was spilled.

So it went in by the mouth.

They were swallowing it.

"Well?" Tobin had come up behind her with the basin, peering over her shoulder. "What is it?"

She got up off her knees. It took a second try, Lyriel's hand under her elbow, gone the moment she was up.

"It starts in the stomach," she said, keeping her voice down for the young man's sake, and for Elsie's, who was watching them across the room. "In everyone who's newly ill, it's only there. In Garran it's everywhere now. It spread out from there. They're taking it in. Swallowing it."

"Swallowing what?"

"I don't know."

"It's the water, then." Tobin set the basin down on the cooper's boy's pallet, hard enough to slop it. "I said so to Willem. Bad water. There's the three wells and the brook, and the brook comes down past the tannery—"

"It might be the water."

It might. The well at Millhaven had read thin, thinner than the doe. Thin water didn't put a fist of it under a dairymaid's ribs between one evening's milking and the next morning, unless she drank a great deal of it. Everybody drank a great deal of water, Jaina; that was rather the point of water. Food, then, whose food? Everybody's, because everybody ate. A cup passed along a bench. A spoon licked and handed on. A mother kissing a child goodnight. Two down in the cooper's house already, and what did a household do together but eat from one pot and drink from one bucket and sleep in one bed and breathe each other's breath?

She couldn't strike out a single one of them. And every hour she stood here not knowing, every house in the village was sitting down to its dinner.

"Lyriel," she said, pointing with her chin. "Bring me that jug." A brown crock with a chip out of the lip, nearly full. "From the pump?"

"From the pump," Tobin said. "Same as every bucket in the street."

Lyriel set the crystal to the glaze, careful of it, and Jaina bent to the ash and sent the light down.

The light went into the water and lay there, dull gold, beating with the slow pulse of the currents coming up through the floor. Each beat came in. Something came in after it, late. Thin, though, a hair's weight on the tail of each beat. She had felt worse in the ditch water below the Pass.

"There's some in the water," she said. "A little. Less than in Elsie, by a long way."

"So it's the water and other things besides."

"It might be." Thin was everywhere south of the Pass by now, as damp is everywhere in a cellar. Did thin put a fist of it under a dairymaid's ribs between one milking and the next? She didn't think so. She knew it was in a jug.

The door banged open behind her before she could decide what that was worth.

Two men came in sideways with a sheep hurdle between them and a woman on it. Big and fair and red-armed, in a brown wool dress with the hem soaked dark, she was fighting, drumming her heels on the wattles. One hand gripped the hurdle's edge and the other clawed at her own collar as if it choked her. She talked fast and low and to no one in the room: the hens, somebody had to see to the hens, nobody ever saw to the hens but her.

"Dunmore's wife," Tobin said. "From the top farm. Put her down there, Hew, by the door-bed, mind the bucket—"

They put her down on the floor between Elsie's trestles and the brazier. The bigger of the two men stood over her with his hands hanging. Behind him, in the doorway, a little girl in a man's coat that came down to her ankles held a shawl against her mouth. She came in after them because nobody told her not to and sat down on the flags by the font, watching.

She could stand here and read jugs until dark, the salt box and the bucket and the ladle and the cat, and every one of them would come back thin while every house in the village went on sitting down to its dinner. Or she could do the one thing she was sure she knew how to do.

"Lyriel. The staff, please."

Lyriel gave it to her. Jaina got it into the crook of her right arm, where the dead hand could not drop it because the dead hand was not being asked to hold anything, and pinned it against her ribs. She knelt by the hurdle on the cracked flags, set the heel of the staff on the stone beside the woman's shoulder, and leaned the crystal out over her.

It was the first holding ward any apprentice learned in Dalaran, and no healing at all. They taught it in the alchemy rooms for the glass: you put it round a flask that had to go into the furnace so it would not crack, and it did nothing to whatever was boiling inside. She had put it round a hundred flasks. She had never put it round a woman.

She said it. The light that came down out of the crystal was blue, not gold, a cold clean blue, the shadow side of snow, coming down over the woman in a fine mesh, settling, sinking in. Through it, under the ribs and spreading, the brown showed murky and yellow, the colour of a fly in old honey.

The drag hit the ward on the next beat.

Jaina felt it in her arm. It came up the staff as a pull, a late weight hung on the tail of the woman's pulse, and the ward took it and held. Next beat, the same. And the next, each one a tug on a line, each one the line holding. The cold came up out of the ash into the crook of her elbow and spread across her chest under the bodice, a wet shirt.

The woman's heels stopped drumming.

Her hand let go of the hurdle, the other sliding down off her collar to lie open on the brown wool, palm up. Her head rolled toward the brazier, and she was still.

Quite still.

Jaina's own breath stopped in her. No. No, she hadn't—

The woman's chest went up. Slow. It hung there a moment and came down, and rose again. The lines round her mouth let go. She was asleep.

In the corner, the little girl in the man's coat took the shawl away from her face.

Jaina's shoulders came down. She stayed on her knees until she was sure the ward would stand without her, then got up, badly, and moved on to the cooper's wife.

Then the cooper's boy. Then Garran, whose ward took the most holding, giving her a cold she felt in the roots of her teeth. Then Elsie, who said, "It's a wet sheet," and then, "It's nice," closing her eyes. The wards stood on their own once they were set, for a while, and then they thinned. She went round the room from cot to box bed to hurdle to door-bed and round again, trimming them before they guttered, a girl in a house with too many lamps and one taper.

Most of them were early. The cooper's boy, Elsie, the young man with his walnut, the woman from the top farm: a fist or less, all in the gut, nothing in the blood. Only Garran was late.

The bandage on Garran's arm had come loose.

She saw it on her second round, kneeling to feed his ward. Tobin had bled him yesterday at the inside of the elbow, and the pad had slid, the cut weeping thin and pink into the sheepskin. Tobin was across the room with Hew Dunmore, who wanted to know if his wife was dead and would not believe she wasn't. Lyriel was at the brazier, warming a bucket of water. Jaina had the staff pinned in her right arm, the ward half-trimmed, the pull coming up the ash in its slow late beats. She could not let go to call anybody.

So she picked up the strip of linen with her left hand.

She got the pad back over the cut easily enough, and the strip round his arm, and round again, fingers that would only curl halfway, the rag on them grey from the morning. Then she had to tie it, and she had one hand to tie it with.

The end slid out when she tried to tuck it under. She tried a half hitch round her own fingers and dragged the linen off with them. The ward thinned under the crystal and she fed it, the cold coming up, the strip hanging off his elbow, a wet ribbon off a maypole.

She had tied a bowline behind her back at eight years old, with her father's bosun watching, for a bet. She knew a sheet bend and a clove hitch and the knot the Kul Tiran gunners used on the breeching ropes, and she could not tie a rag round an old man's arm.

"Here."

Lyriel was crouched on the other side of the truckle bed. She had come without a sound, as they did, reaching across Garran to put her hands over Jaina's on the linen, quick and dry and warm.

"Loop over your thumb. Like that. Turn your wrist under. Tail through. Now take it in your teeth and set it."

Then she let go and sat back on her heels.

Jaina looked at the knot she had not tied. Then she pulled it out.

Lyriel's eyebrows went up.

"Again," Jaina said.

Loop over the thumb. The loop slid off the end as she turned, the thumb too stiff to bend far enough to hold it. She said a word she had learned from the gunners, and Lyriel's mouth twitched. Again, further down, against the root of the thumb. Wrist under. Tail through, which needed the tips of two fingers she barely had and took a few tries.

"Now set it," Lyriel said. "Go on, Jaina. Bite it. It won't bite you back."

She bent and took the tail of the linen in her teeth. It tasted of vinegar and old blood and Tobin's goose grease. She pulled her head back and her hand back, against each other, and the knot closed down flat and small on the inside of Garran's arm. It held.

It was a bandage knot. She had read the inside of this man's liver by spell light not long ago, and she wanted that knot more than she had wanted anything since Dalaran. That was absurd. She was going to tie the next one on her own, if it took her all afternoon.

And Lyriel had said *Jaina*. Just like that, in the middle of telling her to bite something.

"Where do the Farstriders learn that?" she said. "Or is it only you?"

"All of us. In the first year. You do it with the left until you can, and then they make you do it with the right." Lyriel was already standing, the bucket in her other hand. "We learn it so we can patch our own mistakes."

She went back to the brazier. Their own mistakes. A ranger with a hurt hand and nobody to stop and tie it for her, doing it one-handed, with her teeth.

She tied the next dressing herself, on the cooper's boy, who had picked a scab on his knee down to the blood while he lay there with nothing else to do. The wards held, all of them, all the way round, and nobody in the room was any better.

The wards held the way the glass held. Garran was exactly as sick as when she knelt down, and so was everyone else; they were only slower about it. Whatever had put a fist in Elsie overnight was still out in the village, in somebody's kitchen.

What were they swallowing? The light only told her where it was, not how it had got there. She would have to ask them.

"Tobin," she said. "Have you got a slate?"

He had one hanging on a nail inside the door, cracked, in a wooden frame, with a row of names down the side and strokes after them that he said were teeth, and who owed him for them. He wiped the teeth off with his sleeve without a pang.

"How much?"

"Small. Half a fist. A fist. Everywhere." She heard herself and nearly laughed. Antonidas would have had a fit. "Those will do."

He wrote in big square capitals, slowly, the tip of his tongue showing. GARRAN. COOPER, WIFE. COOPER, BOY. ELSIE. COL, who was the young man by the door, the smith's striker, lodging over the forge. WENNA DUNMORE.

Tobin knew which house drew from which well as he knew whose teeth were whose. "Cooper's Lane well, the cooper's lot. Garran, the same; he's three doors down. Col at the forge, the same. Top farm has its own, in the yard. Elsie's at Ashby's, and Ashby's draws from the brook, because the Ashbys were always too mean to dig."

Jaina looked at what he had written. Garran, everywhere. The cooper's wife and the boy, a fist each and more. Col, a walnut. Three houses on one Cooper's Lane well, and it ran from the worst in the room to the best.

So it wasn't the water. It might still be the water; the jug had read thin, and thin was thin. But a whole lane drew one bucket and it came out of them as a dying man and a boy who'd picked his scab and a striker who thought Tobin was an old woman. It didn't explain Garran.

"Who's been nursing Garran?" she said.

"Nobody, till I brought him up here. His wife's been gone since the spring. He's the cottage on his own."

Contact, then: two down in the cooper's house, one pot and one bucket and one bed. But Garran, first in the village and worst in the room, had lived alone with nobody to catch it off.

She had two answers that fitted half the room each, and she wanted very badly for one of them to fit the whole of it. Antonidas would have told her that was exactly when to stop wanting.

"Mam says how are they, and is there anything else."

"Bless her, tell her the same as yesterday, Ellen," Tobin said.

Tobin put the spoon to the old man's mouth. The old man took it as a baby takes it, lips working, swallowing.

Tobin looked round, spoon in the air.

She went round the room again, asking them this time.

In a room full of fevers. With Garran swallowing his sops. Lovely, Jaina. Shall we have a prize-giving?

Bread. So who bakes? Ellen's mother. Who mills? Who sells the flour to the store, and the store to the—

No. Slower. She was running ahead of herself with the answer in her teeth, as she'd nearly run at the storehouse in Millhaven. Rule out the comfortable answer before you build a house on it. Cooper's Lane well. Her own oats. Not sick.

Not grain, then. One flour. Whatever was in the bakehouse loaves and the store sacks, and the more you ate of it and the longer, the worse you were. Garran, morning and night for weeks, and it had gone from his gut into everything. Col, two nights, a walnut. He looked at the bowl, then at the basket of loaves on the end of the door-bed. "Ellen's a good girl," he said.

Tobin got up slowly, knees cracking, and carried the bowl to the slop bucket to tip it in.

The chapel stood at the top of the lane with the whole village lying below it, going down toward the brook. She took hold of the doorframe with her left hand, the wood gritty under the linen. Down the slope a chimney stood up out of a low stone building with a long brick oven built against its end, its smoke brown and warm and lying along the lane. Next to the bakehouse stood a bigger building, long and stone and windowless, with a hoist beam over a door set high in the wall at the height of a cart bed. A woman came out of the bakehouse with a loaf under each arm and went off up the street.

The flour had come into the bakehouse from that long stone building next door, and whoever kept the long stone building had got it from somewhere, from somebody. That half would tell her whether this was one bad harvest gone wrong in a damp barn or something else, and it was down there in who sold what to whom. Windrunner was out there with it, and Jaina was up here holding a doorframe.

She could go down and put the staff to the sacks in front of whoever owned them, frightening him half to death, while up here the wards went out one by one.

Behind her, Wenna Dunmore said something about the hens in her sleep. Jaina went back in.

Sylvanas was down at the long stone building.

The ward on Elsie needed feeding. Jaina fed it.

She was asking about the flour. What does it cost. Who brings it. Since when. Who else buys it. Jaina knew it as she would have known a spell from somebody's hands, without hearing a word. Sylvanas had walked into this village that morning with nothing in her hands and had not had one word from this room. She knelt by Elsie with the cold in her teeth, annoyed, unable to decide whether it was with Sylvanas for getting there or with herself for being surprised. Herself, obviously. In the lane before noon she had decided she was wrong about Sylvanas, and then she had walked in here and gone straight back to thinking of her as maps.

Elsie was awake. "Is it the bread?" the girl said. "I heard you. With Tobin."

"I think so. I'm not sure yet."

"I had two pieces at noon yesterday," Elsie said, twisting the edge of the blanket. "Before the byre. Mistress gave me the end one, because I'd done the calves."

"Then don't have any more," Jaina said. "Oats, if anybody brings you oats. Nothing else." She fed the ward. "You did nothing wrong."

Elsie pulled the blanket up under her chin.

Down in the lane Sylvanas had stopped writing and turned to look up the hill at the chapel, at the window; through that glass there was no telling. Then she went into the bakehouse.

Jaina could have got up then and walked down with Tobin's slate. There, it's the flour. Now find me who. But Elsie's ward would thin, and Garran's, and she stayed where she was, going round again.

The light was going by the time they came up the lane.

Jaina had done more rounds than she could have said. Her left hand was a weight at the end of her arm, the frost come through the linen in a white crust across the knuckles. The window above the altar had gone from green to amber to the colour of weak tea, leaving the brazier the brightest thing in the chapel. She had just finished with Garran and stood up, a thin blue shine still clinging to the silver wire of the staff, a cooling coal.

Sylvanas stopped in the doorway with Cyndia at her shoulder as the smell came out at her. Without any change in her face she took the vinegar and the slops and the pears. Then she came in, as far as the end of Elsie's door-bed and no further, back to the wall and the door at her left hand, and looked round the room the way she would look over a wood: the cots, the people in them, the child in the corner, Tobin with his slate, Lyriel, Jaina.

Her left glove was grey, paler and finer than mud, worked into the seams between the fingers and smeared on the green of her cuff where she had wiped her hand and only made it worse. Her right hand was bare as always.

"Well, mage?" she said.

Jaina had it ready, in small words, fit for Brask: *It's the bread. Stop them eating the bread.* It was true, and it would do, and Sylvanas would nod and go and do it. She looked at the grey glove instead.

"It starts in the gut," she said instead. "In everybody newly ill it's only there, under the ribs. In the old man it's in everything now, and you can follow it back to the same place. So they're swallowing it. There's some in the water, but very little, and it doesn't sort them: three houses on one well, and it runs from the worst in the room to the least. Contact doesn't sort them either. The worst case in the village lives alone. What sorts them is bread." She heard herself going faster and went on. "The old man eats nothing else, morning and night. The cooper's house buys two loaves a day. The striker by the door ate his mother's oats all summer up on the hill, came down two days ago and ate bread, and he has the least of anybody. There's a woman at the end of the lane who eats her own oats and nothing from the store, drinks from the same well as the old man, and she's well. So it's one flour, and the more of it you eat, and the longer, the worse it goes." She stopped. "I can't get further than that from in here."

Then she waited.

"How sure?"

"Sure enough that I've stopped Tobin feeding it to them."

"Cyndia," Sylvanas said, "tell her."

Cyndia reported standing, weight even. "The granary keeper has bought his grain from the valley farms for twenty years. Three months ago a new man came through with a cart, from the west road, offering barley and flour cheaper than the farms by two coppers on the measure. The keeper bought. He's bought four loads from him since, the last a fortnight ago. The bakehouse takes all its flour from the granary. So do most of the houses on this side of the brook, and the top farm since the harvest. The keeper is pleased with the price."

Three months. Every morning of them, Ellen's loaf had gone up the lane to Garran's cottage to be torn up small and soaked in milk.

"What does he look like?" she said. "The new man."

"I asked," Sylvanas said. "Young. Civil. Bought the keeper a jug at the alehouse the first time he came, paying in good coin, asking after his wife's leg. The keeper liked him." *Cheaper than Mister Hollis.* The girl had said it into Neral's collar in the dark at Millhaven, about a man who had called at her house and set her mother and father quarrelling. Nobody at Millhaven could say anything about him except that he was new and cheaper.

Sylvanas had stood half in the doorway of the end house while the girl talked. Jaina looked at her, one new man selling cheap at Millhaven and here, and Sylvanas looked back, and neither of them said it.

Cheap, so the keeper bought it, so the baker bought it, so the cooper's wife with five mouths and no time bought two loaves a day of it, and the top farm with its barley rotted in the field.

The granary had no windows. Close to, it was a long blind wall of fieldstone going up into the dusk, the hoist beam over the high door a gallows arm, the door shut. A mill. There had been a mill on the quay at Boralus that went up the winter she was six. She had been in bed. She remembered the windows of her room coming in.

Through the sleeve. She had watched Sylvanas put an arrow through a running doe's heart at the holly bend. This one had gone through cloth.

Jaina set the heel of the staff on the boards and pushed.

She did it as she would have done it to a bucket of water. There wasn't any other way she knew. She had done this once before, on the road at the holly bend, to a wolf's hind legs. A person made a different sound.

The cold came back up the staff as it always came back, into her left hand, turning the linen hard as card. The jar.

The smell came up off the boards and hit her all at once, her eyes running with it. Pears gone brown. Under the sweetness something thin and sharp and wrong caught at the back of the nose, the smell of a struck flint.

It ran along the grain of the boards in thin dark fingers, toward the crack between two planks, toward the hole under the lifted board, toward the open sack with its tongue of flour; toward Sylvanas's boots.

Underneath this floor were the sacks on their trestles. Rows and rows of them.

It was the same thing that was in Garran's gut and the cooper's wife's, in the doe's blood and the well at Millhaven, the same late drag, the same heaviness hung on the back of every beat. And it was nothing like them. In the sick it was thin and smeared and everywhere. In here it was whole. It had been built.

She could feel where the parts of it met. One part to keep. One part to wait. One part that did something under the other two that she could not follow at all; she came at it from one side and slid off, came at it from another and slid off again, as her eye had slid off the Zandalari script on the cargo tags in her father's warehouse. A stack of spells, laid down one on another, planking on a hull, every seam caulked. And it had gone into a sack of flour and through a baker's oven and into a man's milk, and sat down in him and waited.

Oh, Light.

She knew what this was, as she knew where the reefs lay off Tiragarde, from the charts, from the warnings, from the ships that hadn't come home: the death-currents, the work that ties a thing to the body past the body's end. Every apprentice in Dalaran knew that much. They walked you past the locked shelf in your first year and gave you the signs so you'd know it if you met it. They never gave you the grammar; it stayed behind the grille.

This was written in the grammar. She could read every letter of it and not one word.

And it was beautiful. The thought was out before she could stop it, clean and hot. Whoever had made this had made it as Antonidas built a ward. She wanted to take it apart, to sit down on these boards with a candle and a week and find out what the third part did.

With Garran swallowing it in his sops. Lovely. Write that in the report.

"It's made," she said. Her voice came out hoarse. "Somebody very good made this."

Nobody answered her.

Light came up the stair, yellow and jumping, and a lot of boots on the planks, and Brask's voice at the bottom calling something she didn't catch. Then his head came up over the edge of the loft with a torch, Dickon's behind him with another, the light shaking over the rafters and the blue shell on the floor and the boy's face.

The boy's face was wet. He must have been crying since the frost went over him, and she hadn't heard a sound.

"Tam?" Brask said.

He came up the rest of the way and stood there with the torch, looking at the frost, the arrow in the post, the hole in the floor. His thumb was moving on the torch handle as it had moved on the spear.

"That's Tam," he said, to nobody. "He works the hoist for Joss. He came looking for work at the back end of summer. Joss took him on." And then, to the boy, in a different voice, a father's voice: "Tam. What's this? What've you done?"

Tam kept his face turned away.

"Cyndia, Lyriel, get him loose," Sylvanas said.

They went to him. Cyndia cut the sleeve away from the arrow with her belt knife. Jaina let the hold go, and his legs went out from under him as soon as she did; they'd been kneeling in her frost all this while, with no more feeling in them than her hand. Cyndia and Lyriel took him under the arms and hauled him up between them, his boots dragging and his head down.

Jaina got up too. It took the staff and the post and a while.

"Who made it?" she said.

He lifted his head. In the torchlight his eyes were very pale and very young, on her, then past her at the blue shell on the floor and the black stuff lying under it. He looked at it the way the little ones in the cathedral at Boralus used to look at the reliquary when the priest carried it down the aisle on a feast day, and she had been one of them, at the back, holding her nurse's hand: half hoping it would do something, and terrified that it would.

"I'm not to speak to you," he said.

"Who told you that?"

"I'm not to speak to you," he said again. "You don't know what you're keeping them from."

Brask made a sound in his throat.

Jaina opened her mouth and had nothing to put in it. Who made this, how, from what, who gave it to you, who gives it to them, what does the third part *do*—and he didn't know. He didn't know what was in the jar any more than she had known what was in the reliquary. He had been told to keep it, and to keep it dark, and he had kept it.

"He's a lookout," Sylvanas said, not moving from beside the lamp. "Someone gave him a jar and a floorboard and told him to watch. That's all he's for." She looked at Brask. "Captain. Is there somewhere here that locks?"

Brask stood with the torch hanging at his side, the light on the boy's bent head.

"Captain. Somewhere that locks."

"The cage," he said. "By the pound. At the bottom of Chapel Lane."

"Then we'll take him there. Cyndia, the stair. Nobody comes up. Nobody touches that." A tip of her head at the blue shell. "Mage. Will it hold?"

Jaina looked at it, then at her hand, the grey linen stiff and dark at the palm. "Until morning," she said. "If nobody puts a foot on it."

"Nobody will," Sylvanas said to Cyndia, who had already gone to stand at the head of the stair with her bow. She took the lamp up off the sack. "Down."

It was full dark in the lane.

Brask went first with the torch, the light going ahead of him over the cobbles and up the garden walls, showing her a cat on a gatepost, a child's wooden horse lying on its side in a doorway. Tam came behind him between Lyriel and Brask's man with the pitchfork, the man's hand clamped round the boy's arm above the elbow. Tam walked bow-kneed, drunk, his legs coming back to him, his boots squelching where her frost had melted into them, chin down on his chest and his collar.

Crying again. She couldn't hear it.

Overhead it was full dark. She had told Sylvanas full dark, or a little after, and up the hill behind her the wards in the chapel were going thin one after another, Garran's first. It was all she could do not to turn round and run up the lane. She told herself the cage was on the way, walking downhill, away from it.

Sylvanas walked behind the boy, near enough to touch him, the lamp swinging from her left hand, its little light lying on the back of Tam's neck, on the fair fuzz there and the bones under it.

The pound was a ring of drystone wall at the foot of the lane with a ewe inside, which got up as the torch came by and stood blinking. The cage stood against the wall, a little round house of fieldstone no bigger than a bread oven, with a stone dome for a roof and a door of iron bars. Brask found the key by feel, as he must have done a hundred times for a drunk on a fair night. The door went open with a scream of iron.

They put Tam in. There was a stone bench along the back wall and straw on the floor; Lyriel set him down on the bench, not ungently, and came out. Brask swung the door to, turned the key, and stepped back.

Tam sat on the bench with his hands between his knees.

Sylvanas gave the lamp to Lyriel and went up to the bars. She stopped a step back from them with her arms at her sides and stood there a while.

"Who gives you the jar?" she said.

Nothing.

"Who brings the flour? The young man with the cart. Does he bring the jar too?"

Nothing. Tam's shoulders moved in a shiver.

Jaina came up beside her into the lamplight. Close to, cold came off the bars into her face. She leaned in until her cheek was nearly against them and held up the staff, sending the blue light into the cage over the straw and the bench and the boy.

"Tam," she said. "Look at me. Slowly. Nobody is going to hurt you. Start with who gave you the jar, and we'll leave everything else for—"

He looked up.

There was something on his lip. White. She thought it was flour, at first; he'd had flour all over him. Then it bubbled.

His pale eyes were on her, his face perfectly calm, calmer than it had been all evening—and then it wasn't. His back arched. His heels went out from under him, scraping across the straw. He slid off the bench sideways onto the floor, his whole body drawing up tight as a bowstring, shaking, and shaking.

"Key," Sylvanas said.

Brask fumbled it. Sylvanas took the ring out of his hand and had the door open before he'd finished letting go of it, and was in, and down on her knees on the stone. She turned the boy on his side. She got her bare fingers into his mouth, between his teeth, and his teeth closed on them and she didn't take them out; she hooked whatever she could out of his cheek and threw it on the straw and went in again for more.

Jaina stood at the bars with her face against the iron. She couldn't have said when she'd put it there.

It didn't take long.

The shaking went out of him in waves, each one smaller, as the hum goes out of a struck bell. His heels stopped scraping. A long breath went out of him through the foam, a sigh, and then no breath after it. Sylvanas stayed where she was on her knees with her fingers in his mouth until there was nothing more to stay for. Then she took them out.

The torch hissed in Brask's fist. A dog barked somewhere up the lane and gave it up, and after that Jaina could hear the torch, and the blood in her own ears, and nothing else whatever.

She went in.

She knelt on the stone beside him because she didn't know what else to do and because the staff was something to do. He was warm, and she had not been ready for it. He lay on the cold floor with his cheek in the straw, warm through the smock where her knee touched his back, a little foam on his lip and his eyes half open. She put the staff to him. The light came back clean from his gut; he had never eaten one mouthful of what he kept under that floor. There was just a boy, stopped.

"Here," Sylvanas said.

She had the collar of his smock turned over in her fingers. Its corner was chewed through, a ragged wet hole in the hem no bigger than a fingernail. Inside the hem, when she pulled it open, there was a fold of waxed paper, empty, and a smear of something brownish on the cloth.

"He had it in his collar," Sylvanas said. "Sewn in. He had his chin on it all the way down the lane."

Crying, she'd thought.

Sylvanas sat back on her heels, wiping her fingers on her coat. Two of them had deep teeth marks. She looked at the boy.

"Somebody taught him that," she said. "They sewed it in for him and told him when."

Brask was standing in the door of the cage with the torch, holding it low so the light came from below and made his face strange. "He ate at my table," he said. "At the harvest supper. He sat next to my youngest and cut her meat for her."

Jaina looked down at Tam. *I'm not to speak to you.* And he hadn't. They had had him in the loft, frozen to the waist with an arrow through his sleeve, and nobody had told him what to say. Somebody had, months ago, with a needle and thread.

She got up off her knees, which took a while again.

Brask was talking. She came back into it in the middle. "—Joss up first thing, and we'll go through the loft in daylight, and I'll want to hear it from Joss what he knows about this lad, and then we'll see. We'll see what's to be done." He was looking at Tam, not at them, his thumb going up and down the torch handle. "In the morning. I'll have the whole of it in the morning."

In the morning.

Jaina looked up the lane. Somewhere up there in the dark the baker was asleep, and so was Ellen's mother. Before light, as they did it in every village in Lordaeron and every port in Kul Tiras, one of them would get up and light that oven and put the loaves in. And Ellen would carry them round in her basket with the cloth over, warm, to the cooper's house and the top farm and everybody, and to the chapel, to Tobin, for his sops.

Brask was not a fool. He had a dead boy on the floor of his cage that he'd fed at his own table, and strangers with bows, and a mage with a jar of something nobody could see. He wanted one night. She could explain the stack and the seams and the third part, in a voice that carried, and he'd stand there hearing one word in five and wanting his night all the more.

She turned to Sylvanas instead.

"Ranger-General." She said it low, as Sylvanas had spoken to him at the spear. "The baker lights his oven before light. If the captain waits for the morning, they'll bake with it. The whole village will have it for breakfast. I can ward a room. I can't ward a village."

Sylvanas turned to Brask in the door with his torch held low and his face gone heavy and strange from underneath, and stood, hands at her sides and nothing in them, as she had in the lane before noon.

"Captain," she said. "Your baker lights his oven before dawn?"

"Hob?" Brask lifted his head. "He does. He's at it by cockcrow, him and Nan."

"Then you have until cockcrow."

The rest he worked out himself, as he had worked out his own street. His thumb stopped.

"Tell me what to shut," he said.

Jaina told him. "The granary. The bakehouse. Tonight, now, before anyone's up. Nobody bakes, nobody sells, nobody carries a sack out of either. And every sack of that man's flour that's gone into any house, since the first load—"

"Every house?"

"I'm sorry, Captain. Every house."

"Burn it, then," he said. "All of it. In Joss's yard, where they can see it. I'll answer for the flour." He turned round in the door of the cage, raising his voice. "Piers. Get your brother and the Aldon boys and go round the doors on this side of the brook, every one, and anyone that's bought flour off Joss since harvest, you bring it out to the granary yard. Sacks, crocks, the lot." The man with the pitchfork went off up the lane at a run. "Dickon. Joss sent a cart down to the ford this morning with flour for the Hallams and the alehouse. Take the ponies and go after it and turn it round, and don't let anybody eat off it. Go on."

Dickon went.

Brask stood in the door a moment longer, looking at the boy. Then he said, quite quietly, "Cover his face, somebody," and went off down toward the granary with the torch, and the light went with him.

Lyriel had a spare cloak rolled behind her shoulders. She shook it out to lay it over Tam, over his face and his chewed collar, tucking the edge under before she stood up.

"I have to go up," Jaina said. "The wards."

"Lyriel. With her," Sylvanas said, and went after Brask without waiting to see them go.

Jaina went up Chapel Lane faster than she'd gone up anything in her life, her skirts in her dead hand and Lyriel's hand at her back the whole way. Her lungs were raw by the yew when she shouldered the chapel door open.

Tobin was on a stool by Garran's bed, holding a candle, his face grey. The ward over the old man was a rag, a cobweb with the sun through it, a few threads and a hole.

She went down on her knees on the flags and put the staff to him, feeding it. Then all the others, round the room. Every one of them had thinned, not as far as his, while she had been down in the dark with a jar and a boy. Elsie's was holding by its corners. The cooper's wife's was going at the middle, a worn sheet. She mended them, and went round again.

Somewhere in the rounds the smell came in under the door and through the cracks of the shutters. Smoke, heavy and sour, with a scorch in it, bread left in the oven while somebody stood talking at the door. She went to the window over the altar and wiped the clear pane with the heel of her bad hand and looked down.

The granary yard was full of fire. A heap of something burned in the middle of it with a smoky red flame, and men went to it and away from it with sacks on their shoulders, black shapes against the glow, throwing the sacks in. Every sack went in with a puff of sparks that went straight up. Brask, by the shape of him, stood at the yard gate with a slate or a board, putting his head down over it every time a man came in with a sack.

The bakehouse chimney stood up black and cold against the glow beside it, for the first time all day.

She went back to Garran.

The wards only bought time. She sat back on her heels between rounds with the staff across her knees, making herself say it plainly, because Antonidas would have. They took nothing out of anybody. They held the door, so that nothing more could come in and nothing that was in could get any further. What was in was in, and the body would do the rest or it wouldn't. Nobody had seen this before; or if somebody had, they'd written it on the far side of the grille.

The bread was burning down there in a yard. The door was being shut from the other side, and it had taken Sylvanas and a man with a torch and a heap of burning sacks to shut it, and not one single thing she knew. So she held the door from this side.

She couldn't feel a forehead with her hands, so she did it as her nurse had done it, a long time ago, in a house on a hill over the sea, bending to lay her cheek against them. Elsie's was hot and dry. The boy's was hot. Wenna Dunmore's was hot and wet, and Jaina stayed bent over her longer than she meant to, trying to tell whether wet was better or worse, not knowing. Then Garran's, a stove to lean against.

The boy in the cage. She'd been keeping him off. He came anyway, between Wenna and Garran, with the foam on his lip.

If she'd been quicker with the shell over that spill and come back to him, would she have seen it? A fold of paper in a collar? She'd been looking straight at him in the loft and seen a boy, crying. If she'd frozen him to the neck instead of the waist—

His jaw. She could have frozen his jaw, the frost going up over his mouth and his nose, and the noise he would have made. Would she have done it, if she'd known? She had hated doing it to a wolf. And he'd have thawed in the end and sat in the cage with his chin on his collar, saying *I'm not to speak to you*, waiting. He'd been waiting for months.

Slower. She was doing the sums backward from the answer she wanted, that it was her fault, because if it was her fault there was something she could have done.

Garran's ward was thinning. She fed it.

Some time in the deep of the night Lyriel came and sat down on the flags beside her and took her left hand without asking. The linen across the palm had gone black-red and stiff. When Lyriel cut it off with her knife there was a split in the heel of the hand, an overripe plum, shiny with the cold.

"Hold still," Lyriel said. "You've bled through." She washed it with water from Tobin's can, Jaina hissing through her teeth, Lyriel not stopping. Clean linen, round and round, over the thumb, wrist under, tail through, and Lyriel bit it down. "Don't let Tobin see that. He'll want to bleed you as well."

Jaina looked at the knot.

"I can tie that now," she said.

"Not on your own hand, you can't. Not yet." Lyriel got up. "Tomorrow."

Some time after that Cyndia came in with the cold of the night on her cloak and a bundle done up in a cloth. She reported to the room in general, as she would have reported to Sylvanas. "Brask's man took my place on the stair. The woman at the end of Cooper's Lane was on her step watching the fire. I asked her for oats. She gave me these. Her own oats, from her own field. She wants the cloth back."

Mother Haw. Jaina could have laughed. Tobin broke the oatcakes up small into a bowl, poured milk over them, and carried them to Garran.

Sylvanas came once, very late, a shape in the doorway with the glow of the yard behind her and the smell of scorched flour and smoke coming off her cloak. She stayed on the threshold, her left hand on the doorframe, and looked round the room.

"It's burned," she said. "All of the granary. All of the bakehouse."

"The houses?"

"Most of them. Brask is still going round. The cart's not back."

"And the loft?"

"His sacks came down round your ice, one at a time, with Brask's man calling the steps. Nobody's put a foot on it."

"Thank you, Ranger-General."

Sylvanas stayed a moment longer on the threshold. Then she went, and the doorway was dark again.

Jaina bent to lay her cheek against Elsie's forehead. Hot. Somewhere in the grey before dawn the shell on the loft floor would thin and let go, with that black skin lying loose under it and one of Brask's men sitting on the stair below.

It rained a little before dawn, soft on the chapel roof and then steadier, the smell of the yard fire going out of the air under it. When the window over the altar went grey she was on her knees by Elsie again.

The girl was wet through, her hair stuck flat to her forehead and the striped blanket dark at the neck with it. She was awake, blinking at the ceiling.

"I'm all wet," she said, in a cross little voice. "Is there water?"

Jaina bent to lay her cheek against the girl's forehead and held it there.

Cool. Damp and cool, the outside of a jug.

She sat back on her heels. Elsie looked back at her, puzzled. "Is there?" she said again, and Lyriel was already coming across from the brazier with Tobin's can.

The cooper's boy was awake too, sitting up on his pallet with his lopsided bandage, wanting to know if there was any more of the oat stuff and why couldn't he have bread, he always had bread. Col was on his feet by the door, holding his shirt shut. He was perfectly well, he said, and he was starving, and he was going home. Wenna Dunmore slept, her husband on the floor beside her with one hand on her blanket. Her forehead was only warm, a sleeper's warmth.

Garran was the same. His breath still caught at the top, and caught, and caught, his skin dry and hot as a stove, and under the crystal the brown was still out along his arms and up his neck. It was no further than it had been in the deep of the night.

The rest were holding. The early ones were more than holding. She went and put her cheek to Elsie's forehead again.

Still cool.

"Jaina." Lyriel was watching her from the brazier. "She's the same as she was the last time. And the time before."

"I know."

The wards would hold a while on their own now, and she had nothing left to put in them. She was sitting on the bench under the window with the staff across her knees, unable to remember sitting down. The hum went out of her hands slowly, first the right, then the left, and as it went the left woke up behind it in pins and then in burning, the long split in the heel of the palm throbbing under Lyriel's linen with every beat of her heart.

Garran's bandage had slipped again. The pad had come off the cut at his elbow in the night, the strip hanging loose round his arm.

She knelt by the truckle bed and put the staff in the crook of her dead arm and got the pad back on with her left hand, the fingers poking out of the new wrapping just far enough to do it. Over the thumb. Wrist under, and the loop held at the root of her thumb where she'd learned to put it. Tail through, clean. She took it in her teeth and pulled her head back, and the knot went down flat and small on the inside of his arm, and held.

It was a silly thing to want. A rag on an old man's arm, after a night like that. She looked at it anyway while it went on holding.

Lyriel was grinning at her from the brazier. She tipped her head at the knot, then toward the door where Cyndia had stood in the night. Cyndia wasn't there. Lyriel went on grinning anyway.

Jaina went out into the yard.

The roofs below the chapel were wet and dark and shining, the light coming up pale yellow behind the hill. Hearth smoke went up thin and grey in the still air. The bakehouse sent none, and the village lay under the morning without the brown haze it had worn when she rode in, smaller without it, and barer.

In the granary yard a black heap steamed, flattened by the rain, a cart beside it with its shafts down. The cart from the ford, then. Somebody was still forking sacks off it onto the heap, somebody else trying to light it again.

Millhaven had had smoke in its chimneys too, once. A cradle with the blanket tucked in. Sixty pairs of feet in a cow way. None of that was any different this morning because Elsie was cool. She tried to set the one against the other anyway, standing there in the wet, the cool forehead against the tucked blanket, and it wouldn't come out either way she ran it.

The horses were saddled under the yew, Cyndia going round the girths without looking up.

Sylvanas was standing by her mare's head with Brask. He had a thick book under his arm, a little horn of ink on a cord round his neck and a pen stuck behind his ear. His face was grey with the night, soot on it and on his jack and on the backs of his big hands.

"Dickon caught the cart at the ford," he said, as Jaina came up. "Turned it round. The Hallams hadn't had theirs off it yet. The alehouse had had two sacks. I've sent down. They'll bring it up." He jerked his head at the heap. "And the boards from the loft. Your ice was going thin at first light, so we had them up with a bar and carried them down flat between two of us, faces turned away. They went on the heap with the rest, ice and all."

So the third part had gone on the heap too, whatever it did. It was the right thing, and she would have told him to do it, and some small low part of her minded anyway.

"Thank you, Captain," Jaina said.

Sylvanas put her hand into her coat and took out something folded. She unfolded it and held it out past Brask, to Jaina.

It was a square of sacking, cut neat with a knife from the side of a sack, pale and clean. There was a mark on it in black, stencilled: a sheaf of wheat in a ring, and under the ring, in square letters, a word.

ANDORHAL.

"From the loft," Sylvanas said. "His sacks. I cut it before they went in the fire."

"Andorhal," Brask said, staring at the square of sacking. "That's the big granaries. Down the grain road, south and west, past the ford. Three days, with a cart. Everything in the south goes through Andorhal, one way or another. My barley goes through Andorhal."

The young man with the cart had come from the west road. Cyndia had said so at the chapel. Jaina took the sacking in her left hand and held it, the clean pale weave smelling of sacking.

"Then that's where his cart came from," she said. And then, because it was true: "Or where he wanted us to think it came from."

Sylvanas took the sacking back. She folded it and put it away in her coat. "Either way," she said, "it's the road he drove."

Brask had got the book open against his chest. He took the pen from behind his ear and pulled the stopper out of the horn with his teeth.

"I'll want your names," he said round the stopper. He spat it into his hand. "All of you. If anyone comes asking what happened here, I want to tell it right."

He turned to a clean page and wrote slowly, tongue in the corner of his mouth, saying each word aloud as he made it, as children say their letters.

"The mage," he said. "Jaina." Scratch. "Proudmoore. Spell that for me."

Jaina spelled it. He wrote it, then looked up at Sylvanas, the pen hanging over the page.

"Sylvanas Windrunner," Sylvanas said. She spelled it for him, flat and clear, as she might have given him the bearing of a ford. Brask wrote it letter by letter after her and said it back carefully, getting the middle wrong and then right.

Then Cyndia, from the far side of her horse, without looking up from the girth. Then Lyriel, who spelled hers and then said, "And you can put that one of us ties a good knot, Captain, if there's room on the line," glancing at Cyndia, who didn't look round.

Brask didn't write that.

He blew on the page and shut the book on it, holding it shut against his chest with both hands, as he had held the spear in the lane.

"Right," he said.

He went off down the lane toward the granary yard, where the heap had begun to smoke again.

"Mage," Sylvanas said. "Up."

Lyriel was at her stirrup. Jaina got up badly, as always, a foot in Lyriel's cupped hands and her skirts caught under her; Lyriel set her straight in the saddle and went to her own horse. The mare shifted under her. Jaina sat there shaking all over, as she'd shaken after her first night watch on her father's deck, and put her bandaged hand flat on the mare's warm neck until it stopped.

They went down through the village in the same order they had come up it, Sylvanas in front and Cyndia on the left and Lyriel on the right and Jaina in the middle, and two empty places at the back. People were at their doors. The egg woman was setting out her trestle. Nobody called a price. The woman in the blue apron put her hand up, flat, and held it there a moment before she took it down again. They went past the bakehouse with its oven cold and its door shut, and the baker standing in the yard next door with a fork in his hands, watching the heap smoke, and on past the pound and the cage. Jaina didn't look.

At the bottom of the village, past the brook, the road split. One way went back north, toward Millhaven. The other went off south and west between its hedges, broad and flat and rutted deep by every loaded cart that had ever come up it.

The staff shifted in the crook of her arm, all on its own, leaning toward the ruts.

Sylvanas turned her mare onto the grain road without a word. They fell in behind her, south and west, after the young man with the cart.
