# Chapter 1: The Border

Sylvanas had lain in the bracken long enough for the damp to soak through her leathers at both knees and one elbow, long enough for the jays to forget her and start quarrelling again in the branches overhead. Below her the slope fell away into a stand of ferns that ran thick and waist-high to a stream she could hear and not see. Three of the Amani were dead on the ridge at her back. The fourth was somewhere in those ferns. She knew it because the ferns forty yards down had gone still, and nothing else in the hollow had any reason to keep that still on a morning with wind in it.

Across the hollow, Lor'themar sat his horse at the tree line with the patrol strung out behind him in a loose half-circle, every rider where she had put them. He had his bow across the saddle and an arrow on the string, and he wasn't drawing. It was her shot. He would take it only if she missed, and then she'd hear about it all the way back to the city.

She did not intend to miss.

The troll would go for the stream. They always went for water when a fight turned on them, as if running water had ever stopped an arrow, and there were two ways down to it from where he lay: the open slope, which was suicide, or the gap between the leaning ash and the boulder, which was only stupid. Sylvanas drew halfway and held there, the string resting against her lips, and watched the gap.

He broke cover from the ash side, low and fast, tusks first, one throwing axe already back over his shoulder in case someone stood up in front of him. Big for an axethrower. He came out of the ferns in a spray of wet fronds, and his left foot landed on a root slick with moss, and for half a stride all that weight went the wrong way.

She drew the rest of the way and loosed into the half-stride.

The arrow went in under the arm with a sound like a boot dropped in mud. He kept running for three more steps, because they did, and then his legs went one way and the rest of him another and he folded into the ferns at the edge of the stream and thrashed there, and stopped.

"Down," she called across the hollow, loud enough that the jays went silent again. "That's the last."

The smell reached her before she stood: troll blood, rank and coppery and wrong, cutting through the sweetness that Eversong air always carried in the warm months, resin and sun-warmed blossom and something green that had never had a name because no elf had ever needed to name it. She got up and her knees told her exactly how long she'd been lying on them. She ignored that and went down the slope, ferns crushing under her boots and springing back behind her, the stalks bleeding a sharp green smell into the blood smell.

By the time she reached him, the birds had started again. A finch first, close by, and then the jays, and then something further off she didn't bother to name. That was how you knew a hollow was empty. The birds said so before your own scouts did.

The troll lay on his side with his eyes half open, tusks streaked with stream silt. She put her boot on his axe hand before she trusted it, then drew her knife and made sure, quick and under the jaw, the way she'd been taught before she was tall enough to string a longbow. She wiped the blade on a fern frond, both sides, and then on a second frond because the first had smeared it.

Hoofbeats behind her, unhurried. Lor'themar walked his horse down through the ferns and stopped a length away, out of the smell, and looked at the body and then at her.

"Clean," he said.

"He slipped."

"He slipped into your arrow. I watched him do it." He leaned on the saddle horn. "That's the sweep, then. Four on the ridge path, four down. I'll have them burn these before something with a nose finds them."

"Do."

He turned in the saddle and gave the order in two words, and the patrol came down out of the tree line by pairs, riders swinging off to water their horses at the stream, upstream of the troll. Someone laughed at something. Someone else was already complaining about the state of his boots. Sylvanas pulled her arrow out of the troll, looked at the head, decided it was worth keeping and cleaned that too.

It was barely past the middle of the morning. The sun came down through the canopy in long slants that turned the ferns gold at the tips, and the stream ran bright over its stones, and somewhere upslope a woodpecker started hammering as though he'd been waiting for the noise to stop. If they turned for home now, they would make Silvermoon by dusk. She would sleep in her own bed. And in the morning she would put on the formal greens and sit on a bench in the Convocation chamber while men who had never drawn a bow argued about what bows cost.

She slid the arrow back into her quiver.

"The stones on the southern track want looking at," she said.

Lor'themar did not answer right away. He let his horse drop its head to crop at a fern, and he watched her with the particular patience of a man who had served under her long enough to know when she'd decided something before she said it.

"The stones," he said.

"The outer ring's just south of us. When did anyone last ride that stretch who wasn't a courier?"

"The magisters' wardens rode it in the spring. I read the report. You read the report. It said stable." He scratched his jaw. "It said stable in three different hands."

"Then it won't take long."

"No," he agreed. "Nor will the Convocation."

Stalling, Ranger-General. He knew it, and he was going to let her do it anyway and enjoy every hour of it, which was worse than if he'd argued. She swung up onto her mare without looking at him. The mare shifted under her, settling her weight the way she always did after standing, a little sidestep and a blown breath, and Sylvanas sat it and took up the reins and turned south.

"Six riders with me," she said, loud enough for the stream. "The rest finish here and follow."

They found the first stone a little before noon, where the track bent around a shoulder of rock and dropped toward a dry creek bed. It stood at the bend the way they all stood, taller than a mounted rider, grey and narrow and older than the track itself, with the runes cut deep down the southern face and filled with the faint gold that never quite looked like paint. Moss had grown up over its shoulder on the shaded side, thick and dark as a pelt. Nobody had scraped it. Nobody had needed to. A ward-stone did not care about moss.

She heard it before she was off the horse. Everyone did, if they knew to listen: a low sound below sound, felt more in the teeth than the ear, the hum of the outer ring running stone to stone along the border like a long held note. Most elves stopped hearing it in childhood, the way they stopped hearing their own heartbeat.

She dismounted and handed the reins to the nearest rider and walked up to the stone. Behind her, Lor'themar stayed in the saddle. She could feel him watching.

She pulled off her right glove and laid her bare palm flat against the rune face.

A sound stone was warm. Not sun-warm; the warmth came from inside and stayed the same whether it was noon or midnight, and the hum came through it steady and even, the way a sound horse's flank felt under your hand after a long trot, a working thing doing its work. She had put her palm to stones like this since she was a girl, riding the line behind her mother's rangers, and she had never once felt one that wasn't exactly that.

This one was warm. The hum came through. But it came through wrong, a hitch in it, as though the stone were favouring a leg; the beat came and then came again a fraction late, and then caught itself, and then went late again. And under the warmth, deep under it, where her palm could only just reach, there was cold. Not cold like stone in the shade. Cold like a river rock turned over, sun-warm on top and the underside still holding the water.

She kept her hand there. The moss brushed her knuckles, damp and soft.

Was that real? Or had she ridden half a morning south looking for a reason to stay out, and found one because she wanted one. That was the kind of excuse she'd have flayed a ranger for. She pressed harder, as though that would help, and the hum hitched again under the heel of her hand, and the cold sat there under it like something that had always been there and she'd only now been rude enough to notice.

No. It was real. She knew stones.

And what did she call it, then? Off. Late. Cold. A magister would have a word for it, six words, a treatise, and none of those magisters had ever put a bare hand on a ward-stone in their lives. They sent wardens with lenses and clerks with ledgers, and the clerks wrote stable, and the magisters signed it, and the Convocation read the signatures and moved on to the next thing. Damn them. And damn her too, for standing here wishing she had the word.

"Well?" Lor'themar said.

She took her hand away. The cold went with it, a little, and stayed in the palm.

"It's off."

"Off how?"

"Off." She pulled her glove back on, flexing the hand inside it. "The beat's uneven. There's cold under it."

He looked at the stone, then at her. He had a ranger's face for a moment and not a courtier's, and he leaned out of the saddle and put his own bare hand to the stone, briefly, the way you'd test a kettle.

"Feels like a stone to me," he said.

"You never had the hand for it."

"I never had the patience for it. There's a difference." He straightened. "If it's wrong, it's the magisters' work. Write it up. I'll carry it in with the rest. They'll send someone in a fortnight with a lens and a clerk."

"And the clerk will write stable."

"Very likely." He gathered his reins. "Shall I tell the chamber the Ranger-General has gone into stone-reading? They'll want to fund it. Half of them would rather pay you to stand in a field touching rocks than hear you talk about arrows."

She mounted. The mare stood steady for it, ears forward, and the six riders behind them sat their horses with the ease of people who had done this every day of their lives, reins loose, the horses shifting their weight from foot to foot under riders they knew. Sylvanas looked at the stone once more. Moss on the shoulder. Gold in the runes. Nothing a person riding by would ever look at twice.

She turned the mare along the line, the long way round toward home, and rode on.

The track ran east with the ward line for a while, close enough that every few hundred yards another stone stood up out of the undergrowth at the roadside, and the hum of the ring came up through the ground into the horses' strides, low and constant, so that after a time she stopped hearing it and only felt it, the way you felt a river you were riding beside. The afternoon light came through the canopy amber and thick, the colour of old honey, laying long bars across the road that the horses walked through one after another. Somebody behind her was humming along with the ring without knowing it. Somebody always did.

Her right palm was still cold. She closed the hand on the reins and opened it again inside the glove.

"Tomorrow," Lor'themar said, drawing level with her, "the Convocation sits on the patrol budget. I mention it only because you've gone so quiet I thought you might have forgotten."

"I hadn't."

"One of the magisters' nephews wants to know why the southern posts need new bowstaves when the old ones haven't broken. I'm told he's prepared figures."

"Then he can come south and break one."

"I'll tell him you said so. He'll take it as an invitation and bring a clerk." Lor'themar shifted in the saddle, easing a knee. "They're going to ask what the Pass needs a full company for. Trolls are east. Humans are polite. The wards are stable, it says so in three hands. What do I tell them?"

There it was. The opening. She could tell him about the stone, and he would carry it into the chamber and lay it in front of a row of magisters, and they would ask her what she meant by cold, and she would say cold, and they would look at each other.

Even Cyndia, sitting at the Pass with her full company and her careful reports, didn't expect anything from the south but merchants with the wrong papers and the occasional human lordling who'd taken a wrong turn hunting. South of the wards was Lordaeron's business, Lordaeron's roads, Lordaeron's problems. None of it was hers, unless it came over her line or touched her stones or her riders. And it hadn't. One stone with a limp in it wasn't an army.

"Tell them the trolls can count," she said, "and they'll count our bows before they count our coin."

"That's good. I'll use that." He glanced at her sideways. "You'll be there to say it yourself, of course."

"Of course."

He laughed, quietly, and let it go. That was the thing about Lor'themar; you could be short with him all day and it slid off him like rain off oiled leather, and he never once slowed the horse down to make you pay for it. She wasn't going to tell him that, either.

She rubbed her thumb across the cold place in her palm and said nothing about the stone, and the road went on east under the amber light, and the hum ran on under the hooves.

She heard the rider before anyone else did. A single horse, south and behind them, coming up the track hard, the hoofbeats too fast and too even for anyone who meant to stop politely. She held up a fist and the patrol drew in around her without a word, and Lor'themar pulled his bow off the saddle. Her own hand went to her string.

Then she knew the gait. Long-legged, a slight drag on the near hind. A Farstrider horse, and one that had been ridden too far.

He came round the bend at a dead gallop, and when he saw them he hauled up so hard the horse nearly sat down. The animal was dark with sweat from neck to flank, lather thick and white where the reins had rubbed and between the hind legs, foam hanging off the bit in ropes. It stood with its legs splayed and its sides going like a bellows. The rider was young, green cloak grey with road dust, and he saw her and tried to salute and breathe at the same time and managed neither.

"Ranger-General." He dragged in air. "From the Pass. Ranger Cyndia sends—"

"Skip the compliments. What."

"Intruder. Outer ring, before dawn." He swallowed. "Human. A woman. Mage."

The patrol went quiet around her. Sylvanas didn't move.

"Through the ring?"

"Through it. Nobody felt her cross." He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. "Night watch found her at first light. She was at a stone, Ranger-General. Down off the high road. Studying it. Had her hands on it, and papers spread out on the ground around her, and she didn't run when they came up on her."

"Papers. Credentials?"

"None. No seal, no escort, no letter. Nothing from Dalaran, nothing from the crown. She came quiet." He took another breath, and it shook. "Ranger Cyndia has her at the post. She asked me to tell you what the human said when they took her. Exactly what she said."

"Then tell me."

The runner looked at her straight for the first time.

"She said the leylines are wrong."

Nobody laughed. Behind her, one of the horses blew and shook its head, rattling the bit, and its rider hushed it.

Sylvanas had closed her right hand into a fist without meaning to. The cold was still there in the middle of it, under the glove, exactly where the stone had put it.

"Those were her words?"

"Those were her words, Ranger-General. Ranger Cyndia had me say them back to her twice."

Lor'themar lowered his bow across his knees. He didn't say anything. He was looking at her hand.

A human. A mage, through her ring in the dark without so much as a ripple, crouched at one of her stones with her papers out. A spy, then. Kirin Tor, sent to count the wards and see what they were worth. Then why stand there and let the rangers take her? Why say anything at all? What spy, caught red-handed at a ward-stone, looks up at a Farstrider's drawn bow and talks about leylines? One who wants to be caught. No, that was nonsense. One who's mad, then. Possibly. Or one who put her hand on a stone and felt what Sylvanas had felt at noon.

Sun's sake.

She was glad. That was the worst of it. She was sitting her horse on a forest road with a stranger inside her border and something wrong with her stones, and some low part of her had already worked out that she would not be sitting on a bench in the Convocation chamber tomorrow, and it was glad. Any ranger of hers caught grinning over a breach would be mucking stalls till midsummer.

"Lor'themar."

"I know." He was already sliding the bow back into its loops. "I'll take the patrol north. The chamber can have me instead. They like me better anyway; I don't make them look at the map."

"Tell them the Pass needs its full company."

"I'll tell them you were called off by something with teeth." He met her eyes, and the joke went out of his face for a moment. "Sylvanas. Send word when you know what she is."

"When I know."

She did not thank him. He didn't look as though he'd expected it. He turned his horse and raised his arm, and the patrol peeled off after him in pairs, back up the track toward the fork where the old road went north to Silvermoon. She watched them go for as long as it took the last rider to clear the bend. Then she looked at the runner's horse, still blowing, still splayed.

"Can he go on?"

"He'll go, Ranger-General."

"Then he goes. Keep up."

They split at the fork. Behind her, the northern road ran soft and shaded under the trees, needles and leaf mould deep enough to muffle a cavalry column, and she could hear the patrol on it for a while and then couldn't. Ahead, the southern track turned to packed earth and then to stone, climbing out of the woods in long switchbacks, and the mare's hooves rang on it. The air changed as they climbed. The sweetness went out of it first, and then the warmth, until the wind coming down off the heights had snow somewhere in it and smelled of nothing but rock and distance.

The stones came up along the roadside at a steady pace, one and then the next and then the next, and because she was riding fast now the hum of each one rose under the hooves and fell away behind her before the next one began, quicker and quicker, like a pulse she was running to catch. She tried to hear a limp in them. She couldn't, not at a gallop. She didn't know if that was because there was none.

She rubbed her right palm against her thigh, hard, through the glove. It didn't help. She did it again a mile on.

The Pass post came into sight at the end of the light, a squat dark tower against a sky gone violet over the peaks, lamps already lit in the gate, the outer stones standing in their line across the saddle of the pass below it, grey on grey. The gate rangers saw her colours and dragged the bar back before she had to call for it. She rode through at a canter, the runner's horse labouring in behind her, and was out of the saddle before the mare had properly stopped.

"Where's Cyndia?" Her voice went across the yard and every head in it turned. "Get her out here. I want her report, and I want it now."

Cyndia came out of the tower door while the last of it was still ringing off the yard walls, settling her quiver strap on her shoulder as she walked, and stopped a pace short of Sylvanas with her weight even on both feet and her eyes straight on. She had been up since the night watch. It showed in the grey under her eyes and in the way she held her shoulders a little too square, as if she'd decided hours ago that if she let them drop she wouldn't get them back up, and it showed nowhere at all in her voice.

"Ranger-General."

"Inside. Report as we go."

The guardroom at the Pass was built for winter. The walls were a yard thick, the stones sweating faintly where the hearth's warmth met the cold coming through them, and ward-lamps burned in iron brackets every few paces along both walls so that no corner of the room held a shadow worth the name. The light was white with blue at the edges, steady as noon, and after the long dusk on the switchbacks it hit Sylvanas's eyes like a slap. She narrowed them and kept walking. Her thighs had gone stiff in the last hour of the climb, and they told her so on the step down from the door; she let them.

Four rangers stood along the far wall. That was two more than the hour called for, and not one of them was sitting, though there were benches enough and every one of them looked as though they'd have traded a month's pay to use one. They straightened as she came in—tired eyes, hands not far from their bows, the kind of alert that had been held too long and gone brittle at the edges. On the table someone's supper sat abandoned in a wooden bowl, a skin gone over the stew and a spoon standing up in it on its own.

"She came through the outer ring before dawn," Cyndia said, falling in at her shoulder. "Nobody on the wall felt her cross. The night watch found her at first light on the second ring, at the stone below the high road, the one with the split cap. She had both hands on it and her papers on the ground round her feet, weighted down with stones off the verge." She gave it in order, plainly, the way she'd read back sign on a trail. "The watch put an arrow on the string and told her to step away from the stone. She stepped away and showed her hands. Then she asked to stay."

"Asked."

"Argued, Ranger-General. She said she needed a day with that stone and the next ones along the ring. She said the stones were sick from the inside, and that our wardens would read them sound because they only read the outsides." Cyndia's left hand had come to rest near her quiver while she talked; it stayed there. "She said it twice. When the watch told her she'd be bound and carried if she didn't walk, she asked whether she could be bound at the stone."

Sick from the inside.

Sylvanas found her right hand closed again and opened it. The cold sat in the middle of the palm where it had sat all afternoon, small and patient, like a coin she couldn't put down.

A spy ran. Any spy worth what he was paid would have had the dark on his side and a road at his back, and he'd have been halfway to Lordaeron by the time the night watch found a cold camp and a scuff in the dirt. This one had sat down in front of a drawn bow and asked to be tied to the stone. Arrogance, then. The Kirin Tor bred it the way Eversong bred horses, and they all thought a border was a line on somebody else's map. Or she'd been frightened half out of her wits and talking to keep her teeth still—except frightened people ran too, and faster. Or she was right. Sylvanas didn't much care for that one either.

"Did she fight?"

"No. She talked the whole way up. About the stones." Cyndia paused, the only pause she'd taken. "I don't think she minded that we had her, Ranger-General. I think she minded that we took her off the stone."

"Her gear."

"Lockdown rack. Staff, satchel, the papers. The staff's under a damper."

"Show me. Then her."

The rack stood in a stone alcove off the passage to the cells, where the ward-light was harder still and the air smelled of lamp oil and cold iron. It was a plain frame bolted to the wall, with a dampening plate set into the back of it—a slab of grey slate carved in close lines that drank the light off anything laid near it, so that standing in front of it felt like standing at the lip of a well. The staff lay across two hooks. Pale wood, turned and polished, silver wire wound up the grip in a pattern too even for a human hand to have managed without some help, and at the head a crystal the size of a hen's egg. Under the damper it had gone dim, a dull violet, like an ember banked for the night. Now and then the colour moved in it, slowly, and settled.

The satchel had been turned out onto the shelf below and laid in rows, the way Cyndia laid out everything. A tin of ink with the lid screwed down tight. Pens wrapped in oilcloth. A field-book swollen with damp, the pages cockled. Two pairs of stockings, darned at the heels, badly. A heel of bread gone hard as bark. A small brass compass. Cyndia's file lay open at the end of the shelf with the Farstrider seal on its cover, and on the open page someone had drawn a staff in careful charcoal.

And the map.

Somebody had spread it flat and pinned the corners with lead weights. It was human paper, thick and soft and furred at the edges, folded so many times the creases had gone white, and it was drawn by hand. Sylvanas leaned over it and the lamp threw her shadow across half the sheet, and she shifted until it didn't.

The ward-stones were there. Not every one of them, but more than any human had a right to know—each a small neat dot, strung along a line that followed the outer ring and then the second, curving with the border the way the border curved. Some of the dots had been circled. Some had been circled and then circled again, the second ring darker than the first, pressed hard enough to dent the paper. And from the bottom edge of the sheet, from the south, from Lordaeron, thin straight lines ran up across the paper as if someone had laid a rule on it, each one ending at a circled stone. In the lower corner the ink had smeared into a long grey thumbprint, where somebody had held the sheet down against the wind with one hand and gone on drawing with the other.

A surveyor marked every stone; a surveyor was paid by the stone. A raider marked the ones he meant to break and drew the roads in to them. These had roads. Then why carry it over the border in her satchel and spread it on the verge for the first ranger along to read? Because she was a fool. Because she wasn't. Sylvanas had no idea which, and the not knowing sat in her stomach like bad water.

She found the high road. She found the Pass, a small square with a flag drawn on it, which was almost charming. She followed the outer ring east along the southern track, past the places she'd ridden that afternoon, and came to where the track bent round a shoulder of rock and dropped toward a dry creek bed. Whoever had drawn it had taken the trouble to put in the creek, a wavering line of dashes. There was a dot at the bend. There was a ring of ink around the dot.

Sylvanas put her finger beside it. Not on it. Under the glove her palm went cold, or she only thought it did, and that was the trouble now; she couldn't tell.

At noon she'd stood there with her hand flat on that stone and the moss against her knuckles. And somewhere south of here, days ago or weeks, by lamplight in some tower room, a human had drawn a circle round it.

How? You didn't circle a stone from Lordaeron. You came and put your hand on it, and nobody felt you cross. Or somebody told you which one to circle, and that was worse. Or—

"Kirin Tor ink," Cyndia said at her side. She touched the edge of the map where the lines ran thickest. "It dries violet at the edges. Ours dries black. The staff matches the one in our file, the wire and the crystal both." She turned the file a quarter round on the shelf so the charcoal staff faced Sylvanas. "It's Jaina Proudmoore, Ranger-General. Antonidas's apprentice."

Sylvanas looked at the drawing, and then at the staff on its hooks, and the dim crystal moved its colour and went still again.

She knew the file. She had read it last winter at her own desk with a cup going cold at her elbow, and she'd read it the way she read every file on the human mages near her border: as a list of people who would one day be a nuisance. Antonidas's apprentice. Gifted beyond her years, a Farstrider had written in a flat hand that didn't hand out words like gifted. A lord admiral's daughter. The archmage's favourite, the one he brought to council. A name worth watching.

Well. She'd watched it walk into her post.

Damn the old man. Damn Dalaran and every tower in it.

"Where is she?"

"End of the passage. Second door."

She'd built the prisoner on the walk from the rack, plank by plank, out of the file and the ink and the polished silver wire. Good violet wool with gold at the hem. Hair done up. Clean hands folded. A face arranged into patient courtesy, the way the magisters arranged theirs in chamber when they meant to talk you into the floor, and somewhere about her a letter with a seal on it, held back until the moment it would do the most harm. A nuisance with a pedigree. A morning lost to apologies, an escort to the border, and a sharp letter north from Silvermoon that would take a month to answer itself.

The ranger on the second door saw her coming and stood off it. Two more filled the passage behind, and Cyndia's step stopped at her shoulder.

"Open it."

The bar came up. The door swung in on a room barely wider than itself: stone on four sides and overhead, a ward-lamp in a bracket throwing the same hard white-blue light as the guardroom, one wooden chair, a bucket. To the right of the door three iron pegs stood out of the wall at shoulder height, where a prisoner's staff and cloak and belt would hang if a prisoner were let keep them. They were empty. Across from the door a ward-stone the size of a loaf had been set into the wall at the height of a seated head, grey, its runes filled with gold, humming.

The mage sat in the chair.

She was young. Even for a human she was young; there was a roundness still in the face that the rest of her had outgrown, and the lamp, coming down on her from above, laid cold light along her cheekbones and dug the shadows out from under her eyes until they looked like bruises. Her hair had been braided once, fair, and most of it had come loose and hung down. Her coat was brown wool worn shiny at the cuffs and elbows, the hem stiff with dried mud, and the mud went to the knee on her trousers too. Her boots were good boots walked further than whoever sold them had ever expected, cracked across the toes, the left one tied with a knot that wasn't the knot it had come with.

No violet. No gold. Nothing done up.

Her hands lay on her knees. The knuckles on both were red and raw, the skin split and peeling the way Sylvanas had seen it on rangers coming off a winter watch at the high posts—frost-nip—except that nothing on the road last night had come near cold enough for that. The fingers of the right hand were black-violet with ink in every crease.

And her face. Sylvanas had seen that face before, in polished bronze, after budget sessions: the face of someone who has been saying the same true thing since before dawn to a run of people who listened politely and wrote it down and put it in a drawer. The jaw clamped so hard the muscle stood out under the ear. The mouth a flat line. Frightened people looked at the door. This one was looking at Sylvanas, and she was so angry it had gone quiet in her, and so tired the anger looked like the only thing holding her up in the chair.

Sylvanas didn't like that face any better on a human.

A fool in silk she could have sent home with an escort and a cold letter. This was Antonidas's apprentice and a lord admiral's daughter, without a seal or a letter or a single guard, frost-burned and filthy and sitting in an elven cell at the top of the Pass after a day spent arguing with rangers about the stones. If she'd come to harm on the road, Dalaran would hear of it. If she came to harm here, Dalaran would hear of it and Kul Tiras with it, and Silvermoon would learn about it from them. And if she was right about the stones—

Bad ground. Soft on every side, and a famous name standing in the middle of it.

The mage looked her over. Her eyes went to the bow first, then the quiver, then the green and gold at Sylvanas's shoulder, and stopped there long enough to read them. Then they came up to her face and stayed. They didn't drop. Sylvanas had been sized up by trolls with less care.

Then the mage's eyes slid away from her, to the left, to the ward-stone in the wall. A small frown came between her brows. It went, and her eyes came back to Sylvanas, and a breath later they went to the stone again and the frown came back deeper, the way a person frowns at a noise in the next room that might be a mouse and might not.

Sylvanas listened. The stone hummed the way the roadside stones had hummed under the hooves at a gallop, low and even, below sound, felt more in the teeth than the ear. She couldn't hear anything wrong in it. She couldn't hear anything wrong in it from a doorway, with a lamp hissing over her head and rangers breathing at her back—and her palm remembered moss, and a warm face of stone, and a beat that came late, and then late again.

What did the human hear? What made a mage frown at a sound stone? Or was it sound? She'd ridden past twenty stones since noon and couldn't swear to one of them.

The mage's hands stayed flat on her knees, ink and raw knuckles in the cold light. She'd planted her boots square on the floor, heels and toes both, like someone braced on a deck in a swell. She wasn't going to get up. She wasn't going to speak first either.

Behind Sylvanas nobody moved. She could feel them waiting on her—the ranger at the door, Cyndia at her shoulder, the two in the passage—and she knew what they were waiting for. Shut the door. Let the human sit the night in the cold and be softer for it by morning. Or go in.

A night would take some of the anger off her. It would also take a night.

Send her home, then. Four riders to the border at first light, the prisoner on a remount in the middle of them, and a polite letter to Dalaran in Lor'themar's best hand: we found something of yours. The map would go south with her in its satchel, and the ring of ink round Sylvanas's stone with it, and nobody would ever ask it how. No.

Hold her, then. Send to Silvermoon for a magister and keep the human fed and warm until one came with a lens and a clerk; wait while he put his lens to the stones and the clerk wrote stable; wait while the Convocation read the clerk. A fortnight, Lor'themar had said. Sun's sake, in a fortnight the whole ring could be limping.

She wanted to hand it off. She wanted it badly enough to taste—a rider north tonight, and this could be some magister's evening ruined instead of hers, someone who knew the words. She wasn't going to. She'd known she wasn't going to since the runner opened his mouth on the forest road, and she'd walked the length of this passage knowing it, and she hated that most of all.

And Antonidas. What kind of master let his best student walk over an elven border alone, with no seal and no letter and no escort, with frost-nip on her hands and her stockings darned by someone who'd plainly never darned a thing in her life? Had he sent her? Did he even know where she was? And the mage herself—sitting there exactly as the file had written her, strong enough to cross the ring without a ripple, Antonidas's to the bone, Kirin Tor ink in the creases of her fingers—couldn't even do Sylvanas the courtesy of being the fool in silk she'd come down the passage to deal with.

She stepped into the cell.

The rangers drew back from the doorway to give her the room, a scrape of boots on stone, and Cyndia stayed where she was. The lamplight caught Sylvanas's boots as she crossed the floor and laid blue along the dusty toes. She stopped in front of the chair, close enough that the mage had to tip her head back to keep looking at her, and the mage did.

The stone hummed in the wall. Out in the guardroom someone set a cup down on the table. Neither of them said anything for a long while, and Sylvanas let it go on, let the human sit in it; and the human sat in it, and didn't look away.

"Start talking."
