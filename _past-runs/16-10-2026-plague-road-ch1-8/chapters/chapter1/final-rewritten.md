# Chapter 1: The Border

Sylvanas had lain in the bracken long enough for the morning damp to soak through her leathers at both knees and one elbow. Overhead, the jays had already forgotten she was there. They quarrelled in the branches as the breeze stirred the golden leaves. Below her ridge, the slope dropped away into waist-high ferns toward a stream she could hear chuckling over stones, though the undergrowth hid the water. Three of the Amani raiders lay dead on the pine needles at her back, and the fourth had gone to ground somewhere in that green tangle. Halfway down the bank, a patch of fronds had gone completely still. On a morning with wind running down the draw, nothing else in the woods had reason to hold that quiet.

Across the ravine, Lor'themar sat his horse at the tree line. The patrol was strung out behind him in a wide half-circle where she had put them before dawn. He rested his bow across the saddle with an arrow already on the string. It was her shot. He would loose only if she missed—and then she would hear about it all the way back to Silvermoon.

Sooner or later, the last troll would break for the water. He had only two routes down the bank: the open shale slope, which was suicide under six bows, or the narrow gap between the leaning ash and the boulder, which merely gave him bad odds. Sylvanas drew her bowstring halfway to her cheek and waited. She kept her eyes on the gap.

He burst from cover on the treeward side, running low and fast with a throwing axe cocked back over his shoulder for the first ranger who stood up. Wet fronds sprayed wide as he cleared the brush. His left foot came down on an exposed root slick with moss, and for half a stride all that weight pitched the wrong way.

She drew to her ear and loosed into the stumble.

The broadhead took him high under the arm with a dull thump. Momentum carried him forward another three steps before his legs tangled in the bracken. He pitched into the ferns at the water's edge, thrashed once on the gravel, and lay still.

"Down," she called across the ravine, loud enough to scatter the jays a second time. "That's the last of them."

The coppery smell of troll blood reached her before she stood, cutting through the cleaner scents of pine resin and sun-warmed Eversong blossom. She pushed herself up and flexed her stiff knees. As she started down the bank through the fronds, a finch began calling again from the alders downstream. A jay answered from the ridge.

The axethrower lay on his side with his yellow eyes half open, his tusks streaked with grey silt where he had struck the bank. She planted her boot on his axe wrist before trusting that he was dead. Then she drew her knife and finished the work under the jaw, quick and clean, as she had been taught before she was old enough to string a war-bow. She wiped the blade on a dry fern frond, both sides, and used a second clump to take off the last smear.

Unhurried hoofbeats crunched on the gravel behind her. Lor'themar walked his horse down through the ferns and reined in a length upstream. He stayed upwind of the blood.

"Clean shot," he said.

"He slipped on the root." She thumbed the edge of her knife out of habit and slid it into its sheath.

"He slipped into your arrow. I watched him do it." Lor'themar leaned a forearm on his saddle horn. "That finishes the sweep—four tracks on the ridge path, four raiders down. I'll have the patrol drag them together and burn the bodies before the lynx find them."

"Do that."

He turned in the saddle and called the order up the slope. The rangers came down out of the tree line in pairs and swung off their mounts to water the horses upstream of the dead troll. Sylvanas braced her boot against the ribs and pulled her arrow free. She turned the steel head to the light to check the point; the edges were still true, so she scoured the shaft with moss and slid it back into her quiver.

It was barely past the middle of the morning. If they turned north now, they would reach Silvermoon by dusk, and she would sleep under her own roof. In the morning she would have to put on her formal greens and sit on a bench in the Convocation chamber at Sunfury Spire. She would listen for hours while lords who had never drawn a bow in anger argued over what bowstaves cost the treasury.

She looked south through the thinning trees toward the hills. "The ward-stones along the southern track need checking while we're out here."

Lor'themar slackened his reins. His horse dropped its head to crop at a fern.

"Today?" he asked.

"The outer ring is less than two miles south," she said, walking up the bank to her grey mare. "When did anyone last ride that stretch who wasn't a courier hurrying for the high road?"

"The magisters' wardens rode the circuit in the spring. I read the report, and so did you." He scratched along the old scar on his jaw and squinted south through the trunks. "It said stable. In fact, it said stable in three different hands."

"Then checking them won't take long."

"No," he agreed. "Nor will the Convocation."

She was stalling to buy an extra afternoon in the saddle, and he knew it. Still, he had no objection to letting her have the ride so long as they made the city by nightfall. Sylvanas untied her mare, who tossed her fine-boned head at the turn away from home stables, and swung up into the leather.

"Six riders with me," she called over the rush of the stream. "The rest finish the pyre here and follow our tracks."

A little before noon they reached the first stone on the outer ring. The dirt track bent around a granite outcrop where the ground fell away into a dry creek bed choked with dead leaves. The monolith stood on the verge of the turn. Like all the frontier markers, it rose taller than a mounted rider, grey and narrow and older than the road itself. Deep runes ran down its southern face with faded gold leaf in the cuts. Green moss had crept over its shaded northern shoulder.

The stone was humming before she was out of the saddle—a low vibration that sat more in the back teeth than the ear. Her mare sidestepped once on the gravel and blew through her nostrils, then stood quiet. She had halted beside a hundred such markers over the years. Sylvanas swung down, tossed the reins to the nearest rider, and walked up to the rock. Lor'themar stayed mounted behind her.

She pulled off her right riding glove and laid her bare palm against the carved face.

A sound ward-stone always held a steady warmth from within, unchanging at noon or midnight. Its pulse met the skin with an even thrum, steady as a horse's flank after a long trot. She had put her hand to border stones like this since her first summer riding the line behind her mother's captains. Every intact stone along the frontier had answered with that same unbroken beat.

This rock was warm to the touch, and the vibration rose to meet her fingers, but there was a distinct hitch in its rhythm. The beat pressed against her palm, faltered for a fraction of a breath, caught itself, and then dragged late on the third pulse. Worse still, underneath that surface warmth—deep in the core of the granite, where her sense of the ward could barely reach—lay a band of unnatural cold. It felt like a flat stone pried up from a winter creek bed, sun-warm on top while the underside still held the freezing water.

She kept her hand on the runes. The damp moss brushed her knuckles while she counted three more cycles.

Was the flaw real? Or had she ridden south looking for an excuse to stay out of the city, and imagined one because she wanted it? If one of her own scouts had brought her a tale that thin after dodging garrison duty, she would have had the fool walking the high snow-posts till midsummer. She pressed the heel of her hand harder against the groove and waited through five more beats. The pulse stumbled twice more against her skin, cold at the root.

No. It was real. She knew the feel of the border stones.

Yet if she rode into Sunfury Spire tomorrow and laid that complaint before the council, she didn't even have the words they would demand to take her seriously. Any junior magister out of the academies would have half a dozen technical terms for a limping ward-pulse, even though not one of those scholars had put a bare hand on a frontier stone in twenty years. They sent circuit wardens with brass lenses and clerks with ledgers. The wardens looked through their glass from horseback, the clerks wrote *stable* in neat columns, and the council signed the page without looking up. It galled her that they were blind to their own fence. It galled her nearly as much that she lacked the scholarly phrasing to force them to listen.

"Well?" Lor'themar asked.

She took her hand away from the rock and flexed her fingers against the lingering chill in her palm. "The pulse is uneven."

"Uneven how?"

"It drops a beat every third or fourth measure," she said, pulling her glove back on. "And there's a dead cold sitting underneath the warmth."

Lor'themar leaned forward in the saddle. His brow furrowed as he studied the mossy granite. Reaching down without dismounting, he laid his own bare hand against the runes for a moment, then straightened and shook his head. "Feels like warm stone to me."

"You never had the touch for ward-work."

"I never stood still beside a rock long enough to learn it." He gathered his reins, the bit chiming as his gelding lifted its head. "If the enchantment is slipping, that falls under the magisters' charter anyway. Put a note in the log tonight, and I'll carry it in with the border returns. They'll send an artificer down in a fortnight with a glass and a scribe."

"And the clerk will mark it stable from the road."

"Very likely." He turned his horse back toward the track and gave her a dry look over his shoulder. "Or I could tell the chamber tomorrow that the Ranger-General has taken up stone-divining in her spare hours. Half the bench would gladly vote to fund it—they'd much rather pay you to stand in a field touching granite than hear you lecture them on arrows."

She mounted her mare and twisted in the saddle for one last look at the pillar before turning east along the line.

For the next hour the trail followed the outer ring, taking the long route back toward the main road. Another stone rose out of the undergrowth every few hundred yards. The deep hum of the ward-line came up through the dirt into the horses' strides, steady beneath the crunch of dry leaves. Overhead, the afternoon light slanted amber through the high canopy. Back in the column, one of the younger scouts was humming softly under his breath, matching his pitch to the ring.

"Tomorrow morning," Lor'themar said, nudging his horse up to ride level with her, "the Convocation sits on the patrol budget. I mention it only because you've been quiet for three miles, and I'd hate to think you'd forgotten."

"I haven't."

"One of Lord Saltheril's nephews wants to know why the southern posts need new bowstaves when the old ones haven't broken," he continued, keeping his mount at an easy walk. "I'm told he's spent a week preparing tables of figures."

"Then he can ride south with the next relief and pull a six-year stave until it splinters in his face."

"I'll tell him you said so, though he'll bring a clerk to take notes." Lor'themar rested a hand on his thigh. "After the staves, they're going to ask why the Thalassian Pass needs a full company at peacetime strength. Trolls are east. Humans are polite. The wards are certified stable in three hands. What do I tell the bench?"

For a moment she considered telling him to bring up the faltering stone at the creek bend. Yet she knew how that would go in the chamber: the magisters would ask what instrument she had used to measure a cold core, and when she answered that she had felt it through her bare palm, they would smile politely and move to the next item on the agenda.

Even Cyndia, holding the Pass with her full company and her careful reports, rarely dealt with anything worse than a Lordaeron merchant carrying the wrong toll papers or a human lordling who had chased a stag too far into the hills. Whatever happened south of the mountains was Lordaeron's business. None of it belonged to Silvermoon's rangers unless it crossed the ward-line. One stone with a hitch in its pulse wasn't an army.

"Tell them the Amani know how to count," she said, keeping her eyes on the track ahead, "and they'll count our bows long before they count our coin."

"That's good. I'll use that." He nodded once. "You'll be sitting right beside me to deliver it yourself, of course."

"Wouldn't miss it."

He laughed quietly and let his horse drop back half a length to his usual place off her mare's shoulder. Sylvanas rubbed her gloved thumb across the middle of her right palm. The skin still felt faintly cold against the leather.

Ten minutes later, she heard the approaching rider before anyone else in the patrol reacted. A single horse was coming up the southern track behind them at a flat gallop—iron shoes striking the dirt in a fast, relentless beat that belonged to a courier riding through his mount's wind, not an ordinary traveller. At her raised fist, the patrol drew in around her without a word. Lor'themar pulled his bow off the saddle and nocked an arrow as he turned his mount. Sylvanas set her own fingers to her string.

Then the hoofbeats crossed a shelf of rock, and she recognized the gait. It was long-legged, with a slight drag on the near hind: a Farstrider horse from the Pass stables, and one that had been ridden hard.

The courier came around the bend at a dead run, hauling back so sharply at the sight of them that his bay gelding nearly sat on its haunches. Dark sweat lathered the horse from neck to flank. Foam hung from the bit in thick ropes, and its forelegs splayed wide as it fought for air. The rider was young, his green cloak grey with road dust. He tried to bring his fist to his heart while dragging air into his lungs, fumbling the salute as his chest heaved.

"Ranger-General," he got out between breaths. "From the Pass. Ranger Cyndia sends—"

"Skip the courtesies," Sylvanas said, easing her bowstring down. "Give me the report."

"An intruder crossed the outer line before daybreak," the youth said in a rush. "A human woman—a mage, by her gear."

Around her, the patrol went dead quiet. "She came through the ring?" Sylvanas asked.

"Right through it, and nobody on the wall felt the wards stir." He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. "The dawn relief found her on the second ring, crouched at a marker down off the road. She had her hands on the rock and loose papers spread out in the weeds around her knees. She didn't even try to run when the squad came up on her."

"What sort of papers? Any seal?"

"No seal, no escort, and no letter," the boy said, shaking his head. "She came quietly. Ranger Cyndia has her locked at the post now, but she told me to ride until I found you and repeat what the human said when they took her—her exact words."

"Then tell me."

The runner squared his shoulders in the saddle, catching his breath. "She said the leylines are wrong."

Sylvanas realized her right hand had curled into a fist around the reins. The small patch of chill was still there in her palm, under the leather, right where the monolith had left it two hours ago.

"Those were her exact words?"

"Word for word, Ranger-General. Cyndia had me repeat them back to her twice."

Beside the trail, Lor'themar lowered his bow across his knees. He was looking at her gloved hand.

A human mage had walked straight through the outer ward-ring in the dark without waking an alarm, only to sit down beside an inner stone with her papers spread around her boots. If Dalaran had sent a spy south to chart the wards, why stay until sunrise and let a patrol walk right up to her back? No scout with half a grain of sense waited around to lecture armed rangers about leylines. Perhaps she wanted to be caught as a provocation—though that made no sense without an army on the road behind her—or perhaps her wits had cracked. Or else she had put her bare hand on a stone in the dark, felt the same cold hitch underneath the pulse that Sylvanas had found at noon, and forgotten everything else in her haste to map it.

So much for her own bed tonight. So much for her seat in the Spire tomorrow. Even as the thought crossed her mind, she knew she wasn't sorry to trade a morning of budget arguments for a real breach on the mountain line. She wouldn't have admitted that aloud to Lor'themar, of course.

"Lor'themar," she said, "the Convocation—"

"I know." He was already sliding his bow back into its saddle loops. "I'll collect the others off the ridge and take the patrol north, and the chamber can have me instead. They like me better anyway; I don't force them to look at the border maps."

"Make sure they understand the Pass keeps its full company."

"I'll tell the chamber a border matter kept you in the hills." Turning his horse toward the ridge path, he paused and looked back at her. "Sylvanas—send word as soon as you know what she is."

She lifted two fingers from her reins in answer as he raised his arm to the column. She turned her mare back to the young courier and looked over his winded bay. Its flanks were still heaving hard.

"Can that horse make the climb?"

"He'll make it."

"Then bring him on. Keep up."

The patrol fell in behind Lor'themar in pairs, and she and the runner rode with them only as far as the fork where the old highway branched north toward Silvermoon. They split there without slowing. Ahead, the southern track turned from forest loam to packed gravel and then to cut stone, climbing out of the woods in long switchbacks where the mare's shoes rang against the rock. The sweetness went out of the air first, and then the afternoon warmth. By the upper ledges, the wind sweeping off the peaks had the sharp bite of snow in it.

Ward-stones rose along the roadside verge one after the next. Pushing her mare into a fast canter up the grade, she felt the hum of each marker rise through the roadbed under the hooves and fade away before the next stone began. She strained to listen past the hoofbeats for a limp in the rhythm. At a gallop, she couldn't hear one.

She rubbed her right palm hard against her thigh through the glove. At the next stone she did it again.

The Thalassian Pass post came into sight at the end of the light, a squat dark tower rising against a sky gone violet over the peaks. Lamps already burned in the gatehouse arch. Down across the saddle of the pass, the outer monoliths stood in a pale line through the dusk. The gate sentries saw her colours on the switchback and dragged the heavy bar clear before she had to call for it. She rode through at a canter, the runner's bay labouring in behind her, and swung out of the saddle before her mare had fully stopped.

"Where's Cyndia?" Her voice carried across the stone yard. Every ranger near the troughs turned. "Get her out here. Now."

Cyndia came out of the tower door while the last word still echoed off the walls, settling her quiver strap across her chest as she walked. Her auburn braid was half undone over one shoulder. She had been up since the midnight watch—dark hollows bruised the skin under her eyes, and she held her shoulders with a brittle stiffness.

"Ranger-General."

"Inside," Sylvanas said, already walking toward the steps. "Report as we go."

The guardroom at the Pass was built for winter. Its walls were four feet thick, the inner stone glistening faintly where the hearth's warmth met the alpine cold soaking through the granite. Iron brackets along both walls held ward-lamps that lit every corner with a steady, blue-edged white glare. After the long dusk on the switchbacks it hit Sylvanas hard, and she walked into it narrow-eyed.

Half a dozen off-duty rangers stood along the far wall with their hands near their bows, more than Cyndia normally kept in the hall at that hour. On the trestle table, someone's supper sat abandoned in a wooden bowl. A skin of grease had congealed over the stew, with a horn spoon standing upright in it.

"She slipped through the outer ring before dawn," Cyndia said, falling into step at her shoulder. "Nobody on the parapet felt her cross. Our night watch found her at first light on the second circuit, down at the split-capped stone below the high road. She had both palms pressed to the granite and her papers weighted down with pebbles on the grass around her boots. When the corporal put an arrow on the string and ordered her to step back, she showed her empty hands at once—and then she asked to stay."

"She asked?"

"Argued," Cyndia said. "She claimed she needed a full day with that stone and the next three along the ring. She told the squad that our stones are rotting from the core outward, and that Silvermoon's wardens would read them sound because they only measure the outsides. She said it twice on the climb up. When the corporal told her she'd be bound and carried if she wouldn't walk at their pace, she asked whether they would bind her ankles right there beside the stone so she could finish her work."

Rotting from the core outward.

Sylvanas's right hand had closed into a fist again. She opened it; the small, constant cold was still in her palm, unchanged since noon.

Any real operative sent across a hostile border knew to run once her work was done. A trained scout would have taken her notes in the dark and been three miles south toward Lordaeron before the watch found a scuffed footprint in the dirt. Yet this woman had stayed on her knees in front of drawn bows, asking to be tied to the granite. Either Dalaran's masters bred such blind arrogance into their apprentices that the girl genuinely believed elven law didn't apply to her, or she had been frightened half out of her wits and talking to keep her teeth still. Except terrified trespassers bolted for the trees instead of lecturing soldiers on stonework. Or else she was right: she had touched the rock, felt the same decay Sylvanas had felt at noon, and forgotten every sensible caution.

"Did she fight?"

"No," Cyndia said, leading the way toward the rear passage. "She talked the whole way up the hill about the stones, though what she really minded was us taking her off the marker."

"Where is her gear?"

"On the inspection rack outside the cells—staff, satchel, and the papers. The staff is under a damper."

"Show me the rack first. Then her."

The rack stood in a stone alcove off the cell passage, the air smelling of lamp oil and cold iron. It was a plain oak frame bolted to the wall, backed by a slab of grey dampening slate carved in close containment lines. The lamplight went flat a hand's width from it. Across the two hooks lay a wizard's staff of plain ash, the grain darkened where hands had worn the grip and bound in close, even turns of silver wire. At the head sat an uncut crystal the size of a hen's egg. Under the damper it had gone a dull violet, though every few seconds the colour crawled inside it and stilled.

Below the hooks, the contents of the satchel had been turned out onto the stone shelf and laid in neat rows according to Cyndia's strict garrison habit. There was a tin of ink with the lid screwed down tight, pens wrapped in oilcloth, and a leather field-book swollen with damp. Beside them lay a pair of stockings badly darned at the heels, a heel of bread gone hard as bark, a small brass compass, and a flat grey pebble cut with a single rune on one face. At the end of the shelf lay Cyndia's intelligence file with the Farstrider seal on its cover, opened to a page where someone had drawn a staff in careful charcoal.

Between the field-book and the file lay the map.

Someone had pinned the wide sheet flat with lead weights at the corners. It was human rag-paper, thick and soft, folded so often the creases had worn white. Every line on it was drawn by hand. Sylvanas leaned in, and her shadow fell across the chart.

Many of the ward-stones were plotted there—far more than any human south of the mountains had a right to know. Each was a small neat dot, strung along the outer ring and then the second, curving where the border ridges curved. Several of the dots had been circled in ink, and some twice. Thin ruled lines ran up from the Lordaeron edge of the sheet to end squarely at the circled stones. In the lower corner, a long grey thumbprint smeared the ink where the draughtsman had held the sheet down against the wind with one hand while drawing with the other.

A hired surveyor marked every stone along a road because he was paid by the stone, whereas a raider marked only the ones he meant to break and drew the roads in to them. This sheet showed both the full circuit and the approach lines. Then why carry it over the border in a satchel and spread it on the grass for the first patrol to read? Either she was a complete fool who had never worked outside a library, or she had brought the map north expecting she might need a ranger to read it.

The high road was drawn in, along with the Pass, marked by a small square with a flag. Sylvanas followed the outer ring east along the southern track to a bend around a shoulder of rock, where the trail dipped toward a dry creek bed. Whoever had drawn the map had taken the trouble to put in the dry creek as a wavering line of dashes. There was a dot at the bend, and a ring of ink around the dot.

Sylvanas put the tip of her gloved finger beside it. Under the leather, her palm prickled with a sudden memory of cold stone.

At noon she had stood in the ferns with her bare hand pressed to that stone, the moss against her knuckles. Yet days or weeks ago, a human south of the border had already drawn a circle around it.

How had she known which one to circle? Either she had crossed the outer line more than once this month and put her hand on it without a single patrol catching her, or someone on the elven side of the line had told her which stone to mark. Or else the rot was rising along those ruled lines from the south, and she had followed them straight up the map to the border.

"Kirin Tor ink," Cyndia said at her side, touching the margin of the map where the lines ran thickest. "It dries violet at the edges. Ours dries flat black. And the wire wrapping on the staff matches the sketch in our Dalaran file." She turned the open file a quarter round on the shelf so the charcoal staff faced Sylvanas. "She's Jaina Proudmoore—Archmage Antonidas's apprentice."

Sylvanas knew the file without leaning down to read it. She had gone through the Dalaran folder at her own desk last winter, reviewing the border reports on every notable human mage near the hills, and had written the girl off as a future nuisance. *Gifted beyond her years*, a Farstrider had written in a flat hand—and Farstriders were stingy with words like *gifted*. A lord admiral's daughter, and the archmage's favourite pupil, the one he brought along to council. A name worth watching.

Well, she had watched, and now the mage was sitting twenty paces away in her cells. If Antonidas had sent his prize pupil to test the elven wards, the old man had lost his wits entirely.

"Where is she?"

"End of the passage. Second door."

On the walk down the corridor from the rack, Sylvanas built a picture of the prisoner out of the file and the violet ink: good Dalaran wool with gold at the hem, fair hair pinned up, and a face arranged into the patient, patronizing courtesy that Sunfury magisters wore in the chamber when they meant to talk the Ranger-General into the floor. Somewhere inside her robe would be a letter with a council seal on it, held back until she could do the most harm with it. That would cost them a morning of stiff apologies, an escort to the border, and a sharp protest from Silvermoon to Dalaran.

The ranger on the second door saw her coming and stepped clear of the threshold. Two more filled the passage behind them, and Cyndia stopped at her shoulder.

"Open it."

The iron bar came up, and the heavy door swung in on a room barely wider than itself. A ward-lamp in a wall bracket threw the same hard white-blue light as the guardroom. Inside sat one wooden chair and a bucket. Empty iron pegs jutted from the masonry to the right of the door where a prisoner's cloak and belt would have hung if she had been allowed to keep them. Across from the door, mortared into the masonry behind the seat at the height of a prisoner's head, was a loaf-sized ward-stone—grey granite with gold in the runes, humming softly.

The mage sat upright in the chair.

She was young. Even for a human she was startlingly young; there was a trace of roundness still in her jaw that the rest of her tall frame had outgrown. The lamp dug bruise-dark shadows under her eyes. Her blonde hair had been braided once, but most of it had come loose on the climb and hung down over a travelling robe of brown wool, kilted up for walking and caked stiff at the hem with dried mud. Her boots were good leather, cracked across the toes. The left lace had snapped and been retied with a different knot.

Her bare hands rested on her knees, the knuckles chafed red and raw. Across the backs of her fingers, the skin was split and peeling with frost-nip, like the cold-burns Sylvanas had seen on rangers coming off a winter blizzard at the high posts. Nothing on the mountain road last night had come near cold enough for that. Every crease of her right fingers was stained black-violet with ink.

Then Sylvanas looked at the woman's face. She recognized the expression at once. She had met its match in her own glass after budget sessions at the Spire. It was the face of someone who had been repeating one plain warning since before dawn to people who merely wrote it down and put it in a drawer. The jaw was clamped so hard the muscle stood out under her ear, and instead of looking past her captors toward the open door as frightened prisoners did, she looked straight at Sylvanas out of blue eyes rubbed red at the rims—quiet, fixed, and angry well past the point of shouting.

A court fool in silk could have been sent home with an escort and a cold letter. Instead, sitting in an elven cell without a seal, a letter, or a guard was Antonidas's apprentice and a lord admiral's daughter, frost-burned and filthy from the road. If she came to harm here, Antonidas would hear of it before the month was out, and the lord admiral with him. And the magisters in Silvermoon would learn about it from Dalaran rather than from their own Ranger-General. Worse, if she was right about the stones, sending her away meant blinding herself to the only person who had mapped the rot.

Without rising, the mage looked her visitor over—bow, quiver, and the green and gold at her shoulder, taking long enough to read them—and came to Sylvanas's face last with a calm, measuring care.

Then the prisoner twisted half around in the chair toward the ward-stone behind her head. She frowned, a small crease appearing between her brows, and vanished it at once as she turned back to Sylvanas. A heartbeat later she turned to the stone a second time and frowned harder. Her head was cocked, as Sylvanas had seen hunting hounds cock theirs at a rustle in the bracken that might be a hare and might not.

Sylvanas listened, holding still on the threshold. To her ears, the small wall-stone hummed with the same low, even vibration that the roadside stones had sent up under the hooves on the climb. From a doorway, with the lamp hissing overhead and rangers breathing at her back, she couldn't hear anything wrong in it. Yet in the middle of her palm she still remembered damp moss, a warm face of granite, and a beat that came late and then late again.

What did the human hear from a foot away? What made a mage frown at a sound stone—or was it even sound? She had ridden past stone after stone since noon and couldn't swear to one of them.

On the flagstones, the mage's muddy boots were planted square, heels and toes both, like a rider braced in the stirrups for a bad landing.

In the corridor behind her, the guards were waiting. She could shut the door, let the human sit the night in the cold, and come back when the chill had made her softer by morning. Or she could go in now.

If she sent the girl home at dawn, the map went south with her, ink ring and all, and nobody in Quel'Thalas ever learned how she had drawn it. No—that was out of the question. Yet holding her for a magister meant waiting two weeks on a lens and a clerk, and the whole southern ring could be limping by then.

Part of her badly wanted to hand the matter off. One rider sent north tonight, and this could be some magister's evening ruined instead of hers—someone who actually knew the words for it. She wasn't going to do that. She had known it since the courier opened his mouth on the forest road.

And had Antonidas sent her, or did he not even know where she was? Either way, the mage had to be everything the winter file claimed: strong enough to cross the outer ring without waking an alarm, Antonidas's pupil to the bone, with Kirin Tor ink stained deep into the creases of her fingers.

Sylvanas stepped into the cell.

The rangers drew back from the doorway, their boots scraping on stone. Cyndia stayed where she was. Sylvanas crossed the narrow floor and stopped in front of the chair, close enough that the mage had to tip her head back to meet her eyes.

The stone hummed in the mortar behind the chair, and out in the guardroom someone set a cup down on the table. Sylvanas held her tongue and left the prisoner to sit in the quiet.

"Start talking."
