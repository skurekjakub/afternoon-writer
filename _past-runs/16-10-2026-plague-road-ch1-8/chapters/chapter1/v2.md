# Chapter 1: The Border

Sylvanas had lain in the bracken long enough for the damp to soak through her leathers at both knees and one elbow. The jays had forgotten her, quarrelling again in the branches overhead. Below her the slope fell away into waist-high ferns, running down to a stream she could hear and not see. Three of the Amani lay dead on the ridge at her back, and the fourth was somewhere in those ferns. The fronds far down the slope had gone still. On a morning with wind in it, nothing else in the hollow had reason to keep that still.

Across the hollow, Lor'themar sat his horse at the tree line, the patrol strung behind him in a half-circle, each rider where she'd put them. He had his bow across the saddle and an arrow on the string. It was her shot. He would take it only if she missed, and then she'd hear about it all the way home.

The troll would go for the stream. They always went for water when they were losing, as if running water had ever stopped an arrow. He had two ways down: the open slope, which was suicide, or the gap between the leaning ash and the boulder, which was only stupid. Sylvanas drew halfway and held, the string against her lips, watching the gap.

He broke cover from the ash side, low and fast, tusks first, a throwing axe cocked over his shoulder for whoever stood up. Big for an axethrower. He came out of the ferns in a spray of wet fronds and landed his left foot on a root slick with moss. For half a stride all that weight went the wrong way.

She drew the rest of the way and loosed into the half-stride.

The arrow went in under the arm with a sound like a boot dropped in mud. He kept running for a few more steps, because they did, and then his legs went one way and the rest of him another and he folded into the ferns at the edge of the stream and thrashed there, and stopped.

"Down," she called across the hollow, loud enough that the jays went silent again. "That's the last."

The smell reached her before she stood, troll blood, rank and coppery, cutting through the resin and sun-warmed blossom. She got up, learning from her knees exactly how long she'd lain on them, and went down the slope, ferns crushing under her boots.

By the time she reached him, the birds had started again. A finch first, close by, then the jays, then something further off she didn't bother to name.

The troll lay on his side with his eyes half open, tusks streaked with stream silt. She put her boot on his axe hand before she trusted it, then drew her knife and made sure, quick and under the jaw, as she'd been taught before she could string a longbow. She wiped the blade on a fern frond, both sides, and again on a second because the first had smeared it.

Hoofbeats behind her, unhurried. Lor'themar walked his horse down through the ferns and stopped a length away, out of the smell.

"Clean," he said.

"He slipped." She thumbed the edge out of habit and sheathed the knife.

"He slipped into your arrow. I watched him do it." He leaned on the saddle horn, easing his weight off the stirrups. "That's the sweep, then. Four on the ridge path, four down. I'll have them burn these before something with a nose finds them."

"Do."

He turned in the saddle and gave the order in two words. The patrol came down out of the tree line by pairs, riders swinging off to water their horses upstream of the troll. Sylvanas pulled her arrow out of the troll and turned the head to the light. It was worth keeping, so she cleaned that too.

It was barely past the middle of the morning. If they turned for home now, they would make Silvermoon by dusk, and she would sleep in her own bed. In the morning she would put on the formal greens and sit a bench in the Convocation chamber. Men who had never drawn a bow would argue about what bows cost.

She slid the arrow into her quiver. "The stones on the southern track want looking at."

Lor'themar let his horse drop its head to crop at a fern.

"Today?" he said.

"The outer ring's just south of us. When did anyone last ride that stretch who wasn't a courier?"

"The magisters' wardens rode it in the spring. I read the report. You read the report. It said stable." He scratched his jaw, squinting south through the trees. "It said stable in three different hands."

"Then it won't take long."

"No. Nor will the Convocation."

Stalling, Ranger-General. He knew it, and he'd let her do it and enjoy every hour. She swung up onto her mare, which shifted under her, a little sidestep, a blown breath. Sylvanas sat it out, gathered the reins and turned south.

"Six riders with me," she said, raising her voice over the stream. "The rest finish here and follow."

A little before noon they reached the first stone. The track bent round a shoulder of rock there, dropping toward a dry creek bed, and the stone stood at the bend the way they all stood, taller than a mounted rider, grey and narrow and older than the track. Runes ran deep down its southern face, their cuts filled with a faint gold. Moss had grown up over its shoulder on the shaded side. A ward-stone worked the same under moss as bare.

The stone was humming before she was off the horse, low, more in the teeth than the ear. She swung down, tossed the reins to the nearest rider and walked up to the stone. Lor'themar stayed in the saddle at her back.

She pulled off her right glove and laid her bare palm flat against the rune face.

A sound stone was warm from the inside, the same at noon as at midnight, and the hum came through it steady and even, like a sound horse's flank under the hand after a long trot. She had put her palm to stones like this since she was a girl, riding the line behind her mother's rangers. Every one of them had been exactly that.

This one was warm, and the hum came through. But it came through wrong, a hitch in it, as though the stone were favouring a leg. The beat came, then came again a fraction late, caught itself, went late again. Under the warmth, deep where her palm could only just reach, there was cold, the cold of a river rock turned over, sun-warm on top, the underside still holding the water.

She kept her hand there, the moss brushing her knuckles, damp and soft.

Was that real? Or had she ridden half a morning south looking for a reason to stay out, and made one up because she wanted one? She'd have flayed a ranger for that excuse. She pressed harder, and the hum hitched again under the heel of her hand. Maybe the cold had always been there, and she'd only now been rude enough to feel for it.

No. It was real. She knew stones.

And what did she call it, then? Off. Late. Cold. A magister would have a word for it, six words, a treatise. Not one of them had ever put a bare hand on a ward-stone in his life. They sent wardens with lenses and clerks with ledgers, and the clerks wrote stable, and the magisters read the signatures and moved on. Damn them. And damn her too, for wishing she had the word.

"Well?" Lor'themar said.

She took her hand away, and the palm stayed cold. "It's off."

"Off how?"

"Off." She pulled her glove back on, flexing the hand inside it. "The beat's uneven. There's cold under it."

He studied the stone, his brow drawn down the way it went over a trail. Leaning out of the saddle, he laid his own bare palm flat on the stone and took it away again. "Feels like a stone to me."

"You never had the hand for it."

"I never sat still for it long enough to find out." He straightened in the saddle. "If it's wrong, it's the magisters' work. Write it up. I'll carry it in with the rest. They'll send someone in a fortnight with a lens and a clerk."

"And the clerk will write stable."

"Very likely." He gathered his reins, the bit chiming as his horse lifted its head. "Shall I tell the chamber the Ranger-General has gone into stone-reading? They'll want to fund it. Half of them would rather pay you to stand in a field touching rocks than hear you talk about arrows."

She mounted and twisted in the saddle for one more look at the stone. Moss on the shoulder. Gold in the runes.

She turned the mare along the line, taking the long way round toward home, her right hand shut on the reins. For a while the track ran east with the ward line, another stone standing up out of the undergrowth every few hundred yards. The hum came up through the ground into the horses' strides until she felt it more than heard it. The afternoon light came amber through the canopy. Somebody behind her was humming along with the ring without knowing it.

"Tomorrow," Lor'themar said, drawing level with her, "the Convocation sits on the patrol budget. I mention it only because you've gone so quiet I thought you might have forgotten."

"I hadn't."

"One of the magisters' nephews wants to know why the southern posts need new bowstaves when the old ones haven't broken. I'm told he's prepared figures."

"Then he can come south and break one."

"I'll tell him you said so. He'll bring a clerk." Lor'themar lifted his rein hand and began counting off fingers. "They're going to ask what the Pass needs a full company for. Trolls are east. Humans are polite. The wards are stable; it says so in three hands. What do I tell them?"

The opening. She could tell him about the stone. He would carry it into the chamber and lay it in front of a row of magisters. They would ask what she meant by cold. She would say cold.

Even Cyndia, at the Pass with her full company and her careful reports, expected merchants with the wrong papers out of the south, and the odd human lordling who took a wrong turn hunting. South of the wards was Lordaeron's business. None of it was hers unless it came over her line or touched her stones or her riders. It hadn't. One stone with a limp in it wasn't an army.

"Tell them the trolls can count," she said, "and they'll count our bows before they count our coin."

"That's good. I'll use that. You'll be there to say it yourself, of course."

"Wouldn't miss it."

He laughed quietly and kept his horse at her mare's shoulder, the same half-length back he always rode.

She rubbed her thumb across the cold place in her palm. The road went on east under the amber light, the hum running on beneath the hooves.

She heard the rider before anyone else did. A single horse, south and behind, coming up the track hard, hoofbeats too fast and too even for anyone who meant to stop politely. At her raised fist the patrol drew in around her without a word. Lor'themar pulled his bow off the saddle and nocked as he turned, and she set her own fingers to her string.

Then she knew the gait. Long-legged, a slight drag on the near hind. A Farstrider horse, and one that had been ridden too far.

He came round the bend at a dead gallop, hauling up so hard at the sight of them that the horse nearly sat down. It was dark with sweat from neck to flank, foam hanging off the bit in ropes, legs splayed, sides heaving. The rider was young, his green cloak grey with road dust. He tried to salute and breathe at once and managed neither.

"Ranger-General." He dragged in air. "From the Pass. Ranger Cyndia sends—"

"Skip the compliments. What."

"Intruder. Outer ring, before dawn. Human. A woman. Mage."

The patrol went quiet around her. "Through the ring?" Sylvanas said.

"Through it. Nobody felt her cross." He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. "Night watch found her at first light. She was at a stone. Down off the high road. Studying it. Had her hands on it, and papers spread out on the ground around her, and she didn't run when they came up on her."

"Papers. Any seal?"

"No seal, no escort, no letter. She came quiet. Ranger Cyndia has her at the post. She asked me to tell you what the human said when they took her. Exactly what she said."

"Then tell me."

The runner got his chin up for the first time. "She said the leylines are wrong."

Behind her, one of the horses blew, shaking its head until its rider hushed it.

Sylvanas had closed her right hand into a fist without meaning to. The cold was still there in the middle of it, under the glove, exactly where the stone had put it.

"Those were her words?"

"Word for word. Ranger Cyndia had me say them back to her twice."

Lor'themar lowered his bow across his knees. He was looking at her hand.

A human. A mage, through her ring in the dark without a ripple, crouched at one of her stones with her papers out. A spy, then: Kirin Tor, sent to count the wards. Then why stay and let the rangers take her? What spy, caught at a ward-stone, looks up at a Farstrider's drawn bow and talks about leylines? One who wants to be caught. No, nonsense. One who's mad, then. Possibly. Or one who put her hand on a stone and felt what Sylvanas had felt at noon.

Sun's sake.

Under all of it she had already worked out that her Convocation bench would sit empty tomorrow. Grinning over a breach, Ranger-General. She'd have had any ranger of hers mucking stalls till midsummer for less.

"Lor'themar, the Convocation—"

"I know." He was already sliding the bow back into its loops. "I'll take the patrol north, and the chamber can have me instead. They like me better anyway; I don't make them look at the map."

"Tell them the Pass needs its full company."

"I'll collect the others off the ridge and tell them you were called off by something with teeth. Sylvanas—send word when you know what she is."

She lifted two fingers off the reins at him. He turned his horse, raising his arm. She turned to the runner's horse, still blowing, still splayed.

"Can he go on?"

"He'll go."

"Then he goes. Keep up."

The patrol fell in behind Lor'themar in pairs, and she and the runner rode with them as far as the fork where the old road went north to Silvermoon. They split there. For a while the patrol's hoofbeats came back to her off the northern road, soft under the trees. Ahead, the southern track turned to packed earth and then to stone, climbing out of the woods in long switchbacks, the mare's hooves ringing. The sweetness went out of the air first, then the warmth. By the high switchbacks the wind off the heights had snow somewhere in it.

The stones came up along the roadside one after the next. She rode fast now, the hum of each rising under the hooves and falling away before the next began. She listened past the hoofbeats for a limp in the hum. At a gallop she couldn't hear one.

She rubbed her right palm against her thigh, hard, through the glove. At the next stone she did it again.

The Pass post came into sight at the end of the light, a squat dark tower against a sky gone violet over the peaks. Lamps already burned in the gate, the outer stones standing in their line across the saddle of the pass below, grey on grey. The gate rangers saw her colours and dragged the bar back before she had to call for it. She rode through at a canter, the runner's horse labouring in behind her, and was out of the saddle before the mare had properly stopped.

"Where's Cyndia?" Her voice carried across the yard, and every ranger in it turned. "Get her out here. Now."

Cyndia came out of the tower door while the last word still rang off the yard walls, settling her quiver strap as she walked. She had been up since the night watch, and it showed in the grey under her eyes and in her shoulders, held too square.

"Ranger-General."

"Inside. Report as we go."

The guardroom at the Pass was built for winter, the walls thick, the stones sweating faintly where the hearth's warmth met the cold coming through them. Ward-lamps burned in iron brackets along both walls and lit every corner. The light was white with blue at the edges, steady as noon. After the long dusk on the switchbacks it hit Sylvanas hard, and she walked into it narrow-eyed.

Rangers stood along the far wall, more of them than Cyndia kept on duty at that hour. The benches stood empty. The rangers straightened as she came in, hands near their bows. On the table someone's supper sat abandoned in a wooden bowl, skin gone over the stew, a spoon standing in it.

"She came through the outer ring before dawn," Cyndia said, falling in at her shoulder. "Nobody on the wall felt her cross. The night watch found her at first light on the second ring. She was at the stone below the high road, the one with the split cap. She had both hands on it and her papers on the ground round her feet, weighted down with stones off the verge. The watch put an arrow on the string and told her to step away from the stone. She stepped away and showed her hands, and then she asked to stay."

"Asked."

"Argued. She said she needed a day with that stone and the next ones along the ring. She said the stones were sick from the inside, and that our wardens would read them sound because they only read the outsides." Cyndia had rested her left hand near her quiver and kept it there. "She said it twice. When the watch told her she'd be bound and carried if she didn't walk, she asked whether she could be bound at the stone."

Sick from the inside.

Sylvanas's right hand had closed again. She opened it. The small, constant cold was still in the middle of the palm, as it had been all afternoon.

A spy ran. Any spy worth his pay would have been halfway to Lordaeron before the night watch found a cold camp and a scuff in the dirt. This one had sat down in front of a drawn bow, asking to be tied to the stone. Arrogance, then. Kirin Tor masters bred it into their apprentices the way her own people bred speed into horses. Or she'd been frightened half out of her wits, talking to keep her teeth still, except frightened people ran too, and faster. Or she was right.

"Did she fight?"

"No. She talked the whole way up. About the stones. What she minded was us taking her off the stone."

"Her gear."

"On the rack. Staff, satchel, the papers. The staff's under a damper."

"Show me. Then her."

The rack stood in a stone alcove off the passage to the cells, the air smelling of lamp oil and cold iron. It was a plain frame bolted to the wall, with a dampening plate set into its back, grey slate carved in close lines. The lamplight went flat a hand's width from it. The staff lay across two hooks: plain ash, the grain dark where hands had worn it, silver wire binding the grip in close even turns. At the head sat a crystal the size of a hen's egg. Under the damper it had gone a dull violet, and now and then the colour crawled in it and stilled.

The satchel had been turned out onto the shelf below and laid in rows, the way Cyndia laid out everything. A tin of ink with the lid screwed down tight. Pens wrapped in oilcloth. A field-book swollen with damp. Stockings darned at the heels, badly. A heel of bread gone hard as bark. A small brass compass. A flat grey pebble, too smooth for a riverbed, one face cut with a single rune, wrapped in a twist of cloth. Cyndia's file lay open at the end of the shelf, the Farstrider seal on its cover, and on the open page someone had drawn a staff in careful charcoal.

And the map.

Somebody had pinned it flat with lead weights at the corners. It was human paper, thick and soft, folded so often the creases had gone white. Every line on it was drawn by hand. Sylvanas leaned in, throwing her shadow across half the sheet, and shifted until it cleared.

Many of the ward-stones were there, more than any human had a right to know. Each was a small neat dot, strung along the outer ring and then the second, curving where the border curved. Some of the dots had been circled, and some twice. Thin ruled lines ran up from the bottom edge of the sheet, the Lordaeron side, toward the border, each one ending at a circled stone. In the lower corner the ink had smeared into a long grey thumbprint where somebody had held the sheet down against the wind with one hand and drawn with the other.

A surveyor marked every stone; a surveyor was paid by the stone. A raider marked the ones he meant to break and drew the roads in to them. These had roads. Then why carry it over the border in her satchel and spread it on the verge for the first ranger along to read? Because she was a fool, or because she meant a ranger to read it.

The high road was there. So was the Pass, a small square with a flag drawn on it. She followed the outer ring east along the southern track to a bend round a shoulder of rock, the track dropping toward a dry creek bed. Whoever had drawn it had taken the trouble to put in the creek, a wavering line of dashes. There was a dot at the bend, and a ring of ink around the dot.

Sylvanas put her finger beside it. Under the glove her palm went cold, or she only thought it did.

At noon she'd stood there with her hand flat on that stone, the moss against her knuckles. And days ago or weeks, a human south of the border had drawn a circle round it.

How? To circle that one, you came north and put your hand on it, and nobody felt you cross. Or somebody on this side of the line told you which one to circle. Or—

"Kirin Tor ink," Cyndia said at her side, touching the edge of the map where the lines ran thickest. "It dries violet at the edges. Ours dries black. The staff matches the one in our file, the wire and the crystal both." She turned the file a quarter round on the shelf so the charcoal staff faced Sylvanas. "It's Jaina Proudmoore. Antonidas's apprentice."

Sylvanas studied the drawing, then the staff on its hooks. The dim crystal shifted colour and went still again.

She knew the file. She'd read it last winter at her own desk, the way she read every file on human mages near her border, and written the girl off as a future nuisance. Gifted beyond her years, a Farstrider had written, in a flat hand, and Farstriders were stingy with words like gifted. A lord admiral's daughter. The archmage's favourite, the one he brought to council. A name worth watching.

Well, she'd watched, and now the mage was sitting in her cells. Damn the old man, and Dalaran, and every tower in it.

"Where is she?"

"End of the passage. Second door."

On the walk from the rack she built the prisoner out of the file and the ink: good violet wool with gold at the hem, hair done up, clean hands folded, a face arranged into patient courtesy, the way the magisters arranged theirs in chamber when they meant to talk the Ranger-General into the floor. Somewhere about her, a letter with a seal on it, held back until she could do the most harm with it. A nuisance with a pedigree. A morning lost to apologies, an escort to the border, and a sharp letter north from Silvermoon.

The ranger on the second door saw her coming and stood off it. More filled the passage behind, and Cyndia stopped at her shoulder.

"Open it."

The bar came up, and the door swung in on a room barely wider than itself. A ward-lamp in a bracket threw the same hard white-blue light as the guardroom. One wooden chair. A bucket. Iron pegs jutted from the wall to the right of the door at shoulder height, empty, for a prisoner's staff and cloak and belt if a prisoner were let keep them. Across from the door, a ward-stone the size of a loaf was set into the wall at the height of a seated head, grey, gold in the runes, humming.

The mage sat in the chair.

She was young. Even for a human she was young; there was a roundness still in the face that the rest of her had outgrown. The lamp dug the shadows out under her eyes until they looked like bruises. Her hair had been braided once, fair, but most of it had come loose and hung down over a travelling robe of brown wool, kilted up for walking, worn shiny at the cuffs and elbows, the hem stiff with dried mud. Her boots were good boots, cracked across the toes. The left had been retied with a different knot.

Her hands lay on her knees, the knuckles red and raw. The skin was split and peeling with frost-nip, the way Sylvanas had seen it on rangers coming off a winter watch at the high posts. Nothing on the road last night had come near cold enough for that. The fingers of the right hand were black-violet with ink in every crease.

And her face. Sylvanas knew that face. She'd met it in her own glass after budget sessions. The face of someone who'd been saying one thing since before dawn, to people who wrote it down and put it in a drawer. The jaw clamped so hard the muscle stood out under the ear. Frightened people looked at the door. This one was looking at Sylvanas, quiet and fixed, angry well past the point of shouting.

Sylvanas didn't like that face any better on a human.

A fool in silk she could have sent home with an escort and a cold letter. This was Antonidas's apprentice, a lord admiral's daughter, frost-burned and filthy in an elven cell without a seal or a letter or a guard. If she came to harm here, Antonidas would hear of it and the lord admiral with him, and the magisters in Silvermoon would learn about it from them. And if she was right about the stones—

The mage looked her over: the bow first, then the quiver, then the green and gold at Sylvanas's shoulder, long enough to read them. She came to Sylvanas's face last. Sylvanas had been sized up by trolls with less care.

Then the mage turned her head left, toward the ward-stone in the wall. She frowned, a small crease between the brows, gone at once. She faced Sylvanas again. Then she turned to the stone once more and frowned harder, head cocked, as Sylvanas had seen hounds cock theirs at a rustle in the bracken that might be a hare and might not.

Sylvanas listened, holding still on the threshold. The stone hummed the way the roadside stones had hummed under the hooves, low and even. From a doorway, with the lamp hissing overhead and rangers breathing at her back, she couldn't hear anything wrong in it. In her palm she remembered moss, a warm face of stone, a beat that came late and then late again.

What did the human hear? What made a mage frown at a sound stone? Was it even sound? She'd ridden past stone after stone since noon and couldn't swear to one of them.

The mage's boots were planted square on the floor, heels and toes both, like a rider braced in the stirrups for a bad landing. She meant to keep that chair until Sylvanas spoke first.

Behind Sylvanas the rangers held the doorway. They were waiting on her. Shut the door. Let the human sit the night in the cold and be softer for it by morning. Or go in.

Send her home, then, and the map went south with her, ink ring and all, and nobody ever learned how she'd drawn it. No. Hold her for a magister, and wait a fortnight on a lens and a clerk. Sun's sake, a fortnight. The whole ring could be limping by then.

She wanted to hand it off, badly enough to taste. A rider north tonight, and this could be some magister's evening ruined instead of hers, someone who knew the words. She wasn't going to. She'd known it since the runner opened his mouth on the forest road.

And had Antonidas sent her? Did he even know where she was? The mage had to be exactly what the file said. Strong enough to cross the ring without a ripple, Antonidas's to the bone, Kirin Tor ink in the creases of her fingers.

She stepped into the cell.

The rangers drew back from the doorway, their boots scraping on stone. Cyndia stayed where she was. Sylvanas crossed the floor and stopped in front of the chair, close enough that the mage had to tip her head back.

The stone hummed in the wall, and out in the guardroom someone set a cup down on the table. Sylvanas held her tongue and left the human to sit in the quiet. The human sat in it.

"Start talking."
