# Chapter 1: The Border

Sylvanas had lain in the bracken long enough for the damp to soak through her leathers at both knees and one elbow. The jays had forgotten her, quarrelling again in the branches overhead. Below her the slope fell away into ferns, thick and waist-high, running down to a stream she could hear and not see. Three of the Amani lay dead on the ridge at her back, and the fourth was somewhere in those ferns. The fronds forty yards down had gone still. Nothing else in the hollow had reason to keep that still, not on a morning with wind in it.

Across the hollow, Lor'themar sat his horse at the tree line. The patrol was strung behind him in a half-circle, each rider where she'd put them. He had his bow across the saddle and an arrow on the string—not drawing. It was her shot. He would take it only if she missed, and then she'd hear about it all the way home.

She did not intend to miss.

The troll would go for the stream. They always went for water when a fight turned on them, as if running water had ever stopped an arrow. He had two ways down: the open slope, which was suicide, or the gap between the leaning ash and the boulder, which was only stupid. Sylvanas drew halfway and held, the string resting against her lips, watching the gap.

He broke cover from the ash side, low and fast, tusks first, a throwing axe cocked over his shoulder for whoever stood up. Big for an axethrower. He came out of the ferns in a spray of wet fronds, landing his left foot on a root slick with moss. For half a stride all that weight went the wrong way.

She drew the rest of the way and loosed into the half-stride.

The arrow went in under the arm with a sound like a boot dropped in mud. He kept running for three more steps, because they did, and then his legs went one way and the rest of him another and he folded into the ferns at the edge of the stream and thrashed there, and stopped.

"Down," she called across the hollow, loud enough that the jays went silent again. "That's the last."

The smell reached her before she stood. Troll blood, rank and coppery and wrong, cut through the sweetness Eversong air carried in the warm months, all resin and sun-warmed blossom. She got up, learning from her knees exactly how long she'd lain on them. Ignoring them, she went down the slope, ferns crushing under her boots and springing back behind her. The broken stalks bled a sharp green smell into the blood.

By the time she reached him, the birds had started again. A finch first, close by, then the jays, then something further off she didn't bother to name. That was how you knew a hollow was empty. The birds always said so before the scouts did.

The troll lay on his side with his eyes half open, tusks streaked with stream silt. She put her boot on his axe hand before she trusted it. Then she drew her knife and made sure, quick and under the jaw, as she'd been taught before she could string a longbow. The blade she wiped on a fern frond, both sides, cleaning it again on a second frond because the first had smeared it.

Hoofbeats behind her, unhurried. Lor'themar walked his horse down through the ferns and stopped a length away, out of the smell, looking at the body and then at her.

"Clean," he said.

"He slipped." She thumbed the edge out of habit and sheathed the knife.

"He slipped into your arrow. I watched him do it." He leaned on the saddle horn, easing his weight off the stirrups. "That's the sweep, then. Four on the ridge path, four down. I'll have them burn these before something with a nose finds them."

"Do."

He turned in the saddle, giving the order in two words. The patrol came down out of the tree line by pairs, riders swinging off to water their horses upstream of the troll. Someone laughed at something, and someone else was already complaining about his boots. Sylvanas pulled her arrow out of the troll, turning the head to the light. It was worth keeping, so she cleaned that too.

It was barely past the middle of the morning. The sun came down through the canopy in long slants, turning the ferns gold at the tips. The stream ran bright over its stones. Somewhere upslope a woodpecker started hammering, as though he'd been waiting for the noise to stop. If they turned for home now, they would make Silvermoon by dusk, and she would sleep in her own bed. In the morning she would put on the formal greens and sit a bench in the Convocation chamber. Men who had never drawn a bow would argue about what bows cost.

She slid the arrow back into her quiver. "The stones on the southern track want looking at."

Lor'themar did not answer right away. He let his horse drop its head to crop at a fern, watching her. He'd served under her long enough to know when she'd decided something before she said it.

"The stones," he said.

"The outer ring's just south of us. When did anyone last ride that stretch who wasn't a courier?"

"The magisters' wardens rode it in the spring. I read the report. You read the report. It said stable." He scratched his jaw, squinting south through the trees. "It said stable in three different hands."

"Then it won't take long."

"No. Nor will the Convocation."

Stalling, Ranger-General. He knew it, and he'd let her do it anyway and enjoy every hour, which was worse than arguing. Without looking at him, she swung up onto her mare. The mare shifted under her, settling her weight the way she always did after standing—a little sidestep, a blown breath. Sylvanas sat it out, gathered the reins and turned south.

"Six riders with me," she said, raising her voice over the stream. "The rest finish here and follow."

A little before noon they found the first stone. The track bent there round a shoulder of rock, dropping toward a dry creek bed. It stood at the bend the way they all stood, taller than a mounted rider, grey and narrow and older than the track. Runes ran deep down its southern face, filled with the faint gold that never quite looked like paint. Moss had grown up over its shoulder on the shaded side, thick and dark as a pelt. Nobody had scraped it; nobody had needed to. A ward-stone did not care about moss.

She heard it before she was off the horse—low, more in the teeth than the ear, the ring running stone to stone. She swung down, tossing the reins to the nearest rider, and walked up to the stone. Behind her, Lor'themar stayed in the saddle, and she could feel him watching.

She pulled off her right glove, laying her bare palm flat against the rune face.

A sound stone was warm. Not sun-warm; the warmth came from inside, the same at noon as at midnight. The hum came through it steady and even, like a sound horse's flank under the hand after a long trot—a working thing at its work. She had put her palm to stones like this since she was a girl, riding the line behind her mother's rangers. Never once had she felt one that wasn't exactly that.

This one was warm, and the hum came through. But it came through wrong, a hitch in it, as though the stone were favouring a leg. The beat came, then came again a fraction late—caught itself—went late again. Under the warmth, deep under it, where her palm could only just reach, there was cold. Not the cold of stone in shade. The cold of a river rock turned over, sun-warm on top, the underside still holding the water.

She kept her hand there, the moss brushing her knuckles, damp and soft.

Was that real? Or had she ridden half a morning south looking for a reason to stay out—and found one because she wanted one? That was the kind of excuse she'd have flayed a ranger for. She pressed harder, as though that would help, and the hum hitched again under the heel of her hand. The cold sat under it—maybe it had always been there, and she'd only now been rude enough to feel for it.

No. It was real. She knew stones.

And what did she call it, then? Off. Late. Cold. A magister would have a word for it, six words, a treatise. Not one of them had ever put a bare hand on a ward-stone in his life. They sent wardens with lenses and clerks with ledgers. The clerks wrote stable, and the magisters signed it, and the Convocation read the signatures and moved on. Damn them. And damn her too, for standing here wishing she had the word.

"Well?" Lor'themar said.

She took her hand away, some of the cold coming with it and lodging in the palm. "It's off."

"Off how?"

"Off." She pulled her glove back on, flexing the hand inside it. "The beat's uneven. There's cold under it."

He looked at the stone, then at her. For a moment he had a ranger's face and not a courtier's. Leaning out of the saddle, he put his own bare hand to the stone, briefly, palm flat. "Feels like a stone to me."

"You never had the hand for it."

"I never had the patience for it. There's a difference." He straightened, settling back into the saddle. "If it's wrong, it's the magisters' work. Write it up. I'll carry it in with the rest. They'll send someone in a fortnight with a lens and a clerk."

"And the clerk will write stable."

"Very likely." He gathered his reins, the bit chiming as his horse lifted its head. "Shall I tell the chamber the Ranger-General has gone into stone-reading? They'll want to fund it. Half of them would rather pay you to stand in a field touching rocks than hear you talk about arrows."

She mounted, the mare standing steady for it with her ears forward. Behind them the six riders sat easy, reins loose, their horses shifting weight under riders they knew. Sylvanas looked at the stone once more, twisting in the saddle. Moss on the shoulder. Gold in the runes. Nothing a person riding by would ever look at twice.

She turned the mare along the line, taking the long way round toward home, her right hand shut on the reins. For a while the track ran east with the ward line, another stone standing up out of the undergrowth every few hundred yards. The hum came up through the ground into the horses' strides, low and constant. After a time she stopped hearing it and only felt it. The afternoon light came amber through the canopy, laying long bars across the road for the horses to walk through. Somebody behind her was humming along with the ring without knowing it. Somebody always did.

"Tomorrow," Lor'themar said, drawing level with her, "the Convocation sits on the patrol budget. I mention it only because you've gone so quiet I thought you might have forgotten."

"I hadn't."

"One of the magisters' nephews wants to know why the southern posts need new bowstaves when the old ones haven't broken. I'm told he's prepared figures."

"Then he can come south and break one."

"I'll tell him you said so. He'll take it as an invitation and bring a clerk." Lor'themar shifted in the saddle, easing a knee. "They're going to ask what the Pass needs a full company for. Trolls are east. Humans are polite. The wards are stable; it says so in three hands. What do I tell them?"

There it was. The opening. She could tell him about the stone. He would carry it into the chamber, laying it in front of a row of magisters. They would ask what she meant by cold. She would say cold, and they would look at each other.

Even Cyndia, sitting at the Pass with her full company and her careful reports, expected nothing from the south but merchants with the wrong papers. Now and then a human lordling took a wrong turn hunting, and someone had to walk him home. South of the wards was Lordaeron's business—Lordaeron's roads, Lordaeron's problems. None of it was hers unless it came over her line or touched her stones or her riders. It hadn't. One stone with a limp in it wasn't an army.

"Tell them the trolls can count," she said, "and they'll count our bows before they count our coin."

"That's good. I'll use that." He glanced at her sideways. "You'll be there to say it yourself, of course."

"Of course."

He laughed quietly and let it go, keeping his horse at her mare's shoulder, the same half-length back he always rode. She didn't thank him for it. She never had.

She said nothing about the stone, rubbing her thumb across the cold place in her palm. The road went on east under the amber light, the hum running on beneath the hooves.

She heard the rider before anyone else did. A single horse, south and behind, coming up the track hard, hoofbeats too fast and too even for anyone who meant to stop politely. At her raised fist the patrol drew in around her without a word. Lor'themar pulled his bow off the saddle, nocking as he turned, and her own hand went to her string.

Then she knew the gait. Long-legged, a slight drag on the near hind. A Farstrider horse, and one that had been ridden too far.

He came round the bend at a dead gallop, hauling up so hard at the sight of them that the horse nearly sat down. The animal was dark with sweat from neck to flank, lather white where the reins had rubbed and between the hind legs. It stood with its legs splayed, sides heaving, foam hanging off the bit in ropes. The rider was young, his green cloak grey with road dust. Seeing her, he tried to salute and breathe at once, managing neither.

"Ranger-General." He dragged in air. "From the Pass. Ranger Cyndia sends—"

"Skip the compliments. What."

"Intruder. Outer ring, before dawn." He swallowed. "Human. A woman. Mage."

The patrol went quiet around her. Sylvanas didn't move. "Through the ring?"

"Through it. Nobody felt her cross." He wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist. "Night watch found her at first light. She was at a stone, Ranger-General. Down off the high road. Studying it. Had her hands on it, and papers spread out on the ground around her, and she didn't run when they came up on her."

"Papers. Any seal?"

"No seal, no escort, no letter. Nothing from Dalaran, nothing from the crown. She came quiet." He took another breath, and it shook. "Ranger Cyndia has her at the post. She asked me to tell you what the human said when they took her. Exactly what she said."

"Then tell me."

The runner looked at her straight for the first time. "She said the leylines are wrong."

Nobody laughed. Behind her, one of the horses blew, shaking its head until its rider hushed it.

Sylvanas had closed her right hand into a fist without meaning to. The cold was still there in the middle of it, under the glove, exactly where the stone had put it.

"Those were her words?"

"Those were her words, Ranger-General. Ranger Cyndia had me say them back to her twice."

Lor'themar lowered his bow across his knees, saying nothing. He was looking at her hand.

A human. A mage, through her ring in the dark without so much as a ripple, crouched at one of her stones with her papers out. A spy, then—Kirin Tor, sent to count the wards and see what they were worth. Then why stand there and let the rangers take her? Why say anything at all? What spy, caught at a ward-stone, looks up at a Farstrider's drawn bow and talks about leylines? One who wants to be caught. No—nonsense. One who's mad, then. Possibly. Or one who put her hand on a stone and felt what Sylvanas had felt at noon.

Sun's sake.

A stranger inside her border. Something wrong with her stones. And somewhere under all of it, a thought had already worked out that her Convocation bench would sit empty tomorrow. She caught herself liking that. Grinning over a breach, Ranger-General. She'd have had any ranger of hers mucking stalls till midsummer for less.

"Lor'themar."

"I know." He was already sliding the bow back into its loops. "I'll take the patrol north, and the chamber can have me instead. They like me better anyway; I don't make them look at the map."

"Tell them the Pass needs its full company."

"I'll collect the others off the ridge and tell them you were called off by something with teeth." He met her eyes, the joke going out of his face for a moment. "Sylvanas. Send word when you know what she is."

"When I know."

She gave him a nod, which was more than the chamber would get. He turned his horse, raising his arm. Then she looked at the runner's horse, still blowing, still splayed.

"Can he go on?"

"He'll go, Ranger-General."

"Then he goes. Keep up."

The patrol fell in behind Lor'themar in pairs, and she and the runner rode with them back up the track as far as the fork where the old road went north to Silvermoon. They split there. Behind her, the northern road ran soft and shaded under the trees, needles and leaf mould deep enough to muffle a cavalry column. For a while she heard the patrol on it, and then she didn't. Ahead, the southern track turned to packed earth and then to stone, climbing out of the woods in long switchbacks, the mare's hooves ringing. The air changed as they climbed. The sweetness went out of it first, then the warmth. By the high switchbacks the wind off the heights had snow somewhere in it, smelling of nothing but rock and distance.

The stones came up along the roadside at a steady pace, one and then the next and then the next. She was riding fast now, the hum of each one rising under the hooves and falling away before the next began. She tried to hear a limp in them, listening past the hoofbeats. She couldn't, not at a gallop—and whether that was because there was none, she didn't know.

Her right palm went against her thigh, hard, rubbing through the glove, but it didn't help. A mile on, she did it again.

The Pass post came into sight at the end of the light, a squat dark tower against a sky gone violet over the peaks. Lamps already burned in the gate, the outer stones standing in their line across the saddle of the pass below, grey on grey. Seeing her colours, the gate rangers dragged the bar back before she had to call for it. She rode through at a canter, the runner's horse labouring in behind her, and was out of the saddle before the mare had properly stopped.

"Where's Cyndia?" Her voice carried across the yard, turning every head in it. "Get her out here. Now."

Cyndia came out of the tower door while the last word still rang off the yard walls, settling her quiver strap as she walked. She stopped a pace short of Sylvanas, weight even on both feet, eyes straight on. She had been up since the night watch. It showed in the grey under her eyes. It showed in her shoulders, held a little too square, as if she'd decided hours ago that once they dropped they'd stay down. It showed nowhere in her voice.

"Ranger-General."

"Inside. Report as we go."

The guardroom at the Pass was built for winter. The walls were a yard thick, the stones sweating faintly where the hearth's warmth met the cold coming through them. Ward-lamps burned in iron brackets every few paces along both walls, leaving no corner a shadow worth the name. The light was white with blue at the edges, steady as noon. After the long dusk on the switchbacks it hit Sylvanas's eyes hard, and she walked into it narrow-eyed. Her thighs had stiffened over the last hour of the climb, the step down from the door jarring both of them.

Four rangers stood along the far wall, two more than the hour called for. Not one of them sat, though there were benches enough, and every one looked ready to trade a month's pay for one. They straightened as she came in—tired eyes, hands near their bows, an alertness held too long and gone brittle. On the table someone's supper sat abandoned in a wooden bowl, skin gone over the stew, a spoon standing in it.

"She came through the outer ring before dawn," Cyndia said, falling in at her shoulder. "Nobody on the wall felt her cross. The night watch found her at first light on the second ring. She was at the stone below the high road, the one with the split cap. She had both hands on it and her papers on the ground round her feet, weighted down with stones off the verge." She gave it in order, plainly, the way she'd read back sign on a trail. "The watch put an arrow on the string and told her to step away from the stone. She stepped away and showed her hands, and then she asked to stay."

"Asked."

"Argued, Ranger-General. She said she needed a day with that stone and the next ones along the ring. She said the stones were sick from the inside, and that our wardens would read them sound because they only read the outsides." Cyndia's left hand had come to rest near her quiver while she talked; it stayed there. "She said it twice. When the watch told her she'd be bound and carried if she didn't walk, she asked whether she could be bound at the stone."

Sick from the inside.

Sylvanas's right hand had closed again. She opened it, the cold sitting in the middle of the palm where it had sat all afternoon, small and constant.

A spy ran. Any spy worth his pay would have had the dark on his side and a road at his back. He'd have been halfway to Lordaeron before the night watch found a cold camp and a scuff in the dirt. This one had sat down in front of a drawn bow, asking to be tied to the stone. Arrogance, then. The Kirin Tor bred it the way Eversong bred horses, every one of them sure a border was a line on somebody else's map. Or she'd been frightened half out of her wits, talking to keep her teeth still—except frightened people ran too, and faster. Or she was right. Sylvanas didn't much care for that one either.

"Did she fight?"

"No. She talked the whole way up. About the stones." Cyndia paused, the only pause she'd taken. "I don't think she minded that we had her, Ranger-General. I think she minded that we took her off the stone."

"Her gear."

"On the rack. Staff, satchel, the papers. The staff's under a damper."

"Show me. Then her."

The rack stood in a stone alcove off the passage to the cells. The ward-light was harder there, the air smelling of lamp oil and cold iron. It was a plain frame bolted to the wall, with a dampening plate set into its back—grey slate carved in close lines. The lamplight went flat a hand's width from it. Across two hooks lay the staff. Plain ash, the grain dark where hands had worn it. Silver wire bound the grip in close, even turns, a binding rather than an ornament, rubbed bright on the inside of the wrap. At the head sat a crystal the size of a hen's egg. Under the damper it had gone a dull violet. Now and then the colour moved in it, slowly, and settled.

The satchel had been turned out onto the shelf below and laid in rows, the way Cyndia laid out everything. A tin of ink with the lid screwed down tight. Pens wrapped in oilcloth. A field-book swollen with damp, the pages cockled. Two pairs of stockings, darned at the heels, badly. A heel of bread gone hard as bark. A small brass compass. A flat grey pebble, too smooth for a riverbed, one face cut with a single rune, wrapped in a twist of cloth. Cyndia's file lay open at the end of the shelf, the Farstrider seal on its cover. On the open page someone had drawn a staff in careful charcoal.

And the map.

Somebody had spread it flat and pinned the corners with lead weights. It was human paper, thick and soft, furred at the edges and folded so often the creases had gone white. Every line on it was drawn by hand. Sylvanas leaned in, throwing her shadow across half the sheet, and shifted until it cleared.

The ward-stones were there. Not every one of them, but more than any human had a right to know. Each was a small neat dot, strung along the outer ring and then the second, curving where the border curved. Some of the dots had been circled, and some twice. The second ring was always darker, pressed hard enough to dent the paper. From the bottom edge of the sheet—from the south, from Lordaeron—thin straight lines ran up the paper, ruled, each one ending at a circled stone. In the lower corner the ink had smeared into a long grey thumbprint. Somebody had held the sheet down against the wind with one hand, drawing with the other.

A surveyor marked every stone; a surveyor was paid by the stone. A raider marked the ones he meant to break and drew the roads in to them. These had roads. Then why carry it over the border in her satchel and spread it on the verge for the first ranger along to read? Because she was a fool. Because she wasn't. Sylvanas had no idea which, and the not knowing sat sour in her stomach.

The high road was there. So was the Pass, a small square with a flag drawn on it, which was almost charming. Her eye followed the outer ring east along the southern track to a bend round a shoulder of rock, the track dropping toward a dry creek bed. Whoever had drawn it had taken the trouble to put in the creek, a wavering line of dashes. There was a dot at the bend. There was a ring of ink around the dot. Past it, the ring ran on east over the ground she'd ridden that afternoon.

Sylvanas put her finger beside it. Not on it. Under the glove her palm went cold, or she only thought it did; that was the trouble now. She couldn't tell.

At noon she'd stood there with her hand flat on that stone, the moss against her knuckles. And somewhere south of here, days ago or weeks, by lamplight in some tower room, a human had drawn a circle round it.

How? You didn't circle a stone from Lordaeron. You came and put your hand on it, and nobody felt you cross. Or somebody told you which one to circle, and that was worse. Or—

"Kirin Tor ink," Cyndia said at her side, touching the edge of the map where the lines ran thickest. "It dries violet at the edges. Ours dries black. The staff matches the one in our file, the wire and the crystal both." She turned the file a quarter round on the shelf so the charcoal staff faced Sylvanas. "It's Jaina Proudmoore, Ranger-General. Antonidas's apprentice."

Sylvanas looked at the drawing, then at the staff on its hooks. The dim crystal moved its colour and went still again.

She knew the file. She'd read it last winter at her own desk, a cup going cold at her elbow. She'd read it like every file on human mages near her border, filing it under people who'd one day be a nuisance. Antonidas's apprentice. Gifted beyond her years, a Farstrider had written, in a flat hand that didn't hand out words like gifted. A lord admiral's daughter. The archmage's favourite, the one he brought to council. A name worth watching.

Well, she'd watched it walk into her post. Damn the old man. Damn Dalaran and every tower in it.

"Where is she?"

"End of the passage. Second door."

On the walk from the rack she built the prisoner out of the file and the ink. Good violet wool with gold at the hem. Hair done up. Clean hands folded. A face arranged into patient courtesy, the way the magisters arranged theirs in chamber when they meant to talk you into the floor. Somewhere about her, a letter with a seal on it, held back until the moment it would do the most harm. A nuisance with a pedigree. A morning lost to apologies, an escort to the border—and a sharp letter north from Silvermoon that would take a month to answer itself.

The ranger on the second door saw her coming and stood off it. Two more filled the passage behind, and Cyndia stopped at her shoulder.

"Open it."

The bar came up, and the door swung in on a room barely wider than itself. Stone on four sides and overhead. A ward-lamp in a bracket threw the same hard white-blue light as the guardroom. One wooden chair. A bucket. To the right of the door three iron pegs jutted from the wall at shoulder height, empty. They were for a prisoner's staff and cloak and belt, if a prisoner were let keep them. Across from the door, a ward-stone the size of a loaf was set into the wall at the height of a seated head. Grey, gold in the runes, humming.

The mage sat in the chair.

She was young. Even for a human she was young; there was a roundness still in the face that the rest of her had outgrown. The lamp laid cold light along her cheekbones from above, digging the shadows out under her eyes until they looked like bruises. Her hair had been braided once, fair, but most of it had come loose and hung down. Her travelling robe was brown wool, kilted up for walking, worn shiny at the cuffs and elbows, the hem stiff with dried mud. The mud went to the knee on her trousers too. Her boots were good boots, walked further than their maker ever expected, cracked across the toes. The left one was tied with a knot that wasn't the knot it had come with.

No violet. No gold. Nothing done up.

Her hands lay on her knees, the knuckles on both red and raw. The skin was split and peeling the way Sylvanas had seen it on rangers coming off a winter watch at the high posts—frost-nip. Nothing on the road last night had come near cold enough for that. The fingers of the right hand were black-violet with ink in every crease.

And her face. Sylvanas knew that face. She'd met it in her own glass after budget sessions. The face of someone who'd been saying one thing since before dawn, to people who wrote it down and put it in a drawer. The jaw clamped so hard the muscle stood out under the ear. The mouth a flat line. Frightened people looked at the door. This one was looking at Sylvanas, the anger in that jaw gone past shouting into something quiet and fixed. She looked tired enough that it was the only thing keeping her upright in the chair.

Sylvanas didn't like that face any better on a human.

A fool in silk she could have sent home with an escort and a cold letter. This was Antonidas's apprentice, a lord admiral's daughter, frost-burned and filthy in an elven cell without a seal or a letter or a guard. If she'd come to harm on the road, Dalaran would hear of it. If she came to harm here, Dalaran would hear of it and Kul Tiras with it—and Silvermoon would learn about it from them. And if she was right about the stones—

Bad ground. Soft on every side, and a famous name standing in the middle of it.

The mage looked her over. Her eyes went to the bow first, then the quiver, then the green and gold at Sylvanas's shoulder, stopping there long enough to read them. They came up to her face and stayed there, and they did not drop. Sylvanas had been sized up by trolls with less care.

Then the mage's eyes slid away from her, drifting left to the ward-stone in the wall. A small frown came between her brows and went. Her eyes came back to Sylvanas. A breath later they went to the stone again, and the frown came back deeper. It was the frown a person gives a noise in the next room that might be a mouse and might not.

Sylvanas listened, holding still on the threshold. The stone hummed the way the roadside stones had hummed under the hooves, low and even. She couldn't hear anything wrong in it—not from a doorway, with the lamp hissing overhead and rangers breathing at her back. Her palm remembered moss, a warm face of stone, a beat that came late and then late again.

What did the human hear? What made a mage frown at a sound stone? Or was it sound? She'd ridden past twenty stones since noon and couldn't swear to one of them.

The mage's hands stayed flat on her knees, ink and raw knuckles in the cold light. Her boots were planted square on the floor, heels and toes both, like someone braced on a deck in a swell. She wasn't going to get up; she wasn't going to speak first either.

Behind Sylvanas nobody moved. She could feel them waiting on her—the ranger at the door, Cyndia at her shoulder, the two in the passage. She knew what for. Shut the door. Let the human sit the night in the cold and be softer for it by morning. Or go in.

A night would take some of the anger off her, but it would also take a night.

Send her home, then, and the map went south with her, ink ring and all, and nobody ever asked it how. No. Hold her for a magister, and wait a fortnight on a lens and a clerk. Sun's sake—a fortnight. The whole ring could be limping by then.

She wanted to hand it off, badly enough to taste. A rider north tonight, and this could be some magister's evening ruined instead of hers—someone who knew the words. She wasn't going to. She'd known it since the runner opened his mouth on the forest road. She'd walked the length of this passage knowing it. Her jaw ached from holding it.

And Antonidas: had he sent her? Did he even know where she was? And the mage had to be exactly what the file said. Strong enough to cross the ring without a ripple. Antonidas's to the bone—Kirin Tor ink in the creases of her fingers. She couldn't even do Sylvanas the courtesy of being a fool in silk.

She stepped into the cell.

The rangers drew back from the doorway, giving her room, their boots scraping on stone. Cyndia stayed where she was. The lamplight caught Sylvanas's boots as she crossed the floor, laying blue along the dusty toes. She stopped in front of the chair, close enough that the mage had to tip her head back to keep looking at her. The mage did.

The stone hummed in the wall, and out in the guardroom someone set a cup down on the table. Neither of them said anything for a long while. Sylvanas let it go on, leaving the human to sit in it. The human sat in it, and she didn't look away.

"Start talking."
