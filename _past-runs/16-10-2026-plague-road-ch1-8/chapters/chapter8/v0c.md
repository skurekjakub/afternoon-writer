Jaina had the cold gathered in the two bare fingertips of her left hand, holding it there until it ached under the nails like a bad tooth. Each breath through her nose brought in the dye vats on Tenter Row, a sour stink that sat at the back of her throat; she breathed through her mouth instead and tasted them anyway.

She stood at the corner of the back lane with her shoulder against the lean-to and the staff wedged in the crook of her dead arm, the crystal at its head as dark as a lump of coal. Corin waited behind her, breathing through his mouth too, the buckle of his satchel ticking now and then against the boards. Havel had the corner itself, his back to the stone and his truncheon in his hand. Sylvanas kept to the far end of the lean-to where the lamplight gave out, watching the lane.

It was the lane from the back of Sylvanas's sheet, and Jaina had not expected a sketch done in lead and soot to be so exact: a long straight box with a lamp at each end, a square at the bottom and nothing in between. Loading doors ran down both sides, all of them barred, and the cobbles between them were white with dust. At the far end, on the left, a row of small windows burned yellow along Kett's upper floor. A moth was going at the glass of the near lamp over and over, with a dry little tap each time.

Across the mouth of the lane, at the height of her knees, somebody had strung a ward.

She had taken it for the lamp at first. It had the same steady warmth, but the lamp hung over her head and this sat against her shins in a line from the corner stone at Havel's back to the wall opposite, humming. Her own wards had always hummed like that, low and warm from the inside. It was a tell, first-year work, but whoever had tied this one had sunk both ends into the mortar and closed them off clean, leaving no loose end for a careless hand to catch. It would let the big loader walk through.

"Ranger-General."

Sylvanas turned her head.

"There's a ward across the mouth of the lane," Jaina said. "Knee high, from this corner to that one. If anyone but him walks through it, somebody hears."

Sylvanas studied the place. There was nothing there to see, only the lamplight and the white dust and the barred door opposite, but she went over it from end to end anyway, measuring it as if for a jump.

"Who hears?"

"Whoever tied it. I can't tell you where he's sitting."

"Can you put it out?"

"Not and keep his jaw," Jaina said, lifting the two bare fingertips an inch.

Sylvanas tipped her head back toward the roofs.

"Lady Proudmoore." Corin was so close behind her that his whisper stirred the hair on her neck. "I've— the damper. From the sample-room door. I brought it in case the loft door had something on it, I thought if the hasp was warded we'd—" He got the satchel flap up with a clink of brass and held the thing out past her shoulder: a wedge of grey slate as long as his hand, gold run into the grooves cut along it. "I'll put it back in the morning."

She leaned away from it, the cold in her fingertips gone sluggish at once. "Keep it off me. Low, on the corner stone. Your left, where the mortar's dark. Lower. There."

He crouched and set the point of the wedge into the joint, pushing it home with the heel of his hand. The warmth against her shins thinned from the corner outward and went. She waited for it to flare with her teeth set. It didn't.

Would the man at the other end hear it go quiet? Only if he was listening. Was he? She had no way to know that from a street corner, and neither did anyone else standing on it.

"It's out," she said. "Thank you, Corin."

He straightened, wiping his inky hand down the front of his coat.

"Ranger-General," Havel said. "If it's to be tonight, let me at least send a lad for the magistrate's clerk. If he's stood at the bend with a lantern when we bring the fellow past—"

Sylvanas already had a hand on the rim of the water butt. "When he comes out, let him walk," she said to Jaina. "I'll take him where the lamps don't reach. Come when I call."

She climbed the butt and the slope of the lean-to, then caught the edge of the main roof on the heel of her hand and her two sound fingers, keeping the two that Tam had bitten curled in out of the way. Her boots crossed the gutter without a sound, and then she was over the ridge and gone.

"Right," Havel said, putting the truncheon back through his belt. He glanced up at the place where she had gone, then down at Jaina. "And who's on your back, my lady, with her up there?"

"Corin."

"The lad." He looked past her at Corin and the satchel. "Stay close on her, then."

"Yes, Captain."

Jaina leaned round the end of the lean-to as far as she dared, watching the yellow windows. One man. She had read all the bags on Corin's shelves today, the slips at the river depot before that and the roads from Millhaven down before those, and none of them had told her more than heavy or clean. A sack couldn't tell her who had stood over it with a crock. It couldn't tell her who had filled the crock, or who had shown him how. A man could, if he were asked. A man had a mouth.

And a collar.

Down the lane, iron scraped on iron. Kett's door opened a hand's width, throwing lamplight out across the dust, and a big man edged out sideways through the gap. He pulled the door to behind him and swung the long iron hasp down over its staple; it went home with a clank she heard from the corner. He had a pale leather pad on one shoulder, no mask and a beard she would have called brown, though the lamp turned everything yellow. It was the man from Sylvanas's report, pad and beard and all. He didn't look up at the roofs or back at the windows. He started up the middle of the lane at an easy walk, his boots printing the white dust. She drew her head back behind the lean-to and kept still, shoulder to the boards.

Where the lamps don't reach—halfway down, between the two. She listened to his boots come on.

The cold had gone sluggish again—no, that was the damper, still in the wall a stride from her knee. She shifted a step along the boards away from it, the ache coming back under her nails sharp and clean. It was the last spell she had tonight, and she would do the sums on that tomorrow, with Corin holding the pen. The boots were loud enough now that she'd have seen his face if she had looked.

Then came a sound like a sack dropped off a tailboard, and a grunt cut off short, and boots scraping in the grit, and nothing at all.

"Proudmoore." It wasn't loud.

Jaina rounded the corner into the lamplight, the staff knocking on the cobbles. Where the ward had been, nothing hummed. Halfway down the lane Sylvanas was already coming toward her, walking the man out in front of her fast with a fist in his hair, dragging his head back until his beard pointed at the sky. Her other hand had his right arm wrenched up between his shoulder blades. His boots skidded and caught and skidded in the dust. He was making a noise in his throat, and his chin was nowhere near his collar.

Jaina had asked for one man close enough to touch. Sylvanas brought him the last few strides at a run and stopped him in front of her with his throat bare. He was taller than she'd thought. She reached up past his beard and set her two fingertips into the soft place under his ear where the jaw hinges. Then she let the cold go.

It went out of her all at once. His teeth came together with a click she heard. Frost whitened the beard around his mouth, and the muscle at the hinge of his jaw stood up under her fingertips hard as a knuckle and stayed there. The noise in his throat rose. The cold took her own arm to the elbow, then to the shoulder, and her teeth started going.

Under the sweat on his neck was the loft: faint and wet, the old-coin taste she'd been reading off dry bags all day, fresh on him from the top of that stair. She had wanted the boards while they were still wet and had got a man's neck with a little of the boards on it instead. She kept her fingertips where they were.

Sylvanas let go of his hair and drew her belt knife. She slid the point in under his collar at the back of his neck, cut the band round close to the skin and pulled it off him whole. In the cut hem a fold of waxed paper showed yellow, sewn in. The one in Tam's collar had been empty. Sylvanas ran her thumb down both his cuffs and the front of his shirt and slit one cuff hem to be sure of it. Then she took a cord from her belt and bound his wrists in front of him, twice round and through.

"Done."

Jaina took her fingers away, white to the second joint and past feeling.

"Ranger-General." Cyndia's voice dropped low from the chimney stack across from the loft, though when Jaina looked up there was only the stack against the sky. "Nobody came to the windows. The loft lamp's still lit. They're still at the sacks."

"Stay on them until we're past the bend. Then come."

"Ranger-General."

Havel had come into the mouth of the lane, taking in the man's face, the frost going to water in his beard, the collar band in Sylvanas's fist. "You'll not walk him to the post across the Exchange square with his face like that," he said. "There's a store of ours a minute off Hurdle Lane, behind the chandler's. Lamp oil and rope, a bar on the door, and nobody comes by after dark but my lads." He was already turning toward Tenter Row. "This way's quickest. My lads have the end of it."

Sylvanas shifted her grip on the cord. "Lead."

Nobody argued, and Jaina's teeth were going too hard for her to try.

At the corner Corin stooped for the slate wedge.

"Leave it," Jaina said through her teeth. "It's keeping the lane quiet."

They went back into Tenter Row with Havel leading, the dye stink thickening toward the bend, where Lyriel stepped out of the angle of a wall with an arrow on the string.

"Clear behind. Nobody's come down the row since you went in." She looked at Jaina's hand. "Jaina, you're steaming. You look like a cart horse in January."

Jaina couldn't get an answer past her teeth. Lyriel took her left arm above the elbow and fell in beside her.

Two of Havel's men held the Hurdle Lane end with a hooded lantern on the cobbles between them. "Nobody comes down after us, lads," Havel said, and they stepped aside to let the party through, closing up again behind.

The store was a minute along Hurdle Lane, through a gate in a yard wall and across a yard with a pump in it, in at a door in the back of the chandler's; Havel had the key on a thong at his belt. Inside was one low room that smelled of lamp oil. Spare lanterns stood along a shelf with their glasses cleaned, above coils of rope hung from pegs. There was a cold brazier in one corner and a table with two stools at it. The one window had its shutter across. Havel lit a tallow candle at the tinderbox and set it on the table, filling the room with the smell of mutton fat, then dropped the bar across the door.

Corin set his back against it. Lyriel stayed out in the yard by the pump, and Sylvanas had sent Cyndia up onto the chandler's roof, over the street door.

Sylvanas pushed the man down onto one of the stools, which had a short leg and knocked on the boards whenever he shifted. Havel pulled the other one out for Jaina. She sat without arguing about that either and leaned the staff against her knee, pushing her white fingertips into the armpit of her dead arm, against her own side. Nothing came back from them at all.

The frost in the man's beard had gone to water and was running. She watched his jaw. The muscle at the hinge loosened by degrees until his mouth came open on its own, a string of spit running down into his beard. He worked his jaw from side to side like a man who has been hit.

Then he jerked his chin down, fast, to where his collar should have been. He got only cut shirt and his own bare skin, and stopped there.

The collar was the danger. She had taken his jaw before he could get his chin to it, and now the collar was in Sylvanas's fist. Quick enough, this time. With Tam she'd been too slow by the length of a lane, and he'd had his chin on his collar all the way down it.

Sylvanas laid the collar band on the table between the candle and the man, the fold of waxed paper showing yellow in its cut hem. Then she set both hands flat on the boards on either side of it and said nothing.

The man stared at it, sweat beading in a row along his upper lip. He licked it off, and it came back. The cord had bitten white grooves into his wrists.

"Right, then." Havel moved round to the man's shoulder. "Let's have your name, friend, and where you lodge."

The man kept his mouth shut.

"I'm the law on these lanes. You talk to me and you'll go up before a magistrate in daylight, with a clerk writing it down. That's a door that opens, for a sensible man."

The man's eyes went to Havel and stayed on him a moment, then returned to the collar. Havel was the only one in the room who had said magistrate. Jaina would have looked at him too.

"Fenner's," the man said. His voice came out thick, as if his tongue had swollen. "I load at Fenner's."

"We know where you load."

He gave them nothing more. The candle guttered, and the flame lay down and stood up again. Sylvanas hadn't moved.

Jaina had expected threats, but there weren't any. Sylvanas leaned over a strip of cut cloth with her hands flat either side of it and let him look; his eyes kept going back to it, the sweat welling again on his lip.

Sylvanas could have his eyes. Jaina took the staff in her left fist, since her fingertips were no use for anything, setting the heel down on the boards by his boot. Up through it came the loft on his skin, still wet, and his blood going fast—nothing else. There was no ward on him and no tell, nothing tied to him that she could hear. If anything on him answered to anybody, it would come up the staff before it came out of his mouth.

"You didn't bite," Sylvanas said.

His breath caught.

"They'll hear that you didn't."

"They'll know." It broke out of him in a rush. "They know already. They always know where we—" He shut his mouth on the rest.

Then he leaned toward Sylvanas as far as the cord would let him. "Let me go back. Let me go back up the lane, I'll say I fell asleep in a doorway, I'll say I was took ill—"

"No."

He sat back, the stool knocking under him.

"I carry," he said, to the collar. "That's all I do. Word down from the loft to the yard, and word up from the walls to the loft."

"From the walls," Sylvanas said.

"There's places. Cracks. I put a thing in, or I take a thing out, and I don't see who's been. I'm not meant to see."

"Names," Havel said. "The five upstairs at Kett's. The factor that signs for Fenner's—where does he sleep in this town?"

"We don't have names."

"Every man in Andorhal's got a name, and I've heard most of them."

"Not up there."

"Then where do they lie up? There's a house besides the rope-walk. There's always a house."

The man shook his head.

His town. His names and his houses and his magistrate in daylight—Havel wanted something he could put a lock on by morning and would keep asking until somebody gave it him. Jaina wanted to know who had taught a loft full of men to take a glove off.

"The men in the loft," she said. Her voice was hoarse, but her teeth had nearly stopped. "They take their gloves off by the cuff, without touching the outsides. They wash to the elbow before they drink. Who showed them how?"

For the first time he looked at her properly: her face, the staff in her fist, the dark crystal.

"Shown," he said. "Everyone's shown."

"By whom?"

"The one with the crock. He shows you the once, and you do it his way after."

"Does he tell them what's in it?"

"Nobody's told what's in it." He licked his lip. "You're told what to do with it. Arm in, work it through, sew it up, wash after. You don't need more than that to do it."

"Does he know? The one with the crock?"

"More than me." The stool knocked. "Not all of it. Nobody down here knows all of it."

His blood kicked at that. It came up the staff into her palm, one quick hard jump, the way it hadn't for the crock or the gloves. So it was whatever sat above the crock that frightened him, more than the poison or the man who mixed it. She held the heel steady on the boards, listening under the loft for anything tied to him, a thread with somebody else's hand on the end of it. There was still nothing, and she counted that in her favour.

"Then who does?"

He looked at the collar and didn't answer. Sylvanas touched one finger to the cut band and slid it an inch across the boards toward him. He jerked back from it so hard that the short leg knocked twice.

"One," he said. "There's one over all of it."

"One man," Jaina said. Under the staff heel his blood kicked again, harder.

"A man. Yes."

"Since when?" Sylvanas said.

"Since before the thaw. Before the snow, near enough. He had it written down before any of us came here. Every yard. Every road out."

Written down. Somebody had sat at a table last winter with a pen and laid a whole city's granaries out the way she would lay out the lattice for a ward, seam by seam. The face she'd stopped at on the road surfaced; she put it down again and kept her eyes on the man. Behind her, Corin's boots shifted on the boards.

"That's as may be," Havel said, "but it's the rope-walk key I want, and the man who holds it, and—"

"Captain," Sylvanas said, and he stopped.

Jaina leaned forward over the staff. "Who is he?"

The man's eyes were on her staff. "Learned," he said. "Learned where you were."

"Where I—"

"They called him—"

His lips were already shaped for the start of it. She could almost hear the shape of the word before the word, and the shape mattered: a title she could run down in a day, a name she could run down faster. She was listening with everything she had left, through the staff and through her teeth. If there was anything on him to hear, she'd hear it before the word was out. She would be fast enough this time.

The cold came through the wall behind him.

It was thin as a needle. It drove straight through the plaster and the shelf of lanterns from somewhere outside, laying the candle flame over flat as it passed, though nobody had opened a door. Jaina's head was up and turning toward the wall, and it was already in him.

It was already finished.

His mouth stayed open on the half of the word he'd started. The breath left him through his beard, a long one that didn't come back, his eyes still on her staff. Then his head went forward. Sylvanas reached across the table and caught him by the shoulder before his face could go down onto the collar, the stool knocking once on its short leg and going still.

Jaina pressed the heel of the staff against his boot, listening, though there was nothing left in him to listen for. The loft was still on his skin, loud and wet, the same as in the lane. Under it—where she had heard nothing in the lane, because the loft was so loud—lay a small closed place, tied off clean with its seams all shut, and it was open now and empty.

It had been in him in the lane, under her fingertips.

A ward. A faster one, if she'd had one up on him— She'd had nothing up. She had spent the last of it on his jaw and been pleased with herself all the way here. And a ward against what, from where? It had come through a wall from somebody she couldn't point to; she'd felt it go in at the moment it was done, and not a moment sooner. It had come from close, though. Somebody in this city had reached him through that plaster. Then they knew which room he was in.

Learned where you were. They called him—

His face was right there under Sylvanas's hand, slack, the frost-water still in his beard, and she was listening to half a sentence.

Oh, Light.

Sylvanas let him go. She was at the shutter before he'd finished sliding off the stool, her bow off her shoulder and an arrow on the string, her head turned to the gap. The body slumped against the table leg, shaking the candle.

Corin had gone white to the lips. He had his back flat to the barred door, working the satchel buckle open and shut with his thumb.

Havel's face had gone the colour of the candle. He was staring at the yard door, the one they had all come in by. Then he turned to Jaina and started talking, fast.

"We'll have you out of here, my lady. We'll go through the shop. We've a room over the farrier's on Bell Street, nobody goes up there but me, it's ours, we'll have you in it with the door shut before they've—" There was a dark line of sweat along his collar. He smiled at her, and the smile stayed on his face after he'd stopped talking.

"Proudmoore," Sylvanas said from the shutter. "On your feet."

Jaina got the staff into the crook of her dead arm and stood. Havel had the inner door open before she had her feet under her. Beyond it the chandler's shop lay dark, smelling of cold tallow, and she could make out the counter and a pole hung with candles in pairs by their wicks.

"Through the shop, my lady." He was still talking fast. "Out the front onto Bell Street, and the farrier's is forty yards down on the left. We'll go up his outside stair and bar it behind us, and I'll send a lad running for the rest of mine. We'll sit tight there till it's light."

"A moment, Captain." She set the heel of the staff on the boards between her boots, listening.

The dead man was nearest. The loft was still on him, so loud through the wood that she had to listen past it, out through the dark shop to the street beyond its shutters, the cold climbing the staff into the crook of her elbow and lodging in her back teeth while she did it. Then she had it. Out on Bell Street was the same wet taste she'd had off his neck in the lane—more than one man's worth, and nearer now than when she'd started listening.

"Not the street," she said. "There's more of what's on him out there." She tipped her head at the man on the floor. "On the Bell Street side, past the shop. It's coming this way."

Sylvanas didn't turn from the shutter. "Yard."

"The yard, then. Right you are." Havel shut the shop door on the tallow smell. "Out the gate onto Hurdle Lane and up to the Row end, where my lads are, and from there it's a straight walk to the post. The post has a door that bars and men behind it. That's the lane I know best in this town, Ranger-General. We'll have her inside before they know we've gone."

Sylvanas left the shutter. She took the cut collar band off the table and pushed it into her belt, then lifted the bar off the yard door and propped it against the wall without a sound.

Lyriel waited by the pump with an arrow on the string, watching the gate. Somebody had hung a tin cup from the spout on a bit of string, and a drop was gathering on its rim. Jaina's mouth was as dry as the cobbles. She looked at the cup and walked past it.

Cyndia dropped off the chandler's eaves into the yard, landing with her knees bent and her bow held out clear. "Ranger-General. The lamp at the Bell Street corner went out. Then two men came round the corner and stood at the farrier's gate. Then the singing in the Wheatsheaf stopped." She stood with her weight even and her eyes on Sylvanas. "The carts are still going on the Exchange road."

"Then it's the post," Havel said. "Up the lane."

"Cyndia," Sylvanas said. "Back up. Keep level with us on the roofs, and watch where they go."

"Ranger-General."

Cyndia sprang from the pump trough to the wall top, from the wall to the gutter, then over the ridge.

Sylvanas opened the gate a hand's width onto Hurdle Lane, but nothing moved out there. Jaina stepped up at her shoulder and planted the heel of the staff in the dust of the gateway, and the cold rose through it again, worse. Up the lane, toward the Row end and the post beyond it, the taint lay heavier than it had on Bell Street—a great deal heavier, and holding still. Behind them, past the shop, the Bell Street lot were still moving.

"Up the lane's the worst of it," she said. "Toward the post. It isn't moving."

"We'll keep to the wall, my lady." Havel was close behind her. "My lads have the Row end. I'll have them close up behind us as we pass."

Sylvanas had her head tipped back. Jaina followed her line and saw nothing there but the hoist beams over the lane with their slack ropes, the edges of the roofs black against a sky not much lighter than they were. Then Sylvanas was out through the gate and across Hurdle Lane at a run, bent low, keeping to the crown of the road where the wheels had worn the dust thin. She ducked into the black mouth of a loading lane on the far side.

Jaina crossed after her with Corin at her dead side. Halfway across she saw the heel of the staff leaving a neat round hole in the dust every other step, so she lifted it and carried it crossways the rest of the way.

The loading lane ran between two granaries whose walls went right to the corner, so that she couldn't see a stride into it until she stood in it. At the first turn Sylvanas stopped her with the back of her bow hand laid flat against her collarbone. Jaina hadn't heard anything, but she froze because the hand was there. A moment later a cart came backing out of a yard ahead of them with its axle grinding, the carter at his horse's head with a sack folded over his hair and shoulders against the dust. He backed it across the turn and on out of sight, and the axle went on grinding long enough for all of them to get across behind it.

At the far end of the lane, where loading doors stood barred under a wall higher than her head, Jaina grounded the staff again.

What had been standing up Hurdle Lane had come apart. Some of it held where it had been, up toward the post, but some was coming down the lane behind them toward the chandler's yard, and close—level with the mouth of the loading lane, or nearly.

"Behind," she said. "In Hurdle Lane. Coming down."

Sylvanas pressed her hand flat to the stone footing of the granary on their left. "Under."

The granary stood on stone feet a forearm high, a black gap under its floor between them. Lyriel slid in first, flat on her belly, and turned round to take the staff from Corin. Jaina went in on her left elbow and knees, dragging her dead arm through dust and chaff and old cobweb until she lay on her side with her cheek in the dirt. Corin crawled in behind her breathing hard through his nose, and Havel squeezed in after him, grunting, too broad for it. Sylvanas came last, and Jaina heard her stop at the edge of the gap and lay the bow along the ground in front of her.

The underside of the floor was close enough above her head to touch. Something trickled out of a crack in it onto her neck and ran down inside her collar, dry and light. Barley. She'd be shaking barley out of her clothes for a week.

Light spilled into the mouth of the loading lane.

It was a lantern with its hood half down, carried low at the height of a man's knee; above it she could see nothing but boots and the hem of a dark coat. The boots stopped in the lane mouth, and the lantern swung out over the dust from one wall to the other. Carts and loaders had trodden that dust all day, the carter and his horse just now, and their own prints went in among the rest. She watched the light go over them and come back and go over them again.

The residue was close enough now to sit in her mouth, copper and the loft, off the man with the lantern and two more behind him that she couldn't see. The cold climbed out of the staff into her jaw until she had to set her teeth to keep them from knocking. Behind her Corin held his breath, and up the lane the cart's axle was still grinding.

The boots turned back out into Hurdle Lane, taking the light with them. She listened through the staff as all three carried on toward the chandler's yard, the taste thinning behind them.

"They've gone by." Havel's mouth was right at her ear, close enough that she could smell his sweat and his wool coat. "Now, Ranger-General, while their backs are to us. Back out into the lane and straight up to the post." Against the grey of the lane mouth his hand came up off the dirt with two fingers held out flat together, pointing back the way they had come and on toward the post—the way he'd pointed down the canvas map at the post.

The staff heel was still in the dirt. She didn't have to listen hard: up that way, toward the post, what had been standing still stood there yet, thicker than at any time that night.

Bell Street, and now the post. Twice he'd pointed, and twice it had been where the taste was strongest. Well, he was pointing at his own ground, at his lads and his barred door. Where would anyone wait but on the road a sensible captain would take? So that was nothing. Or it was— The barley had worked down to the small of her back and the cold had her by the jaw, while up the lane the taint sat and waited. She could tell which side of her it was on, and roughly how far. She couldn't have said whether it stood in a doorway or a yard or on a roof. Sylvanas could. Jaina had watched her look into each corner tonight before she went round it.

"Ranger-General." She kept it to a breath. "Not up there. That's the worst of it, and it's standing still."

Sylvanas reached past Corin to lay her hand flat on Havel's back, stopping him with his elbows already under him to crawl out. "Through the yards."

"The gates on this side are barred at night, every one. You'll not get—"

"Lyriel. The captain."

Lyriel turned herself round in the dust behind Havel without a word and stayed there, watching his back.

Havel let his breath go. "We'll go your way, then. We'll go careful. We'll be all right, the lot of us."

They went over the wall at the end of the lane. Lyriel made a step of her hands and put Jaina up onto the coping as if she weighed nothing, Corin passing the staff up after her. Sylvanas was waiting on the far side to take her weight when she came down. Beyond lay a granary yard, empty under its hoist, the dust in it raked into lines. They kept to its edge on the stone of the gutter and cut through an open cart shed that smelled of axle grease and horse, coming out at the back into a passage between houses so narrow that the staff knocked both walls if she let it swing. The shutters were closed all along it, without a line of light at any of them.

Sylvanas stopped at the far end of the passage, where it opened onto a wider street, checking the roofline opposite before she looked anywhere else.

Jaina set the staff down and had it at once, without having to listen for it. It was across the street and both ways along it, in more places than she could separate, all of it standing still. Nobody was running about down there, and nobody was shouting. Whoever had been at the other end of that needle had sent men to stand in doorways, and they had gone and stood in them.

Havel had worked his way up beside Sylvanas on the right, Lyriel a hand behind him, his eyes on the lamp at the far corner up the street—the way to the post.

"Both ways," Jaina said. "In the doorways, all along."

Sylvanas began to turn.

Havel went.

He broke out of the passage mouth at a run, faster than she would have thought a man that broad could move. He pounded up the middle of the street toward the lamp at the corner, shouting. "This way! The post—it's clear, it's clear—lad, bring her—"

Corin took hold of her sleeve. All night he had kept his hand a finger's width from it, but now he had a fistful of the cloth and didn't move.

The bowstring went beside her ear.

Havel's shout stopped. He took one more stride before his legs went out from under him. He pitched down on his face in the white dust with his arms flung out, sliding a little. Then he lay still, the arrow standing up out of his back between the shoulders.

Under the lamp the wet moved, all of it at once, and somebody up the street called, "Now—"

The doors up there were already opening. Men spilled out into the lamplight with their faces wrapped to the eyes, carrying long knives and the iron crooks of sack hooks. They charged down the street, passing Havel without a look.

"Back!" Sylvanas said, loud for the first time that night. "Yard!"

Jaina didn't remember turning round. She was in the passage with the walls going by, the staff knocking off one and then the other, Corin hauling at her sleeve and elbow. Somewhere behind her Lyriel's bowstring snapped twice. Then they were in the cart shed with its grease smell. Halfway through, the taint rose hard on her left out of the dark at the back of the shed, sudden as a hand laid over her face.

"Left—"

Sylvanas had turned left before the word was out, the bow already drawn. A man lunged from behind a cart with his knife up. He took the arrow in the chest two strides off, the knife skittering away over the cobbles. Sylvanas stepped over him without looking down.

She'd said it before he was there, and Sylvanas had turned before she'd finished saying it. And back in the street Havel lay in the dust with his arms out, and she— Corin was pulling at her arm. Later.

Sylvanas didn't take them back over the wall. She led them left along the foot of it to a wicket gate in the corner that Jaina hadn't seen on the way in. She lifted the bar off it and slipped through. Behind them, back in the passage, a blade rang on the cobbles, and boots came on.

Lyriel brought up the rear, walking backward with an arrow on the string. Jaina felt it come up behind her, close and fast, on the right.

"Down—"

Lyriel dropped to one knee. Something hummed over her head and struck the gatepost by Jaina's shoulder with a crack. It stuck there: a sack hook, its point sunk an inch into the wood. Lyriel loosed from the knee into the dark of the yard, and out there somebody fell and didn't get up.

"By the hoist. Down." She ducked through the wicket and shut it and dropped the bar on their side.

Then they ran, and Sylvanas took them right and left and right again, down lanes so blind that Jaina lost any idea which way she was facing. At one corner she felt the wet ahead, close. She had barely said "Stop" before Sylvanas was flat against the wall with her arm out across the rest of them. A man ran past the end of the lane and on without turning his head.

At the next corner another turned into the lane in front of them and pulled up short. An arrow out of the dark overhead took him, folding him over the shafts of a handcart. Jaina never saw Cyndia, but she saw the fletching, pale against his coat.

Somewhere off to their right an axle had started grinding again. Under it their own feet made no sound she could hear.

They stopped at last in the angle behind a stack of empty barrels at the back of somebody's yard. Jaina set the staff's heel down one more time and listened. Her jaw was shaking too hard to keep shut, and she let it. The wet was behind them now and spread out, some of it drifting back toward the post and some toward Bell Street. None of it was coming on.

"It's behind us," she said. "It isn't following."

"It will." Sylvanas didn't look round from the yard gate. "Every door the captain had, they'll have."

"I have a room," Corin said.

They all turned to him. He was hugging the satchel to his chest with both arms.

"Ranger-General. I took it in the spring, for the copies. I didn't want them at the office, I didn't— I never told the captain. I never told anyone." He swallowed. "It's behind the Exchange. At the top of the house."

Sylvanas held still a moment longer, weighing him. "Show me."

---

Corin's room was at the top of a tall thin house behind the Exchange, up a stair that turned twice and creaked on every other tread, and nobody lived in it.

There was a cot against one wall, made up tight, its one blanket folded square at the foot the way first-years fold them for the warden's inspection. There was a table, and a stool. Along the other wall stood crates, lidded and roped, as if somebody had meant from the start to carry them down that stair in a hurry, with nail holes above them where an earlier lodger had hung his pictures. On the sill under the only shutter stood a row of ink bottles, each with a paper label in Corin's small square hand: black, black, blue, black, red.

Sylvanas crossed straight to the shutter, opening it a finger's width. Corin lit a candle and set it on the floor behind the table, where its light wouldn't reach the gap. Jaina sat down on the cot with the staff leaning against the wall beside her.

They were out. She kept coming back to it, her knees shaking harder each time, until she pressed down on them with her left forearm and they went on shaking under it. The cold had gone right through her. She was sitting in a warm room on a warm night, under the roof, with sweat drying on her, and she was shivering. She couldn't stop it, and after a while she gave up trying.

Lyriel crouched in front of her, taking her left hand by the wrist to turn it to the candle. Frost crusted the edge of the linen round the two bare fingertips, going to wet spots as they watched, though Jaina couldn't feel any of it. Lyriel held the hand a while with her thumb across the back of it, then laid it on Jaina's knee and got up.

Someone scratched at the door. Lyriel opened it a crack with her knife out, then wider. Cyndia slipped in, shut it behind her and took her place before the shutter, Sylvanas turning her head from the gap just far enough to listen.

"Ranger-General. While you were under the granary, six men went in at the chandler's yard gate. They went straight to the store door. Nobody tried the shop. Then four more went up the farrier's outside stair on Bell Street and broke the door in. The two at his gate stayed at the gate. After that the rest went to the corner under the lamp, on the way to the post, and stood in the doorways until the captain ran." She paused before the last of it. "They didn't search." She stepped back against the crates and began going through the arrows in her quiver with her fingers, one at a time.

"A magister in Silvermoon would have written first," Lyriel said from the door. "Three pages before the knife, and he'd want an answer."

Cyndia carried on through her arrows. Lyriel leaned her shoulder on the frame and put her eye back to the crack, facing the stair.

Corin had gone pale where he stood by the table, his hand flat on the satchel. He patted it once, the way he did before he answered anything, and then he said it.

"He was paid." It came out thin, and he tried again. "The captain. He was on their coin." He swallowed. "There's a book at the Exchange, the corn guild's, of who owes what. I go through it for the bills. He was in it all spring—the roof on the post, his own house, something to a factor on Mill Lane, I never found out what. Pages of it. At high summer it was struck through. All of it, every line, in one hand, the same week Fenner's and the Exchange changed suppliers." He looked down at the satchel. "I saw it. I thought somebody had been good to him."

He rubbed his inky thumb along the edge of the flap. "And the notices." He was going faster now. "Anything I took him about a bill on Fenner's mark, or the Exchange's, it was leave it with the others, lad, and it went under the stone. Anything on a true mark, one of the mill yards, he'd have a man round there that afternoon. I thought it was me. I thought he didn't care to have anybody from Dalaran telling him about his own streets."

At the shutter, without turning round, Sylvanas said, "He told me Kett shut his gate at noon."

Jaina looked at the candle on the floor. She should have seen it. The doors he'd named tonight—the store, the farrier's, the post—all of them.

No. She had seen it. She'd sat at the post table this evening watching him draw the same chalk line down Hurdle Lane three times in the same groove, and she'd watched his two fingers point her at each turn tonight. A captain who wanted his own streets, his own lads, his own magistrate in daylight—like any gate captain who had ever kept her waiting while he hunted for the right book. Territorial, she'd called it. Territorial and slow. Then she'd gone on to the next thing.

And the farrier's. He'd named it in that room after the man died, with nobody there to hear him but themselves, and four men had gone up that stair.

She leaned forward over her knees, and barley pattered out of her collar onto Corin's floor. "They planned for someone coming," she said. "Months ago." It came out hoarse and too fast, and she made herself go slower. "Tam had his collar sewn before he ever saw us. The thing in that man was tied off long before tonight, with somebody else holding the end of it. And the doors were picked out ahead of time, I believe. His doors. So that whoever found the loft would be walked into one of them."

"Into the first of them," Sylvanas said, without turning from the gap. "I said lead."

She stayed at the gap with the bow in her hand and an arrow still on the string. Watching her, Jaina heard the bowstring go by her ear again, Havel's shout stopping halfway through a word. She'd been glad. When he went down in the dust she had been glad, and she'd stayed glad all the way through the cart shed.

She didn't look at that. She looked at the candle.

They called him—

If she'd had a ward on him. The holding ward, the first one anybody learns, laid over him in the lane and fed in rounds all the way to the store. Would it have stood against something that came through a wall? It might. It might have held as long as it took him to finish the word. She'd had nothing left to lay, and she hadn't known there was anything in him to lay it against. She ran it again anyway: the lane, the ward going down over him, the needle through the plaster, his mouth getting to the end of what it had started.

"Kett's will be empty by now," Sylvanas said. "The rope-walk by first light."

They could go back for it. They could sit on the cracks in the walls and take the next runner, then the one after him, each of them ending on a stool with his mouth open on half a word while somebody they couldn't point to listened through the plaster.

One, the man had said. There's one over all of it. He had it written down before any of us came.

She reached for the pen on Corin's table, but her fingers wouldn't close on it. It rolled away across the boards and stopped against the inkwell.

She wanted the pen in her hand. She always wanted it when something had gone this badly; she'd have had a page of it by now, dated and ruled, with the next test at the bottom. Her two fingertips lay on the boards where the pen had been, white, and did nothing.

"Corin," she said. "Would you write for me? Please."

He was at the stool before she'd finished, pulling a sheet off a pile and uncorking the black. "Yes. Of course, Lady Proudmoore. The— like the sample room? Heavy and clean?"

"These are for whoever finds them, if we don't come back for them." She'd been making notes for the next morning since Millhaven, columns and questions and what to test first, and none of that was any use to anybody now. "Put the date at the top. Then: The grain leaving Andorhal on copied yard marks is poisoned. It goes in at the packing, by hand, after the dressing and before the sacks are sewn."

The pen scratched, his hand unsteady. The nib caught on something and threw a fine spatter of black across the sheet, and he took hold of his right wrist with his left hand and went on.

"The marks," she said. "Fenner's hook drawn from the bottom. You know the rest better than I do. Put them all in."

"Yes."

"The men who do it are drilled. They carry poison sewn into their collars, and they'll use it." She stopped. "Write that a man taken alive will die before he can speak, by a working done from close by and through a wall. Write that I felt it go in."

The nib kept moving. He'd gone paler, but the letters were coming out level now, one line under the next.

"I'll make two," he said, without looking up. "I always make two."

"Three. And one goes somewhere none of us sleeps."

He nodded and wrote.

Over the cot, pinned to the wall with four tacks, hung a cheap printed map of the roads round Andorhal, marked all over in Corin's small square hand. She turned on the cot to face it. The copied bills, she supposed, road by road.

"Corin," she said. "When you've finished that line. The map."

Corin wrote out the rest of the line, reading it back under his breath. Then he laid the pen across the neck of the inkwell and leaned over her to get at the tacks, saying "Sorry—sorry, Lady Proudmoore" to her shoulder as he worked them out of the plaster one at a time into his palm. The map came away with all four corners curling.

"Ranger-General," Jaina said. "Your sheet, please. If you would."

Sylvanas pulled the shutter to. Cyndia was at it before the latch had dropped, her eye to the crack down the hinge side and her bow hand resting on the sill. Sylvanas hooked a boot round one leg of the table and drew it across the boards until its edge touched the cot at Jaina's knees. Then she took the sheet out of her coat and opened it flat. At once it began to roll itself back up round the drawing on its back—Kett's, in lead and soot, the crock and five strokes—until Corin weighed one corner with the inkwell and Sylvanas laid her belt knife across another.

Corin fished a little tin lamp out from between the crates and lit it at the candle on the floor. Sylvanas took it from him and turned the wick down until the flame was no bigger than a barleycorn, setting it at the far side of the table, where it smoked.

"Until the first carts," she said. "No longer."

Corin poured something from a jug on the crates into a tin cup, setting it down by Jaina's hand. It was tea, black and cold, stewed since whenever he'd last been up here. She got the cup to her mouth between the heel of her hand and two fingertips that couldn't feel it and drank half, the bitterness making her eyes water. She set the cup down on a third corner of the sheet.

She knew the sheet as well as her own notes, though hardly a line of it was in her hand. Sylvanas had drawn the roads in lead at Corley's and lettered the names along them, narrow and upright; over the top of it all, from the ford down, lay Jaina's blots, made at mile-posts and crossings with the pen pinched in two fingers. Heavy. Heavy and old. All the roads on the sheet ran down to Andorhal at the bottom, the blots getting thicker the closer they came. There was a slit through the paper at Strahnbrad where Sylvanas had once put her knife through it into a bench. Under Andorhal lay a thumb's width of clean paper, then the edge.

Each night since Corley's she had looked at it and read the same thing off it. Here. Come down here.

"Wherever a copied bill went," she said. "Would you put them on this for me, Corin, in another ink, so I can tell yours from mine? A dot for each bill. And if the reeve has written in sick since, a ring round it."

Corin glanced from the sheet to Sylvanas. "On the Ranger-General's—"

"Yes," Sylvanas said.

He uncorked the red and sat down on the stool with his printed map open across his knees, one finger on that and the pen over this. All the fingers of his right hand were inked already, black to the second knuckle on the first two and blue on the rest; red got onto the side of his thumb before he'd made a mark. He found a place on his own map, found it again on hers and set the nib down.

His first dots landed on top of her blots. Then he carried on past them.

"We rode that." Sylvanas laid a finger on the road beside his hand, drawing it up the sheet as far as the ford. "To there. Not this one." She moved the finger across to the next road, where Corin's red was going down a line without a single blot on it. "Nor this."

"The weir road," Corin said. He followed it down until he ran out of paper and stopped with the nib in the air.

"Keep going," Jaina said. "Up the margin."

He was halfway up the margin, writing the names sideways and small, when Cyndia spoke.

"Ranger-General." She didn't turn her head from the crack. "Lantern in the street below. Two men. They tried the door across the way."

Corin's pen stopped.

"They've gone on toward the Exchange."

"Tell me if they come back," Sylvanas said, and the nib dropped to the paper again.

When the weir road was done he drew the east road in himself, a red line out of the other bottom corner, filling the other margin. Then he returned to the first road and added more red below the place where her blots stopped. Now and then he made a ring. Jaina counted the rings as he made them, and after a while she stopped counting.

"Kett's," she said. "Tonight's."

"All of them for the first carts out." He didn't look up. "I'd be guessing at the roads."

"Then leave them off."

"Seven," Lyriel said from the door, without taking her eye from the crack. "Loaded, going down the lane, while we were at the post. They'll be well along by now."

"And across the top," Jaina said. "What he said. Every yard. Every road out. He had it written down before any of us came here."

Corin dipped the black and wrote it across the head of the sheet, above the ford, and his hand stayed steady the whole length of the line. He pressed a blotting sheet down over it, peeled it away and sat back on the stool, and she looked at what they had.

Her blots closed in on Andorhal from the top. Corin's red left it at the bottom, running down all the roads the paper had room for, off the edges and up both margins, in the names of villages she had never ridden through—farther out across the north of the kingdom than Sylvanas had paper for. It was one knot, with as many roads going out of it as coming in; she had only ever read the half that came in.

She sat very still.

She'd come here to shut it. Since Strahnbrad that had been the whole of the plan—get into Andorhal, find the yard, shut the yard—and she had come down this sheet a blot at a time with the drag getting heavier in her teeth, sure that heavier meant nearer and nearer meant in time. She could shut Kett's. Shut Fenner's and the Exchange and the malt-house as well, tonight, if she had to sit in their gateways herself. And then? Forty-one bills would still be out on those roads. They had been going out since midsummer into bakehouses, coming out of the ovens as loaves that had since been eaten; some of the reeves had already sat down and written in.

Four loads had filled Tobin's chapel. She started the sum for forty-one and left it half done.

All night she'd been reading it forward: which side of them the taint was on, how close, whether it was standing or moving. She had kept them a corner ahead of it the whole way here. And all that time the grain had been out past her. Below Strahnbrad, where the road had filled up with grain carts, she'd pulled the mare onto the verge a dozen times to let them by. How many of those had been coming the other way?

She had asked for one man on a stool and one plain answer out of him. Instead she had a sheet that ran off its own edges, a count she couldn't finish and nothing anywhere on it that said who.

The lamp guttered, sending up a thread of black smoke. Sylvanas reached across and turned the wick down another notch.

Whoever it was had laid all of it out before the thaw and then waited for the spring.

Learned where you were.

She had the rest already. The thread across the mouth of Kett's lane had been the first ward any student learned to tie, the one for a doorway, its ends sunk and closed as neatly as any examiner could ask. The stack in the jar at Brask's had been written in the grammar the Kirin Tor kept behind the library grille. And somebody had struck through a militia captain's whole spring of debts in one week at high summer, before anyone had come asking about Kett's at all. Who bought a captain a season before he needed him? Somebody who worked in the order Dalaran taught, every seam closed before the ward was fed—first term, the same as anyone who had sat a bench there, her included.

The face she had stopped at on the south road surfaced again. This time she didn't put it away.

"Lady Proudmoore." Corin had stopped writing, and the colour had gone out of his face again, right down to the lips. He laid his hand flat on the satchel and patted it once. "In the store. He said learned where you were. He was looking at your staff."

"Yes."

"When I was— while I was still at the Citadel, before they sent me out here. There was a master put out of the Kirin Tor." He swallowed. "The Archmage did it himself. Nobody at the desks was told why, only that it was necromancy, that he'd been told to leave it alone and hadn't. The clerks talked about nothing else for a month. Then nobody would say the name at all."

He looked down at the line across the top of the sheet, in his own hand. "Kel'Thuzad."

It fit. She had known it would.

Was it the word the loader's mouth had been shaped for, on the stool? She couldn't have sworn to that, but she would have sworn to the face.

Kirin Tor. Put out by Antonidas. Necromancy. Those three and a face were all she knew of him, and Corin had just said the three.

And Corin had written to the Citadel each week all summer without an answer to one of his letters. She set that beside the name. She didn't know what the two of them meant together. She left them there.

Her fingertips lay on the table edge, white to the second joint, and she didn't move them.

By the door Lyriel had taken her eye from the crack and turned her head. At the shutter Cyndia looked round too, at Jaina and not at Corin, before putting her eye back to the street.

"Where did he go?" Sylvanas said to Corin.

"I— nobody said. They wouldn't put that on a notice." He patted the satchel, then opened it and dug in with both hands, coming out with a thin fold of copies tied with string. He picked at the knot with his inky nails until it gave. "But the factor. The one nobody at the Exchange has met. His grain came into Fenner's and the Exchange at high summer, and I've the inbound bills for it, the second copies." He spread them flat and moved the tin cup onto them, and the corner of the sheet where the cup had stood lifted off the table. "Every load came in on the east road. They're billed from farms out in the Eastweald, past the last toll, and I've never found one of those farms on any roll I can get at."

Sylvanas went still, her eyes past Corin on the east road, where his red ran out of the corner and up the margin.

East of Andorhal. Somewhere in the Eastweald, off the paper.

Corin still had the pen in his fingers, the red drying on the nib. The lamp had burned down to a blue bead, a thread of smoke standing up off it. Cyndia kept her eye to the street, and Lyriel kept her head turned. Sylvanas faced her across the table with her hands at her sides. All of them were waiting on her.

Same libraries. Same lecture halls, the same benches in them, and for all she knew the same tutors, telling him to close his seams first in the same dry voices they'd used on her.

Antonidas had put him out and said almost nothing about why. And she'd never asked. Why hadn't she? Because it was the Council's business and not hers. Because she'd had her own work, and no room in her head for— No. Because she had never once thought of a man put out of Dalaran as anybody's trouble but his own. He'd gone, and she'd gone back to her books. And Antonidas had one note from her now and nothing since.

And the other thing. She knew that one already; she'd been sick over the mare's shoulder with it on the south road. Part of her wanted to see the keeping part work, just once, and that had been bad enough when nobody had written it. Now somebody had. Somewhere east of here his notes were lying in his own hand, and the part of her that wanted them was already wondering how he'd closed the seam between the keeping and the waiting—

She took her fingertips off the table.

"Jaina," Lyriel said from the door.

Jaina looked up. "I know the name," she said. It came out hoarse, and she cleared her throat. "He was trained where I was. In the same rooms, by the same people."

There was more. She kept it.

Across the lamp, Sylvanas moved her hand to her bowstring and rested it there. She didn't ask.

Jaina set her two white fingertips on the east road, where Corin's red ran off the edge. She couldn't feel the paper under them, but she could see them on it.

"I'm going east," she said. "That factor's grain came in on the east road, all of it, and I want to know where it was put on the cart."

"Before light," Sylvanas said. "The horses are outside the wall, and the captain's paymaster can watch the gates all he likes."

She took her knife back off the sheet and lifted the inkwell, letting the sheet roll up round Kett's of its own accord. She folded it small with the red inside and slid it back into her coat.

Corin drew his notes back across the table, uncorking the black. "The other two copies," he said. "They'll be done before you go."

Jaina tucked the staff under her dead arm, its crystal dark. Barley was still working its way down the small of her back.

And every seam she'd ever been proud of closing—how much of that had he known first?
