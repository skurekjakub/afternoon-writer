The south road climbed under the hills in long bends between the hedges, every bend of it reading the same.

Jaina felt it come up through the mare each time a forefoot came down—a dull weight under the road, as if the road had been laid over something heavier than itself. It rose through the saddle into the staff where the staff lay in the crook of her dead arm, then went in behind her eyes and sat there. The crystal rode a hand's width from her cheek. In Dalaran it had been a good clear gold. This morning it was washed out to the colour of straw left in the rain, the light in it giving a small flinch every time the weight came up.

Old at the bottom. Thin and new on top, going the same way. She had said so at the fork with her teeth chattering. It had been true at the first bridge, and it was true now, a long way up the climb, with the mist lying in the road as high as the mare's knees and the fields gone grey and shapeless on either side.

Above the mist, where the road went on climbing, the brown smear lay along the slope of the hills. It had been there since first light. It was wider now—or they were closer to it.

Her head hurt. It had hurt since the fork, a slow ache climbing from the back of her neck to lodge behind her eyes and press outward, harder each time the road gave its weight up through the staff. She had slept sitting against a stone block with her cheek on the end of a bench; the cold had got into her right shoulder in the night and stayed. The arm itself she could not feel at all. It held the staff against her ribs, which was the one thing it was still good for. The left hand had the reins, wrapped in Cyndia's linen to the wrist with two fingertips left bare, the palm under the linen throbbing in time with the mare's walk. Lyriel's burnt crust was still in her back teeth.

They rode in the old order. Sylvanas went in front with her back straight and her bow across the saddle. Cyndia had the left of the road and Lyriel the right, her knee nearly touching Jaina's. Behind them were the two places nobody filled.

They passed a field gate with a horseshoe nailed over it, points down. On any quay in Boralus somebody would have been up a ladder turning it the right way before the luck could run out of it; her father's bosun used to slap the one over the chandlery door every morning on his way past, to make sure it was still holding. Nobody here had bothered. The mist had put a bead of water on each nail head.

The road dipped. At the bottom of the dip a stream ran under it through a culvert, the mare's hooves going hollow on the stones over the top. The weight came up through them so hard that the crystal guttered, and Jaina's stomach dropped as if the mare had missed a step.

She knew that drag. The beat of it came late—the same small hitch behind every pulse, the same weight under it. She had felt exactly this at Millhaven, kneeling in the lane with the heel of the staff set down in a dead man's footprint while Cyndia laid her hazel switches out a step apart.

She went still in the saddle. The mare walked on under her.

The mare carried her out of the dip and up the far side, the road reading the same the whole way.

A young man with a cart had sold flour two coppers cheaper on the measure at the sick village, four loads of it, the last a fortnight before they came. Corley's book had loads like it going out on every road from his crossing, and every slip in Sylvanas's coat had Andorhal at one end. Garran had eaten bread sops in milk twice a day all summer, and he was the worst of all of them. The jar under the loft boards had been made to keep and made to wait. And Millhaven's dead had got up out of their beds and walked north up the cow way four and five abreast, leaving prints in the lane that read like this road.

Bad luck, then. A wet harvest. A mould in the stores, a fever that liked villages because villages shared wells. She had kept hold of that one since Millhaven without ever quite saying it, even after the slate in the chapel had ruled it out—kept it like the last biscuit at the bottom of her satchel through a whole day of lectures. No. A fever didn't come in layers a fortnight apart. A fever didn't keep to one road and leave the King's road clean beside it.

One man, then. One clever, bitter man with a cart and a grudge, who had found something in an old book and—

No. Slower. One man couldn't drive every load in Corley's book. One man hadn't swept the depot floor, stuffed the stove, kept the overseer's answers ready for him. One man hadn't sewn a fold of poison into a boy's collar and told him when to use it. And a man good enough to build that jar wouldn't need to sell flour cheap at a village market with his own two hands, unless somebody else had told him to.

It was one thing. It was one thing made in parts, every part with a job: the carts to carry it, the mills and bakehouses to put it into bread, the people to eat it a little at a time, morning and evening. The jar's keeping part kept it whole inside them. The waiting part waited. And at the end, when the bread had done its work, their own bodies did the last of it.

Too even for luck. Too many hands for one man. Every piece of it in its place, so that it ran without anybody standing over it.

Oh, Light.

If she put it as they put things in Dalaran, it would come out sounding like a paper for the Council—all balance and qualification, for anybody to argue with. So she didn't.

"Ranger-General."

Sylvanas turned in the saddle, her horse walking on beneath her.

"It's one thing," Jaina said. "The flour and the jar and Millhaven. Somebody built it to ride in the grain. People eat it in their bread, a little every day. It keeps in them. It waits." Her mouth had gone dry, and she swallowed. "It comes in on the wagons and walks out on its own feet."

Lyriel's head came round at her knee. Over on the left, Cyndia glanced at Lyriel, then back to the hedge.

Sylvanas heard her out with her face quite still, as she heard Cyndia out. She didn't ask whether Jaina was sure. She looked Jaina over once—the crystal, the hand on the reins—and came back to her face.

"Somebody," she said.

"Somebody with training, a great deal of it—more than I have, I think, and more than one of them, if it's to run the way it's running." Jaina shifted the staff against her ribs, wincing as the ash knocked the bone. "I can't tell you who. I can't tell you how it gets from a knot under a man's breastbone to his feet walking after he's dead, either; that's the part of the jar I couldn't read."

"And when you say that to a man who can't feel any of it?"

"I give him your slips, the sacking, the map with your knife hole in it. Things he can hold in his hand, since he can't feel the rest."

Sylvanas watched the road ahead, the bow lying easy across her saddle. "Paper. He'll have clerks for paper."

"Then he can come with me to a granary and watch. I'll find him a jar."

Sylvanas looked at her a moment longer. "He has orcs in his hills, Mage," she said, turning forward again. "Make it short."

Short. She began cutting it down in her head—the slips first, then the road, then the jar—and stuck on the jar.

One part to keep. In the sick village she had taken that to mean the stuff kept in the jar, in the damp under the boards. No—it had to keep through far more than a damp floor. It went through a mill. It went into a bakehouse oven in the dough and came out again in a hot loaf. Somebody broke the loaf and ate it, and it was still whole enough inside them to wait. What kept through an oven? Nothing she'd ever been taught. Nothing she'd ever made. She wanted to know. She wanted it on a bench under a good lamp with nobody in the room, so she could lift the keeping part away from the rest and turn it over and see how it was laid—seam on seam, what held it, where the joins were.

And Garran had been eating it out of a bowl with a spoon.

Her stomach turned over, sour spit running under her tongue. She leaned out to the left, away from Lyriel, and spat into the mist, then wiped her mouth on her own shoulder because she had no hand to spare for it. She had shut her left hand hard on the reins, and the split in the palm pulled under the linen, a thin bright line of hurt from the heel to the root of her thumb. She eased her grip, and the mare tossed her head and went quiet.

There were books on the death-currents in Dalaran. They stood behind the grille at the top of the library stair, and first-years got the warnings and the spines and nothing else. She had read the spines; everybody read the spines. In her first year a master had stood under that shelf and told the room, in the voice he used for the fire rules, that the whole of that art came down to keeping a body useful after the person in it had gone, and that this was why the grille was locked. Somebody at the back had laughed. She thought it might have been her.

Somebody had gone past the spines. Somebody had been given a key—or had been in the room on a day the grille stood open—and had read to the end of those books and gone further, laying the work out so cleanly that it ran on flour and finished on its own. She knew that cleanness. She had been taught to lay a ward that way, every seam closed, nothing left loose for the next spell to catch on. That was Dalaran teaching, in a Dalaran hand.

Somebody she might have passed on the library stair, then—somebody she'd borrowed a pen from. She started down the faces in her head—masters, senior students, the ones who'd left and never written—and stopped at the third. She made herself look at the road.

Mist had beaded on the staff, a fine grey wet along the ash, running down into the silver wire on the grip. She watched a drop gather on the lowest turn and fall.

Assessment. That was all it was. A piece of work, looked at properly, with its strengths set down in one column and its faults in the other, as she'd write it up for Antonidas. She needed it. She couldn't take apart a thing she refused to look at, and nobody else on this road could look at it at all. That much was true.

The rest was a lie, and she knew it for one even while she told it. She'd have given a hand to see the keeping part work just once. She was running short of hands.

Arthas, then. She'd put all of it in front of him: the slips, the sacking, the map with the hole in it, the road under them, the whole shape of it from the cart to the cow way, said as plainly as she'd just said it to Sylvanas. He had always been quick, quicker than the court gave him credit for, and he hated more than anything to be told a thing couldn't be done. Lay it out clean and he would move, and he'd move before Andorhal went like Millhaven.

At the next bend the road climbed out of the mist.

One moment the mare was wading in grey, and the next her legs were clear, wet and dark to the knee, the hedges standing up sharp on either side with the sun on them. Jaina had to squint. The air was warmer here. It smelled of woodsmoke, and under the woodsmoke of horses, a great many of them, more than any farm kept.

The brown smear hung over the shoulder of the hill ahead, where the road went over. It was dust. It hung in the air above the road the way flour hangs over a mill floor, smoke going up into it from somewhere behind the hill. It moved because the road under it was moving.

A wagon came down out of it, iron tyres grinding on the stones. Two big, heavy-footed horses pulled it. Its bed was empty except for a few loose wisps of straw, and a man walked at the horses' heads with a rag tied over his nose and mouth against the dust. Somewhere far up the hill behind him a horn blew, and men shouted, a long way off.

Sylvanas put her horse onto the verge to let the wagon by. Cyndia and Lyriel closed in, flanking Jaina. The driver looked at the four of them over his rag as he passed—the bows, the green cloaks, the staff—and didn't stop.

Jaina put her heels to the mare and rode up after Sylvanas, into the dust.

It came up off the road under every wheel and hoof to hang at head height, tasting of dung when she breathed it in. It clung to the sweat of the mare's neck and turned it to paste. The road went over the shoulder of the hill in a cut between two banks. Its ruts ran so deep that the mare had to step down into them and climb out again, rocking Jaina in the saddle at every other stride while the staff knocked against her ribs. More wagons came down past them, empty, two and three together. One went up, loaded high and roped under a tarpaulin, its team blowing and a man at the tailboard shoving with his shoulder. Nobody on the road looked twice at the bows now. There was too much else on it to look at.

At the top of the cut Sylvanas stopped her horse. Jaina came up beside her and saw the army.

It covered the whole long slope below them, running down to a huddle of grey roofs where the road crossed a stream and up the other side. Tents stood in rows across what had been pasture, brown canvas and white, the lanes between them trodden into a brown soup. Horses stood along the hedges in lines, hundreds of them, tails to the wind. Wagons were drawn up in a square near the bottom, and smoke from a great many cookfires went up out of the camp and flattened under the dust. Above the biggest tents banners cracked on their poles, blue and white, with the King's gold on them. Near the stream a knot of men in heavy plate were walking their horses, the sun catching the white of their tabards and the silver hand stitched on each one.

Across the valley, on the hills to the east, there was other smoke. Four columns of it rose dark and thick at the root, leaning together as they went up. One of them had a small orange flicker at the bottom, very small at this distance. A roof.

Sylvanas sat quite still in the saddle. She looked across the camp from one end to the other, stopping in three places. Jaina followed her look, and was slower.

"The big tent's his," Sylvanas said. "Riders go out from it east and come back east. Pickets on the east edge, doubled." She lifted her chin toward the near side of the camp, where the road came in. "On this side, one sergeant and a hedge."

It took Jaina a moment more, and then she had it. The pickets stood along the eastern edge of the camp in a line, every man of them looking at the far hills. The archers on the earth bank above the stream had their backs to the road. And up the road at their backs—the road she had been reading all morning—the wagons came in one after another, turning in behind the cook-tents. Nobody in the whole camp turned round to see what was in them.

Facing the wrong way. The whole army, every man in it, facing the wrong way.

No—that wasn't fair. They were facing the smoke, and the smoke was real; somebody's roof was coming down under that orange flicker while she sat here. They'd been sent to fight orcs, and they were looking where the orcs were. It was only that the other thing was coming up the road behind them in sacks, with nobody to tell them so. Well. She had come to tell them.

Sylvanas had said at the fork that they'd put the flour after the orcs. Sylvanas had been right about Brask, right about the overseer, right about most men Jaina had watched her measure since the Pass. But Sylvanas had never met Arthas. Jaina had known him since she was a girl. He'd look at the sheet with the knife hole in it, and then—because it was Jaina holding it—he'd look again, harder. For once the Ranger-General would turn out to have been too hard on a prince.

Sylvanas put her horse down the slope at a walk, the rest of them following in the old order.

The sergeant at the gap in the hedge had a spear and a beard grey with dust. He stepped into the road with the spear held crossways, more to stop the horses than to threaten anyone, and looked along the four of them: the green cloaks, the bows, the elf ears, the staff, Jaina's wrapped hand on the reins.

"Hold there. Who's this?"

"Jaina Proudmoore, of Dalaran." The dust had got into her throat, and she swallowed, trying to get some voice behind it. "Apprentice to the Archmage Antonidas. I've come to see Prince Arthas. He knows me."

"Everybody knows him, miss."

"Ranger-General Sylvanas Windrunner," Sylvanas said, "of Quel'Thalas. Proudmoore rides under my escort."

The sergeant looked at her for a while, leaning on his spear. Then he turned and bawled for a boy. One came running out from between two tents, and the sergeant said something in his ear and sent him off up the lanes at a run, splashing.

They waited at the hedge, the camp shouting round them. Lyriel could ride a whole morning at Jaina's knee and never let a buckle clink. Cyndia went into a dark wood like a cat going into a cupboard. Sylvanas gave her orders so low you had to be watching her mouth. Here a man was walking up and down the horse lines, bellowing for somebody called Dace, but nobody answered him. Two officers on either side of a banner pole were shouting at each other about a ford, neither one listening. A farrier was hammering. Somewhere a mule had begun to bray and could not seem to stop. The air smelled of wet canvas, horse, iron, the smoke of the cookfires, and under all of it, faint and bitter, the other smoke from across the valley.

A soldier sat on a cask outside the nearest tent with his helmet upside down between his knees, full of water, shaving without a glass. He nicked himself as she watched, looked at the blood on his thumb, and went on shaving.

Sylvanas got down from her horse. Coming to Jaina's stirrup, she put her hand inside her coat and brought out the map sheet, folded small, with the Barov slips and the square of sacking tucked inside the fold. She held it up and waited.

Jaina let go of the reins, taking it in the two bare fingertips of her left hand. It weighed nothing. All of Corley's book, the river depot's stove, the jar, Garran, the cow way—folded into one sheet no thicker than a letter. Sylvanas didn't say anything. She gave the mare's rein to Cyndia and stepped back.

Lyriel was already off her horse. She reached up, took Jaina under the arms, and lifted her down as she'd done at every stop since the sick village, without being asked and without making anything of it. Jaina's boots went into the mud to the ankle. It was cold, and it pulled at her when she shifted her feet. Lyriel let go and went back to the horses' heads. Cyndia drew all four of them in close, sitting with her eyes on the soldiers' hands.

The boy came back down the lane. He wasn't alone.

Arthas came between the tents at a long stride, the boy trotting behind him and two officers behind the boy. He was still talking to one of them over his shoulder when he saw her. He had his helmet under his arm. His hair was dark with sweat at the temples and gold everywhere else, and longer than she remembered. Somebody polished his armour for him; it shone like a good pan, the crest of Lordaeron catching the light on the breastplate. From the knee down his greaves were plastered brown. He'd got broader, and he was a man now, with a hammer at his belt instead of a practice sword.

He stopped in the mud and laughed out loud.

"Jaina!" He came on, the officers stepping quickly to keep up. "Light's sake. Jaina Proudmoore, on my hill. Did Antonidas finally throw you out?"

"He'd have to find me first." It came out in the old voice before she'd decided on any voice at all. "Look at you, though. They've given you a whole hill, Your Highness, and somebody to polish you."

He laughed again, delighted. Then he was close enough to see properly, and the grin went thin. He looked at the wrapped hand, and at the right arm hanging with the staff caught in its crook. "What happened to your hands?"

"It's part of why I came."

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he turned to Sylvanas and gave her a small, correct bow from the neck. "Ranger-General. You're a long way from home."

"Your Highness," Sylvanas said, the two words perfectly level, each one set down in its place.

Jaina glanced round at her. Sylvanas was looking from his face to Jaina's and back, and her own face didn't change at all.

"Arthas, I need you to listen to me—all the way to the end."

"I always listen to you all the way to the end. It's the only way to get a word in afterwards." But he folded his arms and set his weight, his face going serious. "Go on."

So she gave it to him short.

"It's in the grain. There's something in the flour—a made thing, a necromancer's work, I believe, or so near it I can't tell you the difference. It goes in at the mills. People eat it in their bread, a little at every meal. It stays in them and builds. Millhaven's dead, the whole village. And the dead didn't stay down; they got up and walked north, sixty of them at least. There's a village south of Millhaven that would have gone the same way, if we hadn't burned every sack of flour in it in one night."

He nodded. He was listening; she could see him listening, the way he used to listen to a fencing master, with his eyes on her face and his mouth pressed.

"And it isn't one village. It's carts and depots and account books." She lifted the sheet and shook it open against the staff, pinning the bottom edge to the ash with her thumb's knuckle so that it hung flat. The marks were there—her blots and Sylvanas's neat ones, the slit where the knife had gone through at Strahnbrad. "These came out of the stove at a depot on the river road. Every slip with Andorhal on it at one end. Every road I've read south of the Pass is carrying it, old under new, and every one of them runs to Andorhal."

He bent to look, the sweat-dark hair falling over his brow. He didn't take the sheet. He put one gloved finger on the knife slit. "That's us."

"That's you. And this road runs on into Andorhal by the back gate."

He drew one of the slips half out of the fold with the same finger, turning it to read the seal. "That's Barov."

"It's on every sack in the south, good or bad. It's his granaries." She kept her voice exactly even. "I'm saying nothing about Lord Barov. I'm saying the grain goes through Andorhal."

"Good. He'd sue me for the rest of it, and he'd talk for an hour first." He let the slip slide back into the fold. "Jaina. I don't doubt you."

Her chest loosened. There. She'd known he would.

Then his eyes went past her shoulder, over the camp, to the far hills and the smoke.

She talked faster. "Then you see why it can't wait. If it's in the granaries there, it's in every loaf in the city and everything that goes out of it. People are eating it this morning. I need men to shut the Andorhal granaries before—"

"He's going to write to his father, Proudmoore," Sylvanas said behind her, quietly, "and then he's going back up that hill."

Arthas looked at Sylvanas. For a moment Jaina thought he'd laugh at her or get angry—say something to put her in her place, as men in Dalaran did when an elf told them their business. He did none of it. He looked at Sylvanas for a long, steady moment, as he might have looked at a boy across the practice yard who had read his feint. Then he looked at the hills again.

"Yes," he said. "I am."

"Arthas—"

"Come here a moment." He put his hand on her shoulder, turning her gently round to face the valley. "You see that? The one that's still burning?"

She saw it. The little orange flicker under the column of smoke.

"That's the Garrow farm. Old Garrow, his daughter, her two boys. I ate supper at their table on the way up here; the boys wanted to hold my hammer. Yesterday I helped carry them out." His voice didn't change. It stayed warm and low and kind, and that was worse. "They came out of the camps, Jaina. A whole band of them, with axes. They know these hills better than my scouts do. Every farm between here and the river is waiting to be next, and I've got men in this camp whose families are on those farms. I can see that from my tent door. I can't see your grain."

"I can."

"I know you can. And I believe you."

He did. That was the trouble. She had put it in front of him in order—the flour, the dead, the slips, the roads—and watched every piece of it go in. He'd nodded at each one, and he'd put his finger on the right place on the map and asked about the right seal. He'd heard all of it, and he was standing exactly where he'd stood before she began, his face turned to the smoke.

"So write it down. All of it, just as you've told me. I'll send it to my father tonight with my own rider. He'll put it in front of the council, and they'll listen to you a good deal sooner than they listen to me. As soon as these hills are quiet, I'll ride to Andorhal myself, and you can show me what you've found."

"By the time these hills are quiet—"

"I know." He squeezed her arm. It was the right arm, and she watched his gauntlet close on her sleeve and felt nothing at all. "I'm sorry. I am. Get something to eat before you go; you look like you haven't eaten since Dalaran." He turned his head to the nearer of his officers. "See they're fed, and the horses watered."

A rider was coming down through the lanes from the east at a hard canter, spattering men right and left, and somebody up by the big tent was already shouting the prince's name. Arthas let go of her arm, stepping back. He gave Sylvanas the same small bow from the neck, then turned and went up the hill to meet the rider, putting his helmet on as he walked. The officers closed in round him. She could see the gold of his hair for a few steps more between their shoulders—then she couldn't.

He had never taken the paper.

She was still holding it up. The sheet hung open against the staff, her knuckle pinning the bottom edge, the marks turned toward the place where he had stood. The two bare fingertips on the corner had gone white with gripping. At the back of her mouth there was a sour metal taste, as if she'd bitten the inside of her cheek. She hadn't. She ran her tongue round to be sure.

Two officers stepped into the space he'd left, carrying a board between them with a map pinned to it, still at their argument about the ford. One of them glanced at her and at the elves behind her, moving the board so its corner wouldn't touch her staff. Then they had their backs to her.

Out of the camps. He'd said it as if it explained everything. To him, she supposed, it did.

The year she went to Dalaran, the escort had stopped for the night within sight of one. Arthas had come and scratched at the wall of her tent after the fire was banked, whispering that she'd never seen an orc, had she—nor had he, not up close—and there was a gap in the bracken under the second watchtower that nobody was looking at. She said no. He said she was frightened. She went. They crawled the last of it on their elbows through wet fern, Arthas whispering all the way, very sure, about what they'd see: beasts, Jaina, you'll see, that's why there are fences. At the fence she put her face to the gap between two stakes. Orcs lay by fires that had gone out, great grey shoulders under blankets, nobody keeping watch at all. Against the wall of a hut, close enough to throw a stone at, a female sat with an infant on her lap. It had hold of one of her tusks with its whole small fist. She was rocking it and humming something low, and she didn't look up.

All the way back through the fern, and at the tent door with the wet still on their knees, he had been just as sure—sure before the fence and sure after it. Jaina had lain in her blankets till morning thinking that he couldn't see what was in front of him, and she'd never said so to him. She'd been rather pleased with herself for knowing it.

Was that fair? She'd been a child, and so had he. He'd been saying what his father's council said; she'd been saying what she'd decided in a tent. And the band that had burned the Garrows' roof today had come out of those same camps, with axes. Perhaps the fences had been the right answer all along, and she'd been unfair to a boy for years over one night in the bracken.

No. That was the question she'd be asking if he'd listened badly, and he hadn't. He'd listened slowly and politely, every word to the end, and been exactly as sure at the end as at the start. He'd been kind about it. He'd been kind about it the whole time.

And she'd ridden down that hill counting on him. That was the part that made her face hot. She'd counted on him saying her name as he used to say it—on that being worth a second look at the paper. It had bought her a laugh and a supper. And a squeeze, on the arm she couldn't feel.

Sylvanas had told her at the fork. Sylvanas had told her again at the hedge without saying a word, just by looking at which way the pickets faced. And she'd said it a third time to his face, while he could still have turned round and proved her wrong. He hadn't even tried. Light—she'd have given a good deal for the Ranger-General to be wrong once. She was going to have to say so out loud.

The officers moved off with their board. Sylvanas stepped round, taking the place where Arthas had stood.

"Mage."

Jaina let the sheet fall closed against the staff. She tried to fold it again with her two fingers and made a poor job of it, the slips sliding half out of the fold. Sylvanas held out her hand. Jaina gave it to her, and Sylvanas pushed the slips back in and folded it small, putting it away inside her coat where it had come from.

"You were right," Jaina said. It came out more easily than she had expected. She'd been dreading it since the officers moved in, and then it was said. "At the fork. You said they'd put it after the orcs. And you called it in front of him before I'd finished."

Sylvanas didn't look pleased. She looked up the hill toward where the rider had gone, at the crowd round the big tent.

"He's not a fool," she said. "He's a good commander, with an enemy he can see and men who'd follow him into it. He'll fight that war. He'll likely win it."

"And the other one?"

"Is ours."

Jaina waited for her to ask the rest. Why he'd laughed. Why he'd called her Jaina in front of four strangers and a sergeant. Sylvanas didn't ask, and Jaina had no wish to tell her about the bracken or the fence or the female humming by the hut; it wouldn't have changed a thing Sylvanas had read off him. She'd had Sylvanas down—somewhere at the back of her head—as a soldier who'd think every prince a fool. Sylvanas hadn't. She'd only read him faster.

An officer was picking his way toward them through the mud, the one Arthas had told to see them fed. Sylvanas looked at him once and turned her back.

"Andorhal," she said.

"Andorhal."

Lyriel was there, her hands going under Jaina's arms, and then Jaina was up in the saddle, the staff back in the crook of her dead arm and the reins wound round her wrapped hand. Cyndia was already turning the horses. The four of them came round together, away from the hill and the smoke, going out through the gap in the hedge past the sergeant with his spear, who watched them go and said nothing.

Behind them the camp went on shouting. Somebody was still calling for Dace. The farrier's hammer kept on, and the mule. The noise of it all grew thinner at their backs as they rode down through the trampled grass onto the road and south past the grey roofs of Strahnbrad, toward Andorhal.

The road came down out of the hills in the middle of the afternoon, and once it reached the flat it began to fill.

Below Strahnbrad there had been nobody on it but an army wagon going up and a boy with a switch driving geese, and Jaina had ridden that stretch half asleep, the camp still shouting in her head. Somewhere along it Lyriel had dug an apple out of her saddlebag and held it up.

"Half for you and half for her," she'd said, nodding at the mare's ears. "She's walked through more mud today than a Silvermoon gardener sees in his life."

Jaina ate half of it in small bites, pinning it to the pommel with her two fingers, then leaned down and gave the rest to the mare. Now there were carts. They came in from the farm lanes on either hand, heaped with sacks and roped down, turning onto the road ahead of the horses. Others came the other way empty and rattling, the drivers sitting sideways on the boards with their whips across their knees. There were men on foot with bundles on their backs. A woman went by with a yoke and two milk pails. A cart of empty hen crates passed so close that a drift of small white feathers came off it and caught in the linen on Jaina's hand. She blew at them. Most went. One stayed, stuck in the weave over her knuckles, and she gave it up.

Every lane that came in brought more of the drag with it. She could feel each one join, a fresh thread of lateness under the road where a farm track met it. The road under the mare had grown heavier with every mile since the bridge, like a river swelling below every ditch that drains into it. Her head had gone to a steady ache. She let it ache.

Sylvanas rode through all of it as though the carts weren't there. Drivers saw the bows and the green cloaks coming and pulled over onto the verge, and one or two took a second look at Jaina's staff, then at her face, then looked away.

A man was coming toward them on the verge, on foot, walking out of Andorhal while everything else went in. He had a leather satchel on his hip, the flap buckled down over the bulk of a ledger. The strap had worn a long pale stripe across the front of his coat from shoulder to hip, as if he'd worn that satchel on that coat every working day for years. He was in his middle years, clean-shaven, going grey above the ears, with ink at the edge of one cuff. He walked like a man with a good way still to go and no wish to hurry it.

Sylvanas put her horse across his path and stopped.

"You've come from Andorhal."

He stopped too. He looked up at her, then along the rest of them—the bows and the tall horses, Cyndia on the left with her hand resting near her quiver, the staff in the crook of Jaina's arm. He took a half step back onto the grass.

"This noon," he said, putting his hand over the buckle of his satchel. "Yes, ma'am. Can I help you?"

"Who runs the granaries?"

He laughed at that, not unkindly. "Depends who you ask. The factors will tell you it's the factors. The corn guild will tell you it's the guild, the town will tell you it's the town, and all three of them will send you to sit on a bench in the hall till they light the lamps."

Jaina leaned forward over the mare's neck. "We need somebody who knows the yards," she said. "Somebody who'll walk us round them today, if he can. It's important, or we wouldn't stop you on the road."

He turned to her, his shoulders coming down. "Then you want Captain Havel. He's militia. Keep to this road all the way in; at the bottom of the hill the carts bear off left for the yards, but you keep straight on, and it brings you to the market gate. There's a militia post just inside it on the left, with a bench outside the door. Ask there for Captain Havel." He looked back over his shoulder, up the road toward the granaries, though from here there was nothing to see but the rise ahead and the dust hanging over it. "He knows the granary district better than anybody. Better than the factors do, and they'd sooner not hear it."

"Captain Havel," Jaina said. "At the market gate. Thank you, sir. You've been very kind."

"It's the satchel." He patted it. "Folk see it and think I've the whole town written down inside." He gave a small duck of his head that took in all four of them, stepping back off the road to let them by.

Sylvanas went on. Cyndia went by him on the left, then the mare drew level, and he looked up at Jaina as she passed.

"May the stars keep your road," he said, and walked on.

Ahead of her, Sylvanas turned in the saddle.

She turned all the way, one hand flat on her horse's rump, looking past Jaina and past Lyriel to the road behind them. It was full. A carter was standing up on his board, cursing at a wheel that had dropped into a rut. Two women with baskets had stopped to talk in the middle of the road, a string of pack mules edging round them. Behind the mules came another wagon, and another behind that.

"Mage." Sylvanas looked round at her. "Did you read anything off him?"

Jaina opened her mouth to say yes, of course, and had nothing to put after it.

She had been listening to the road the whole time he talked. She always was now; it came up through the mare and the staff whether she asked for it or not. He had stood on the verge in the middle of all that drag, the same as the carters and the women and the mules. Nothing had come off him that didn't come off the road—no knot in him that she could feel, no lateness that was his own. And she couldn't read a man in any case. She had told Sylvanas that herself.

Had she looked, though? Properly? She hadn't turned the staff toward him, or tried. Why would she? He was a clerk with ink on his cuff who had told them the way.

"Nothing," she said. "A tired clerk. That's all there was."

Sylvanas looked back again, and Jaina with her. The wagon was out of the rut. The women had moved on and the mules were past. There were men on foot all down the verge with bundles and sacks and baskets, and none of them had a pale strap across his coat. He was gone.

Jaina twisted round to face Sylvanas, the staff knocking her ribs. "Why?" she said. "What was it?"

"The blessing." Sylvanas faced forward again. "I've had it once before. At Millhaven, on the bridge. An old carter going north."

Millhaven. Jaina had a picture of a wagon on the bridge and voices, and nothing at all of what had been said. She had sat the mare that morning with her hands tucked under her arms, Millhaven's empty street at her back, the stream going under the bridge louder to her than any of them.

"It's a farewell," she said. "Country people have dozens of them."

"Once, it's a farewell." Sylvanas put her heel to her horse. "Twice, it's wrong. That's as far as it goes yet."

She rode on. Cyndia had already taken the left of the road again, watching the traffic ahead. Lyriel came back to Jaina's knee, and nobody said anything more about it.

Jaina sat with the reins wound round her hand and the white feather still caught in the linen.

A turn of phrase. Sylvanas hadn't so much as turned her head for the prince—she'd stood behind Jaina in the mud and told him what he was going to do before he knew it himself, never once raising her voice. And a clerk wished them well on the road, and she twisted round as if somebody had loosed an arrow at her back. A farewell was a farewell. If every carter who wished her well was a clue, Jaina would be at it till she was ninety.

But Sylvanas had asked her. She had turned round and asked plainly, as she'd ask Cyndia how many sets of tracks, and Jaina had had to say nothing. She didn't like nothing. She had hated it in her first year, on her feet in front of a master with the question asked and her mouth open, and she hated it now on a horse. She went back over him once more: the strap, the ink, the hand on the buckle, the look over his shoulder at the dust. A clerk. She'd have said the same if Antonidas had asked her.

She blew at the feather. It stayed where it was.

Past the next farm the road went in between walls. They were orchard walls at first, old stone with moss along the coping and apple boughs hanging over. Then came the backs of barns and long low sheds built right up to the road's edge, forcing the carts going in and the carts coming out to take turns at the narrow places. Lyriel dropped back behind Jaina where there wasn't room, coming up again where there was. The dust from the loaded carts had nowhere to go between the walls. It hung in the air at the height of a man's face, fine and pale, and it got into Jaina's mouth and stuck there, sour, like flour left in a damp sack.

The air had gone thick. There was no wind between the walls; the heat of the afternoon sat in the road, every breath coming in slowly, as if it had to be pulled.

Then the drag came up all at once.

It didn't come through the staff this time—or not only. It came in behind her eyes and pushed. She had dived off the end of the quay at Boralus once on a dare, as deep as she could go after a coin somebody had thrown. This was that: the weight of water closing on her head from every side, her ears hurting with it. The ache she'd been carrying all day went bright. Her teeth began to chatter, here in the heat, sweat running down her back under her coat. When she looked at her left hand, the two bare fingertips had gone white with frost, the rime creeping out over the edge of the linen. The crystal by her cheek sank and sank until she could look straight into it and see the grain of the stone.

Sylvanas's horse stopped dead.

It threw its head up and went sideways into the wall, scraping Sylvanas's boot along the stones. Cyndia's went the other way, half round, ears flat. The mare backed under Jaina, two short hard steps, then sidestepped toward the ditch. At the ring stones on the border they had done this once and then stood, ears forward, like horses that knew what wards were for and had been taught to stand to them. These didn't stand. Sylvanas was talking to hers, low and steady in Thalassian, her hand flat on its neck. Cyndia had leaned down along her horse's shoulder and was saying something into its ear. Both of them got their horses going again, but neither horse settled; they went on crabwise, tossing their heads, the whites of their eyes showing. Behind them a carter shouted at them to make up their minds.

Something warm ran over Jaina's lip.

"Jaina." Lyriel had come up at her knee again. "Your nose."

"I know."

She wiped it with the back of her wrapped hand. The linen came away red, and the feather came away with it at last. It stuck to her lip instead, and she had to blow it off.

"Get down a moment," Lyriel said. "Sit on the wall. I'll hold her for you. She'd be glad of a rest even if you wouldn't."

"No."

Lyriel looked at her, then at Sylvanas's back, then moved her own horse in until their knees were touching. Her hand came over Jaina's on the reins and held it there, steadying the mare. When the mare stopped fighting the bit, she let go.

For most of the road it had been sums. Marks on a sheet. Heavy and light, old and new, a jar under the boards and a sick man's gut and a line of loads in a clerk's book—all of it something she could hold at arm's length and turn over and think about. She'd been glad of the arm's length, if she was honest. It was gone now. It was in her teeth. Every breath of this dust had it in it, her own heart going hard against it. The mare could feel it too. The mare had never read a book in her life.

Stop, then. Sit on Lyriel's wall in the dust with her head between her knees until it eased. And then what? Wait for it to be bearable? Wait for a letter to go north, a council to read it, the hills to be quiet? She had stood in the mud on that hill today, waiting for a man to take a sheet of paper out of her hand. He hadn't.

She put her heels to the mare. The mare went, sideways and unwilling, and Jaina let her go sideways as long as she went forward too.

The walls ended.

The road came out from between the last two barns onto open ground at the top of a long low rise. The dust thinned, and Sylvanas pulled up on the crest with her horse still dancing under her, Cyndia beside her. Jaina brought the mare up between them with Lyriel at her knee and looked down, and there was Andorhal.

It was big. It filled the shallow valley below the rise from one side to the other, a long grey wall with the roofs packed in close behind it—slate and thatch and tile—chimney smoke going up from all of them. Above the roofs stood the granaries. They were tall square timber buildings on stone feet, five and six storeys of them, each with a hoist beam jutting out under the gable. On the nearest one a sack was going up on its rope while a man leaned out of the loft door with his arm out to take it. There were more beyond it, and more beyond those, a whole quarter of them. Grain dust lay over that quarter in a haze, the low sun coming through it and turning it gold.

Wagons stood in lines on the roads in. There was the line on this road, below her, going down to the gate the clerk had told them of. There was a longer one off to the right, on a broad straight road coming in to a bigger gate, and that must be the King's road. There were more further round, to the west, where she couldn't see the ends of them. They were all waiting their turn. They were all full.

And the noise came up the hill to her. Iron tyres on cobbles. A bell somewhere. A dog that wouldn't stop. Somebody was shouting numbers. A hammer was going, and a mill wheel; under the dog a man was singing a song she half knew—a rude one, badly. On the warm air, through the dust, she could smell the bake ovens.

She had braced herself for Millhaven. All afternoon her own head had been telling her to: the drag coming up through the road, the frost on her fingers, the horses going wild. She had expected an empty street at the bottom of this hill, doors standing open, nobody to answer.

It was working. Oh, it was working. Every road she'd marked on Sylvanas's sheet came in here, and every slip with Andorhal at one end of it. Down there the wagons were still going in full and the hoists were still taking up the sacks and the ovens were still baking. People would eat that bread tonight. They'd break it at supper and give the crusts to the children. And under the bell and the dog and the singing—under the whole bright busy noise of it—the drag beat on, slow and late, the same as at the bottom of the road but so much bigger that she could feel it in the roots of her teeth.

They hadn't been chasing it. Not really. It had been here the whole time, running, while they read the roads that ran into it. And somebody was keeping it running. Somebody in there knew which wagon went to which yard and which sacks went out on which road. Somebody kept the books for it.

Nobody was coming down this road behind her. Arthas was on his hill with his helmet on. His letter would go north tonight, and a council would sit and listen to it being read. Dalaran didn't know where she was. There was a captain's name a clerk had given them on the road. There was a gate, and the city.

Sylvanas looked across at her, waiting.

Jaina wiped her lip again with the back of the linen. "The market gate," she said.

Sylvanas put her horse down the hill. Jaina followed, the mare going sideways under her every few strides. She let her, keeping her head turned toward the gate.
