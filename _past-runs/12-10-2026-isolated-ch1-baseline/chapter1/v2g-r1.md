# Chapter One: The Border

The last of the Amani axethrowers broke from the ferns three heartbeats later than Sylvanas expected.

She had already drawn—had drawn when the one before him went down howling in the gully, and she held the string at her cheek through the hush that came after. The woods had gone quiet around something large deciding whether to run. Her shoulder burned. A bead of sweat crept from her hairline toward her left eye, and she let it. Below her the slope dropped toward a stand of young silverleaf, and past it the trees thinned to a gap no wider than a hay cart, the only clean way back over the ridge to the Amani side. He would have to cross it.

He came out low and fast, an axe in each fist and a third bouncing on a cord across his back, his long splayed feet tearing up the ferns. Trolls always ran as though the ground owed them money. He took his first ten strides under the silverleaf, where she had no line. She let him have them. She let him have the eleventh too, when he jinked left around a boulder and looked back up the slope at nothing.

On the twelfth he reached the gap and stepped into the light.

She loosed. The string slapped her bracer, and the arrow went down the hill in a flat grey line. It took him under the arm, where the jerkin he'd stripped off some dead hunter didn't close. A breath later the sound came back up to her, a wet, heavy *thock*. He ran two more strides on dead legs. Then he folded into the bracken and didn't get up.

Sylvanas lowered the bow and rolled her shoulder once, slowly, until the old ache settled back into the joint. She'd brought it home from a winter spent lying on a frozen ridge above Zul'Aman. It had never entirely left.

"That's eight," someone called down the slope. Someone else started a laugh and swallowed half of it.

Standing on the rock with the bow loose in her hand, she counted them herself anyway. Two in the creekbed where the patrol had sprung them. Three along the deer track, one still twitching, which her sergeant was seeing to with the butt of his spear. One in the gully. One under the oak, pinned to the trunk by her second arrow before her third finished it. And this one in the gap, his axes scattered in the crushed ferns. Eight. She had read eight sets of tracks at the creek. Nobody had gone over the ridge to warn the rest of the warband.

Down the slope her rangers came out of cover one at a time, bows half-drawn, still facing the treeline. Good. The youngest had a long red scratch down her forearm where a thrown axe had kissed her on its way into a silverleaf trunk; she held it up to the light and scowled at it as though it had insulted her family. That was the worst of it. No one carried. No one limped.

Sylvanas went down the slope sideways, heel first on the wet stone, choosing footholds before she'd consciously seen them. That root would hold. That slab would slide.

Up close the troll smelled worse, sour and coppery under the musk of whatever grease he'd rubbed into his hair. She set a boot on his ribs, worked her arrow loose with a twist and a pull, and turned the head to the light. Bent at the tip. She'd have to rehone it.

Crouching, she wiped the steel on a frond, both sides. As the troll-stink thinned, the other smell came back underneath it: the sweet green air of Eversong, honey and sap and the peppery bite of whatever was blooming in the gold canopy this week. She had smelled it every day of her life.

Above her a thrush tried a few notes. Stopped. Tried again. A second bird answered, then a third, and within ten breaths the woods had gone back to their business. Sylvanas stayed crouched a moment longer than she needed to, listening.

The light through the leaves had deepened from white to amber. Behind her on the deer track the horse-holder was bringing the mounts up on a long lead, fitted tack creaking, the horses blowing at the smell of blood and settling anyway. Someone was already arguing about who had taken the troll in the creekbed.

She liked this part of the day best, the quiet after the kill, with the work done properly and everyone she'd brought out still alive to be irritating.

"Clean," Lor'themar said, coming down the deer track with his sword already sheathed. A smear of green crossed his jaw, fern sap or troll. A leaf had caught in his hair, and he didn't know it. "That last was a long shot."

"It was a short window. The shot was the easy part." She stood and slid the arrow into her quiver with the bent ones. "He looked back. They always look back."

"Not all of them."

"The ones who don't are the ones who make it over the ridge." She lifted her chin at his head. "There's a leaf in your hair."

He found it on the second try and flicked it away. Then he tilted his head at the light through the trees, doing the sum she had been avoiding for the past hour. "If we turn north now, we're in Silvermoon by full dark. Fresh horses at Fairbreeze if we want them. You'd have a night's sleep before the session."

There it was. Before she could stop it she was back in the Convocation chamber at Sunfury Spire three months ago: high gold windows that didn't open, lilies and lamp oil and warm velvet, a treasury magister reading out, line by line, for most of an afternoon, the cost of every fletching and bowstring and horseshoe the Farstriders had used in a year. She had counted the windows, then the panes in the windows. At one point she had counted the pearls on the neckline of the woman across from her, getting a different number each time because the woman kept breathing.

Tomorrow would be worse. Tomorrow was the frontier allotment, and the frontier allotment meant her.

"The southern stones," Sylvanas said. When Lor'themar only waited, she gestured south with the bow. "The ring along the Thalassian Pass road. We're halfway there already, and it's a waste not to walk them. We follow the ring as far as the post and come back up through Tranquillien. A day. Two at most."

He was silent. Her own rangers had walked the southern ring three weeks ago, and she had signed the report herself. He let it sit exactly as long as it took her to know that he knew, and to know that he knew she knew. He had been doing that to her for longer than most human kingdoms had existed.

Then he turned his head and called up the track. "Mount up. We're going south."

A groan went along the patrol. Someone said, not quite quietly enough, "*South?*" Her sergeant told him to shut up and get on his horse.

The mare lipped at her sleeve while she checked the girth, hunting the dried apple she usually kept in that cuff. Sylvanas pushed the nose away and swung up onto the old ranger trace that followed the ward ring. The patrol strung out behind her and Lor'themar by twos, and for the first mile nobody talked.

The mare settled into the long, rolling walk Thalassian horses could hold from dawn to dusk, white ears swiveling at every squirrel. Lor'themar rode through the bars of light at her side, going gold and shadow and gold again.

If she let herself listen, the ward-stones hummed under all of it. She felt each one in her back teeth as she passed it, a faint pressure just below hearing that came up through the mare's hooves and into her spine. She had grown up with that hum. As a small girl she had thought the forest was singing to itself, and nobody had ever convinced her otherwise.

Three weeks had changed nothing. Moss on the north faces. Runes clean. Nothing chipped, nothing leaning. She checked every stone anyway, because she had said she would, and because Lor'themar was watching her do it.

"Lord Saltheril," Lor'themar said at the fourth stone, "intends to propose consolidating the southern posts." When she didn't answer, he went on as if she had. "Three posts into one. He has a map. He's had it painted." He ducked a low branch. "A very handsome map, I'm told. There's a little gilded elf on it, standing guard at the Pass."

"One elf."

"A large one. Very well armed."

"Then he can paint the trolls too, and they can fight it out on the parchment. Three posts into one leaves forty miles of ring with nobody on it."

"Yes. I imagine someone ought to stand up tomorrow and say so." His voice stayed perfectly pleasant. "With figures. In front of the Convocation. Someone whose name is on the reports."

"You're the one who enjoys standing up," she said.

"I don't enjoy it. I'm *good* at it. There's a difference, and it's mostly that I've stopped expecting anyone to listen." He rode with the reins slack in one hand. "They'll vote on it whether you're in the room or not, Sylvanas."

"They'll vote the same way whether I'm in the room or not."

"Not necessarily. Last time you were there, Saltheril lost his nerve halfway through his own motion and sat down."

"Last time I was there, I said one sentence."

"It was an excellent sentence." He let that sit for a few strides. "He still talks about it. He tells people you threatened him."

"I told him the trolls would be grateful for his support."

"Yes. That's the sentence."

She closed her hand hard on the reins and touched her heels to the mare's sides. The mare had been waiting all afternoon for permission and went from walk to a long, swinging canter in three strides, the trail opening ahead straight and soft and dappled. Behind her the patrol scrambled to follow, someone cursing, tack creaking and a hoof striking stone.

Lor'themar's gelding came up beside her inside fifty yards and stayed there, nose level with her stirrup. He rode it out at her side, sitting deep in the saddle, the reins still slack in one hand.

She held the canter for a quarter mile and eased back when the trail began to climb. The patrol came straggling up in twos and threes, red-faced and grinning, and fell back into file on their own.

A day. Two if she was careful. She would sleep in the bunkhouse and eat whatever terrible stew they were making this week. Nobody at that post would want anything from her—Cyndia never did. Their reports came up every ten days without fail: a troll sighting near the Pass, a lynx in the grain store, a request for bowstrings, the stones all reading clean. By the time she rode back through Tranquillien the session would be over, and she could be angry about whatever the Convocation had done from horseback.

Lor'themar had turned his head toward her, lips pursed on a remark. She could hear it coming. "Don't."

"I said nothing."

"You were about to."

"I was about to observe," he said, "that the stones look very well."

She opened her mouth to answer and stopped, listening. Hoofbeats, south of them and coming fast, a single horse at full gallop, the beats running together into one long drumroll under the canopy. The mare's head came up, ears pricking forward. Sylvanas had two fingers on the bowstring before she had decided to reach for it.

The rider came out of the south where the old Pass road crossed the trace a hundred yards ahead, bent low over his horse's neck. She knew the cloak before she knew the face, Farstrider green gone nearly black with road dirt, the hood thrown back, and she let the string go slack.

He saw them a heartbeat later and hauled on the reins. The horse, a rangy chestnut with the cropped mane of a post animal, fought the bit for three strides before dropping into a stiff, lurching trot. Foam hung in ropes from the bit rings and lather whitened its chest. When it stopped at last it stood splay-legged, head hanging, every breath sawing in and out of it.

"Ranger-General." He was young, with the kind of thin, freckled face that would still look nineteen when he was two hundred. He tried to salute and found his hand still wrapped in the reins. "Theris. From the Pass. Ranger Cyndia's post." He stopped, dragging in air. "Riding for Silvermoon. Didn't think—" Another breath, ragged at the bottom. "Didn't think to find you down here."

"You found me. Report."

He gave it in pieces, as Farstriders were taught when they had no breath to spare.

"One human. A woman. A mage." Breath. "Crossed the first ring before dawn, in the rocks east of the road. Nobody saw her cross." Breath. "Taken at the second ring. An hour past sunup. Kneeling at a stone."

"Kneeling."

"Hands on it, General. Studying it. She had a book open on the ground beside her. No papers. No seal. No escort. Nothing on her that says who sent her."

Her breastbone went cold. For an instant she was five years old at the bottom of the garden at Windrunner Spire, both palms flat on a runestone, the hum coming up through her wrists. Her mother caught her by the back of the collar—*not those, never those*. Then it was gone. She was sitting a horse in the bracken with a boy gasping at her.

"Did she fight?"

"No, General. She—" He frowned, hunting for the report word and not finding it. "She argued. She hasn't stopped. She says the leylines are wrong."

Beside her, Lor'themar's saddle creaked as he shifted. "Wrong how?" Sylvanas asked.

"She didn't say. Or she said, and none of us could follow it. She said it a great many times, General. Cyndia has her in irons in the storeroom. Her kit's impounded. Cyndia was starting through it when I left."

"Her name."

"She hasn't given one."

"Who sent her?"

"Won't say. Or doesn't know, or—" He shook his head. "Cyndia said to tell whoever I found that she doesn't know what the woman is or whether any of it means anything. She isn't ranked to decide. Someone had better come down who is."

That was Cyndia, word for word; Sylvanas could hear her saying it, flat and put-upon, arms folded across her chest. Behind her the patrol had come up, bunching along the trail. Every one of them was listening. So much for Cyndia never wanting anything from her.

"Dalaran," Lor'themar said.

"Possibly."

"Possibly a great many things." He was facing south down the road, past the roadside ward-stone with its moss gone dark on the north face. "But if it is Dalaran, and they learn from someone other than us that one of theirs is chained in a storeroom at the Pass, there will be a letter. Several letters. The Convocation will want to have known first."

"The Convocation always wants to have known first. Then it wants us to handle her."

He let the joke go by. "Someone has to tell them."

"Yes."

Someone had to ride south, see this mage, and decide what she was. Someone had to ride north and tell a hall full of lilies and velvet that a human had walked through the first ward ring in the dark without a single ranger seeing her. She could send her sergeant south, or Lor'themar, who would do it well. Then she would sit in the gold chamber tomorrow counting window panes, while at the Pass a stranger in irons explained to someone else what was wrong with the lines under Sylvanas's stones.

No.

Her ring. Her stones. Her rangers had put the irons on. A clumsy spy, an envoy who'd lost her letters and her sense with them, or both at once—with Dalaran, it could easily be both. It didn't matter which. She had been inside the wards with her hands on a stone. Sylvanas did not intend to hear about that secondhand, a week from now, from some magister holding a writ for her release.

"Take the patrol north," she said. Lor'themar turned his head, and she went on before he could speak. "Brief the Convocation. Tonight, if anyone's awake to hear it. Tell them a human mage crossed the first ring unseen and was taken at the second. Tell them we don't know who she is or who sent her, and that I'm finding out. Don't give them anything else. We don't have anything else."

"And the leylines?"

"Tell them she said it. Not that it's true."

He tilted his head, the way he had over the dead troll when she named the southern stones. This time she waited him out.

"You realize," he said, "that you are sending me to the session."

"You're good at it. You told me so."

"I said I was good at it. I don't recall volunteering." But he was already gathering his reins, turning the gelding's head. "And Saltheril?"

"Tell him to paint a mage on his map."

That got a twitch at one corner of his mouth, there and gone before she'd properly seen it. He twisted in the saddle and raised his voice up the trail. "Sergeant! North. We change horses at Fairbreeze."

"The runner stays with me," Sylvanas said. "Give him your remount, Sergeant. That one's finished."

The sergeant led the spare forward, a long-legged grey with a white sock. Theris slid down off the chestnut and very nearly sat when his boots hit the ground, catching himself on the stirrup leather. Someone pushed a waterskin into his hands, and he drank greedily until Sylvanas said his name. Then he wiped his chin and went to the grey. The youngest ranger took the chestnut's reins unasked, stroking its wet neck with her scratched arm and murmuring to it.

"Walk him the whole way," Sylvanas told her. "Small sips at the streams. Nothing more till he's cool."

"Yes, General."

They rode the last hundred yards to the crossing together. North of it the road ran broad and soft under the gold leaves toward Fairbreeze, ground to pale dust by a century of supply carts. South it narrowed into packed gravel and bare rock, climbing steep and straight toward the Pass.

Lor'themar brought his gelding alongside her one last time, leaning close. "Sylvanas." When she turned her head, he said, "Do try to leave her able to talk."

"I'll leave her whatever's useful."

He waited a moment longer, then nodded once and turned the gelding north. The patrol went after him by twos. The youngest twisted round in her saddle until the sergeant barked at her, and she faced front. Sylvanas sat listening to them go—tack creaking, a laugh that started and was cut off—until the sound faded out among the trees. Then the thrush had the woods to itself, somewhere up in the canopy, trying the same three notes.

She turned the mare south. The mare's shoes rang on the gravel.

The road climbed. Within a mile the gold canopy began to break apart overhead, and the silverleaf and the tall Eversong oaks gave way to pine and juniper and twisted thorn, grey boulders jutting up out of the thin soil. The honey smell went out of the air. Resin and stone dust replaced it, and under both a cold mineral bite pouring down off the peaks ahead. Without slowing, she drew her cloak closed at the throat.

She rode hard, and the mare let her, taking the grade in long, digging strides. Once the trees thinned, the wind came on flat and cold and smelling of snow that hadn't fallen yet. It pushed at Sylvanas's left side until she was leaning into it.

Theris rode behind her. He had taken the junior's place a length off the mare's left hip. Which left the space beside her stirrup empty.

Twice on the climb she turned her head to speak. The first time it was *that stone's set too close to the cut; if the bank goes, it goes with it*. She had the words nearly out before she remembered who had ridden north. The second time she never did remember what it had been. She breathed out through her teeth and stopped turning her head after that.

The ward-stones stood thicker here, as they always had near the Pass, where the border was narrowest and the old builders had set them close as stitches. The hum in her back teeth stopped being a hum and became a pulse, swelling as she came up on a stone and fading as she passed it, the gaps running together until it beat through the saddle. The mare flicked an ear at each one and did not break stride; she had done this road a hundred times. That was how the Pass was meant to feel. Every stone stood upright and mossed and clean-cut against the grey rock, and she checked each one anyway, foot to crown. Her eyes held on each one longer than they had under the gold, because a stranger had knelt at stones like these this morning and called what lay under them wrong.

A spy, then. Dalaran had them; everyone did. But a spy caught kneeling at a ward-stone in daylight was a poor one, and a spy who then told her captors exactly what she'd come to look at was no spy Sylvanas had ever heard of. A spy would have had a story ready. Lost on the mountain road. Gathering herbs.

An envoy, then. Dalaran kept those by the dozen, soft-handed and well-fed in violet, riding up the Pass road with banners and letters and an escort. Envoys did not come over the rocks in the dark. Envoys did not come alone. Or both.

None of the three told a Farstrider post that the leylines were wrong.

She knew the lines ran under Quel'Thalas and that the wards drew on them, the same as she knew there was bedrock somewhere under the forest floor. She could read a trail, a treeline, the set of a man's shoulders the moment before he lied about where he'd been. She could not read a leyline. Whoever this mage was, she had walked straight into the one subject in Quel'Thalas where Sylvanas would have to take someone's word.

The light had gone copper by the time the post came into view, a squat tower of grey stone on a shoulder of rock above the road, a timber palisade built tight against its back, a thread of chimney smoke torn sideways by the wind. A ranger on the wall shouted down into the yard, and the gate began to swing.

Sylvanas put the mare through the moment the gap was wide enough and dropped to the packed earth before the mare had stopped.

"Cyndia." She pitched it low. Everyone in the yard had already turned, and so had the two on the wall, a boy and a grey-haired woman with a longbow across her knees. "Eyes south," she said, and they jerked round.

Cyndia came out of the tower door wiping her hands on a rag. Lean and scout-built, young for a post commander, she wore her auburn braid frayed loose after a day and a night without sleep. The rims of her green eyes had gone pink, and when she saluted she forgot the rag, which went up with her hand to hang beside her ear.

"General." She lowered both. "Theris found you, then."

"He found me." Sylvanas held out the mare's reins without looking to see who took them. Someone did. The yard smelled of horse, woodsmoke, and scorching grain. "Inside. Tell me as we go."

The tower's ground floor was one room, and it was never warm. The only window was a slit in the south wall, letting in a blade of copper light and a steady whine of wind. Beside the door a ward-lamp burned in an iron cage, the same hard blue-white glare every lamp along the border gave. Four rangers stood about the room. On the hearth a pot of barley sat over the coals with a crust going black around its rim, and no one had stirred it in some time.

"I have Theris's version," Sylvanas said. "Give me yours. Start before dawn."

"Before dawn's the trouble, General. Nobody saw her before dawn." Cyndia tossed the rag onto the table. At the far end, squared away from the tally sheets, sat a thick bundle wrapped in oilcloth. "Feren tracked her back once we had her in. She came up the scree east of the road—in the dark, which I wouldn't have done with a rope and a lantern—and crossed the first ring between the sixth stone and the seventh. Then she went along it. Stone to stone. Six of them, west along the trace, before she cut north for the second ring. Feren found where she'd knelt at every one. Two knee-marks in the dirt, and a flat place beside them where she'd set something down."

"The book."

"The book."

Sylvanas found the first ring on the map pinned beside the hearth. Six stones. Alone, in the dark, on a mountain she didn't know, a stranger had walked a quarter-mile of Sylvanas's border and laid her hands on it six times. "Go on."

"Feren came up on her at the second ring about an hour after sunup. She had two hundred yards on him, General, and she didn't run. She stood up and asked him to wait. Said she had three stones left in that stretch and it wouldn't take her an hour, and then he could do what he liked. He was still angry about it when he brought her in."

"And since?"

"Since, she's asked for her book back every time I've gone in. And an escort out to the stones—she says we can chain her to them if we like. Then it's the lines again. I'm not ranked for that kind of talk, General, and never pretended to be."

"Theris says she claims the leylines are wrong."

"Theris says what she says. This is me. I sent Liron out to the stone she'd been kneeling at, in case she'd done something to it. He put his hands on it the way the magisters showed us at Fairbreeze. He says it reads wrong. Reads like a cracked stone reads under your palms—only there's not a mark on it. So he tried the next one, and the one after. Six since noon, General, second ring and first. Every one looks as it has for a hundred years. He says every one reads like the trouble's inside it. Like knocking on a cask you know is full and hearing it answer hollow."

Sylvanas had ridden past those stones an hour ago. She'd felt them come up through the saddle, quick and close, exactly as they always did, and checked every one. The muscles at the base of her skull drew tight, and she went very still. "Did she do it?"

"I don't know." Cyndia said it straight out. "Nor does Liron. Could be her, could be there before her, could be nothing but his hands are tired. He wants a magister. I told him he could have one when I did."

"Where's her kit?"

"In there with her, chained to the spear rack. Staff, pack, and a belt full of pouches I haven't opened and won't." Cyndia jerked her chin at the barred door in the back wall, where a ranger sat on a keg with a spear across his knees. "The book I kept."

Sylvanas could make three women out of that: one arrogant, one frightened, and one who'd been right in the wrong country.

"There's one more thing," Cyndia said, arms folded hard over her chest. "Something in her things. I'd rather you looked at her first, General. Then I'll show you, and you can tell me I've got it wrong."

"Have you?"

"I'd like very much to have."

Sylvanas weighed her. Cyndia stood there, tired and stubborn and not budging, while the barley went on burning behind her. Somebody would have to scrape that pot.

She crossed to the barred door in three strides. The ranger on the keg stood up so fast the keg rocked. "Open it."

He lifted the bar from its brackets and swung the door inward. Cold air met her in the doorway.

The storeroom had no hearth. It kept the post's salt pork and flour and lamp oil, and the stone held the mountain's chill. A second ward-lamp hung from a ceiling hook, smaller than the one by the door, throwing its blue-white light straight down in a hard circle. In the middle of the circle was a chair.

Sylvanas took the room first, as she always took a room. One door, the one she stood in. A slit window high in the east wall, too narrow for a cat. A ranger in the far corner, bow strung, an arrow laid across it but not nocked. Along the north wall stood the post's spear rack, mostly bare this late in the season, and on the end pegs hung a staff of plain dark wood shod in worn metal, a chain run through its crook and padlocked to the rack's iron upright. A pack slumped beneath it. From the next peg hung a leather belt, every pouch still buckled shut.

Then the chair, and the woman in it. She had expected violet: silk, and rings on soft fingers, and the smooth, well-fed face of someone who had spent her life in rooms where people listened. Every Kirin Tor she had met, on the Pass road and in the gold chambers of Silvermoon, had worn that face. This one hadn't.

She was young. Even by human reckoning she was twenty or not much past it; by any elven measure she was barely out of the nursery. She wore a long travel robe of heavy blue-grey wool, double-breasted and buttoned to the throat, cut like a ship's officer's coat and stiff with dried mud from hem to knee. Her boots were scuffed pale at the toes and split along one welt, the soles packed with grit. Her fair hair had been pinned up that morning, or the morning before, and had come down somewhere on the scree. The irons sat on her wrists over the robe cuffs, a chain running to a ring-bolt in the floor, short enough that sitting up straight took all its slack.

She was sitting up straight.

The cold ward-light showed every hard line of her face. The skin under her eyes had gone dark as a bruise, and her lips were chapped and split at one corner. She faced the doorway with the look Sylvanas had seen on rangers ordered to stand down from a fight they knew they could win. A muscle jumped along her jaw. She sat with her hands clasped in her lap, gripping her own fingers hard enough to whiten them.

Her knuckles were raw, the skin red and shiny and lifting at the edges. Sylvanas knew that burn. Rangers who wintered at the Pass got it when they were careless with their gloves; she'd had it herself, her first winter there, and hidden it from her mother for a month. But the Pass only got cold enough for that in deep winter, on the tops, and the woman had come up the scree well below them.

The first two fingers of her right hand were stained dark to the second knuckle, ink worked into every crease and down into the nail beds. A scribe's hand. A student's.

The mage drew breath to speak and held it. She took in Sylvanas once, fast—the bow, the road-grey leathers. Then she closed her mouth on whatever she'd meant to say.

She'd had a speech ready. She'd been giving it all day to Feren and Cyndia and Liron and anyone else who came through that door, people who wrote it down and didn't listen. Whether swallowing it now was sense or pride, Sylvanas couldn't tell yet.

Cyndia came up at her shoulder with the oilcloth bundle held against her chest in both arms. "Well?"

"She isn't what I expected."

"No, General. Nor me." Cyndia stepped past her to the edge of the light and peeled the oilcloth back with her fingertips. Under it was a book near the size of a ledger, bound in blue leather gone soft at the corners. "I'll tell you straight, I didn't want to open this. You hear things about mages' books. It sat on my table a full hour after Theris left before I'd so much as touch the cover, and when I did, I did it with a knife." She lowered her voice. "Not where she can hear it, General." Sylvanas stepped back over the threshold, and the ranger at the door eased it to until only a hand's width of blue light was left. Cyndia turned the book. "Here."

An inscription ran across it in a long, slanting, old man's hand, the ink faded to brown. Cyndia read it low, picking over each word as she would a dispatch. "'For Jaina Proudmoore, at the end of her first year in the Citadel. Write in the margins. The authors were wrong about a great deal. Antonidas.' That's the name in it, General. And the margins are full, every page I've looked at. Small writing. Same ink as—" She tipped her head toward the mage's right hand and didn't finish. "If the book's hers, then she's Antonidas's apprentice."

Sylvanas called up the watch-file from memory. It was a thin file, a few dozen human mages the Farstriders kept current because the Convocation would not, and she had read it on enough long nights at Windrunner Spire to see the pages. *Proudmoore. Kul Tiran; daughter of Lord Admiral Daelin Proudmoore. Taken into the Violet Citadel very young; apprenticed to Archmage Antonidas personally, his only such apprentice in some years. Fair. Slight. Gifted well beyond her age. Worth watching.* Someone had underlined the last two words years back. Beneath them, in a different hand, someone else had added a line: *Antonidas is said to be fond of her.*

Kul Tiran. The robe's ship's-officer cut, the high double row of horn buttons. The fair hair coming down. The ink.

Sylvanas set a hand on the doorframe, as she would on a missed stair in the dark. Her own heartbeat thudded loud over the whine of wind through the slit.

Antonidas's apprentice. The one apprentice, the one he was fond of, chained by the wrists to a ring in a Farstrider storeroom beside the salt pork. And Lor'themar riding north this very hour to tell the gold chamber, on her orders, that no one knew who the prisoner was.

Lor'themar had said several letters. There would be dozens, every one of them sealed in violet.

And under that, colder: Antonidas would never have sent her up the scree alone in the dark. Either the old Archmage had let his one apprentice walk out of Dalaran with no escort and no papers, for reasons Sylvanas couldn't begin to guess, or he had no idea where she was.

*Do try to leave her able to talk.*

Her face went hot. At the human for coming. At Antonidas for whatever he had done or failed to do. At six stones on her hillside that looked exactly as they always had and answered hollow under Liron's hands. Her jaw locked, and her fingers tightened on the bow grip until the wrapping creaked. She made herself unclench them one at a time.

The staff hung chained to the rack, well out of the mage's reach. Put her out, then. Send her down the Pass road under escort with an apology and a bill for the barley, and let Dalaran have her back. The Convocation would like that. It would also put the only person in Quel'Thalas who claimed to know what was wrong with the stones across the border by morning, leaving Sylvanas with Liron and his tired hands. Or leave her in irons one more night and wait for a magister from Silvermoon who could tell a true thing about a leyline from a false one. That was a night, a hard ride, and another day after it, with the stones standing out on the hillside the whole time and Sylvanas not knowing what was inside them.

Waiting was no better than letting her go. It only looked more careful.

Which left going in herself.

She had no reason to trust a word the woman said. She would have to go and find one.

Without looking, she held her bow out to the side. Cyndia took it, shifting the book into the crook of one arm.

Sylvanas walked into the light. The rangers moved without being told, the one in the corner stepping back along the wall to give her a clear line. The ward-light slid up over the toes of her boots as she crossed into the circle. Cold came up off the flagstones and into her shins. Around her the rangers held still. Wind whined in the slit, and chain clinked faintly as the mage moved her hands in her lap, then went quiet.

An arm's length from the chair, she halted. Close enough to reach. Close enough to be reached, if the woman had anything left to reach with. The mage had to tip her head back, and she did, chin up.

At this range, the eyes were blue, the whites shot red with sleeplessness, and she kept them on Sylvanas, though the staff and the open door were both a turn of the head away. A fine tremor ran through the clasped hands, and the mage was holding them still against it by will alone. She smelled of wet wool and old sweat and, very faintly, of a sharp clean smell Sylvanas couldn't place, the air on the tops before snow.

Sylvanas stood over her—the raw knuckles, the ink, the chapped mouth, shut now after a day of arguing—and let the silence run.

"Start talking."
