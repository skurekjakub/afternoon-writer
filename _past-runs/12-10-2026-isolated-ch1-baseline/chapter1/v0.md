# Chapter One: The Border

The last troll broke from the ferns three heartbeats later than Sylvanas expected.

She had already drawn. She had drawn when the one before him went down howling in the gully and the woods went quiet in the way woods did when something large was deciding whether to run, and she had held the string at her cheek through all of that quiet while her shoulder began to burn and a bead of sweat crept down from her hairline toward her left eye. She didn't blink it away. Below her the slope dropped off in a tumble of moss-slick granite and bracken toward a stand of young silverleaf, and past the silverleaf the trees thinned to a gap no wider than a hay cart. The gap was the only clean way back over the ridge to the Amani side. He would have to cross it.

He came out low and fast, an axe in each fist and a third bouncing on a cord across his back, his tusks yellow and ringed with copper, his long splayed feet tearing up the ferns. Trolls always ran as though the ground owed them money. He took his first ten strides under the silverleaf, where she had no line, and she let him have them. She let him have the eleventh too, when he jinked left around a boulder and looked back up the slope at nothing.

On the twelfth he reached the gap, and the light found him.

She loosed. The string slapped her bracer, and the arrow went down the hill in a flat grey line and took him under the arm, where the leather jerkin he'd pulled off some dead hunter didn't close. The sound came back up to her a breath later—a wet, heavy *thock*, like a cleaver going into a side of pork. He ran two more strides on legs that hadn't heard yet. Then he folded into the bracken, and the bracken closed over him, and he didn't get up.

Sylvanas lowered the bow. Her shoulder ached deep in the joint, the old ache she'd brought home from a winter spent lying on a frozen ridge above Zul'Aman, and she rolled it once, slowly, until it settled back into place.

"That's eight," someone said down the slope, and someone else let out a breath that turned halfway into a laugh.

She counted them herself anyway, standing on the rock with the bow loose in her hand. Two in the creekbed where the patrol had sprung them. Three along the deer track, one of them still twitching, which her sergeant was already seeing to with the butt of his spear. One in the gully. One under the oak where her second arrow had pinned him to the trunk by the shoulder before her third finished it. And this one, in the gap, with his axes scattered around him in the crushed ferns like dropped cutlery. Eight. The sign at the creek had said eight. Nobody had gone over the ridge to tell the rest of the warband what had happened to their friends.

Down the slope, her rangers came out of cover the way she'd taught them to—one at a time, bows still half-drawn, eyes on the treeline instead of on each other. Good. The youngest of them had a long red scratch down her forearm where a thrown axe had kissed her on its way into a birch, and she was holding it up to the light and scowling at it as though it had insulted her family. That was the worst of it. No one carried. No one limped.

Sylvanas went down the slope sideways, heel first on the wet stone, the way she'd gone down a thousand slopes like it, and her body picked the footholds before her eyes had finished finding them. That root would hold. That slab would slide. The moss on the north face of the boulder was thicker and drier and would take her weight better than the bare rock beside it. She didn't think about any of it. She hadn't had to think about a hill in this forest since she was a girl.

The troll smelled worse up close. They always did—sour and coppery underneath, like a butcher's drain on a hot day, with the musk of whatever grease they rubbed into their hair laid over the top. His blood was soaking dark into the fern beds around him. She set a boot on his ribs, worked her arrow loose with a twist and a pull, and looked at the head. Bent at the tip. She'd have to rehone it.

She crouched and wiped the steel on a frond, both sides, and as the troll-stink thinned the other smell came back underneath it: the sweet green air of Eversong, honey and sap and the faint peppery bite of whatever was blooming up in the gold canopy this week. She had smelled it every day of her life. She still noticed when it came back.

Above her, a thrush tried a few notes. Stopped. Tried again, and this time a second bird answered it from farther up the ridge, and then a third, and within the space of ten breaths the woods had decided the killing was over and gone back to their business.

Sylvanas stayed crouched a moment longer than she needed to, listening to them.

It was early afternoon. The light through the leaves had gone from white to the deep amber it turned when the sun tipped past its height, and the air down here in the hollow was starting to cool. Somewhere behind her, on the deer track, the horses were blowing and stamping as the horse-holder brought them up on a long lead—she could tell her own mare by the sound of her, the impatient double snort she gave when she'd been made to stand too long. Her sergeant said something low, and the youngest one laughed at it. Someone was already arguing about who had taken the troll in the creekbed, which meant they were all fine.

This was the part of the day she liked best. Not the kill itself. The quiet after it, when the work was done and done properly and everyone she'd brought out was still alive to be irritating.

"Clean," Lor'themar said, coming down the deer track behind her with his sword already sheathed. He had a smear of something green across his jaw, fern sap or troll, and a leaf stuck in his hair that he didn't know about. "That last was a long shot."

"It was a short window. The shot was the easy part." She stood, sliding the arrow back into her quiver with the bent ones. "He looked back. They always look back."

"Not all of them."

"The ones who don't look back are the ones who make it over the ridge." She glanced at him. "There's a leaf in your hair."

He found it on the second try and flicked it away, and then he looked up through the trees at the light and did the sum she had been avoiding doing for the past hour. "If we turn north now, we're in Silvermoon by full dark," he said. "Fresh horses at Fairbreeze if we want them. You'd have a night's sleep before the session."

There it was.

Her stomach went tight and small, the way it did on the edge of a bad drop, and before she could stop it she was back in the Convocation chamber three months ago: the high gold windows that didn't open, the smell of lilies and lamp oil and warm velvet, and a treasury magister reading aloud—aloud, line by line, for most of an afternoon—the cost of every fletching and bowstring and horseshoe the Farstriders had used in a year. She had counted the windows. She had counted the panes in the windows. At one point she had counted the pearls on the neckline of the woman sitting across from her and come up with a different number each time, because the woman kept breathing.

The budget session tomorrow would be worse. It was the frontier allotment, and the frontier allotment meant her.

"The southern stones," Sylvanas said.

Lor'themar waited.

"The ring along the Pass road. We're halfway there already." She gestured south with the bow, toward the long climb of the ridge and the stretch of forest beyond it that ran down to the second ward line and the border post past that. "If we're this close, it's a waste not to walk them. We can follow the ring as far as the post and come back up through Tranquillien. A day. Two at most."

He looked at her. He didn't say anything. He didn't raise an eyebrow, or sigh, or point out that the southern ring had been walked three weeks ago and that she had signed the report herself; he just looked at her, steadily, for exactly as long as it took her to know that he knew, and to know that he knew she knew. It was one of the more irritating things about having served alongside someone for longer than most human kingdoms had existed.

Then he turned his head and called up the track. "Mount up. We're going south."

A groan went along the patrol. Someone said, not quite quietly enough, "*South?*" and her sergeant told him to shut up and get on his horse.

Her mare swung her head around and lipped at Sylvanas's sleeve while she checked the girth, looking for the dried apple that usually lived in that cuff. Sylvanas pushed the nose away, swung up, and turned onto the old ranger trace that followed the ward ring. The patrol strung out behind her and Lor'themar by twos, and for the first mile nobody talked.

Sylvanas let her mare find her own pace. The mare knew this trail almost as well as she did, and she settled into the long, rolling walk that Thalassian horses could keep up from dawn to dusk without tiring, her white ears swiveling at every squirrel. The saddle creaked under Sylvanas in its quiet, familiar way. The leaves overhead were turning the light the color of dark honey, and it fell across the trail in long bars that the horses walked through, one after another, so that Lor'themar beside her went gold and shadow and gold again.

Under all of it, if she let herself listen, was the hum.

It wasn't a sound exactly. It was more that her back teeth knew when she passed a ward-stone, a faint pressure like a held note just below hearing, and here near the southern ring the stones were close enough together that the note never quite went away. It came up through the mare's hooves and the saddle and into her spine, swelled as they passed one of the tall grey runestones half sunk in the bracken beside the trail, faded, and swelled again at the next. Farther south, toward the Pass, they stood thicker still. She had grown up with that hum. When she was very small, she had thought the forest was singing to itself, and nobody had ever quite convinced her it wasn't.

The stones looked exactly as they had looked three weeks ago. Moss on the north faces. Runes clean. Nothing chipped, nothing leaning. She looked at every one of them anyway, because she had said she would, and because Lor'themar was watching her do it.

"Lord Saltheril," Lor'themar said, at the fourth stone, "intends to propose consolidating the southern posts."

She didn't answer.

"Three posts into one. He has a map. He's had it painted." He paused to duck a low branch, came back up. "It's a very handsome map, I'm told. There's a little gilded elf on it, standing guard at the Pass."

"One elf."

"A large one. Very well armed."

"Then he can paint the trolls too, and they can all fight it out on the parchment." She watched a jay flick across the trail ahead and vanish into the undergrowth. "Three posts into one leaves forty miles of ring with nobody on it."

"Yes. I imagine someone ought to stand up tomorrow and say so." His voice was perfectly pleasant. "With figures. In front of the Convocation. Someone whose name is on the reports."

"You're the one who enjoys standing up."

"I don't enjoy it. I'm *good* at it. There's a difference, and it's mostly that I've stopped expecting anyone to listen." He glanced over at her. "They'll vote on it whether you're in the room or not, Sylvanas."

"They'll vote the same way whether I'm in the room or not."

"Not necessarily. Last time you were there, Saltheril lost his nerve halfway through his own motion and sat down."

"Last time I was there, I said one sentence."

"It was an excellent sentence." He let that sit for a few strides. "He still talks about it. He tells people you threatened him."

"I told him the trolls would be grateful for his support."

"Yes. That's the sentence."

The irritation came up in her like heat off a forge, and she didn't answer it. She touched her heels to the mare's sides instead, and the mare, who had been waiting all afternoon for permission, went from her walk into a long, swinging canter in three strides. The trail opened ahead of them, straight and soft and dappled, and the wind of their passing pushed her hair back off her face and cooled the sweat drying on her neck. Behind her she heard the patrol scramble to follow, someone cursing, harness jingling.

Lor'themar's gelding came up beside her before she'd gone fifty yards and stayed there, nose level with her stirrup, matching her stride for stride. He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He had known her long enough to know that she was going to make him ride rather than make her talk, and he rode, easily, sitting deep in the saddle as if this were what he'd intended all along.

It was the one thing about him she had never been able to fault. Most people, when she put her heels in, fell behind.

She held the canter for a quarter mile, until the trail began to climb and the mare's breath roughened, and then eased her back to the walk. The hum swelled again around them as they passed another stone. Her shoulder had stopped aching. The air smelled of crushed bracken and horse and the faint cool green of the stream somewhere off to their left, and the patrol came straggling up behind in twos and threes, red-faced and grinning, and fell back into file without being told.

A day, she thought. Two if she was careful about it. She would walk the ring down to the border post and sleep in the bunkhouse there and eat whatever terrible stew they were making this week, and nothing at the post would want anything from her, because nothing at that post ever did. Their reports came up every ten days, regular as tides: a troll sighting near the Pass, a lynx in the grain store, a request for bowstrings, the stones all reading clean. Nobody down there was expecting trouble from the south any more than she was. By the time she rode back up through Tranquillien the session would be over, and whatever the Convocation had done, it would already be done, and she could be angry about it from horseback.

Lor'themar was looking at her sidelong. She knew that look, too.

"Don't," she said.

"I said nothing."

"You were about to."

"I was about to observe," he said mildly, "that the stones look very well."

She opened her mouth to answer him, and stopped.

Hoofbeats. South of them, and coming fast—not the easy walk of patrol traffic, nor the trot of a supply rider with somewhere to be by evening, but a single horse at a full gallop, the beats running together into one long drumroll under the canopy. Her mare's head came up, ears pricked forward. All down the line, the birds had gone quiet again.

Sylvanas's fingers found the bowstring before she had decided to reach for it.

The rider came up out of the south where the old Pass road crossed the trace a hundred yards ahead, bent low over his horse's neck, and she knew the cloak before she knew the face—Farstrider green gone nearly black with road dirt, the hood thrown back and flapping behind him. She let the string go slack.

He saw them a heartbeat later and hauled on the reins. The horse, a rangy chestnut with the cropped mane of a post animal, fought the bit for three strides and then came down out of the gallop in a stiff, lurching trot that nearly put its rider over its ears. Foam hung in long ropes from the bit rings. Its chest and neck were white with lather, and where the lather had worn through, the coat was dark as wet bark and steaming in the cool under the trees. When it finally stopped, it stood with its legs splayed and its head hanging, and every breath went in and out of it with a sound like a bellows with a split in the leather.

"Ranger-General." He was young, with the kind of thin, freckled face that would still look nineteen when he was two hundred. He tried to salute and found his hand still wrapped in the reins. "Theris. From the Pass. Ranger Cyndia's post." He dragged in air. "Riding for Silvermoon. Didn't think—" Another breath, ragged at the bottom. "Didn't think to find you down here."

"You found me. Report."

He gave it to her in pieces, the way Farstriders were taught to give a report when they had no breath to spare for anything but the bones of it, and he had to stop between the pieces and pull air in through his mouth.

"One human. A woman. A mage." Breath. "Crossed the first ring before dawn, in the rocks south of the road. Nobody saw her cross." Breath. "Taken at the second ring. Just after sunup. Kneeling at a stone."

"Kneeling."

"Hands on it, General. Studying it. She had a book open on the ground beside her." He swallowed. "No papers. No seal. No escort. Nothing on her that says who sent her."

Something went cold and small under Sylvanas's breastbone, and for an instant she was five years old at the bottom of the garden at Windrunner Spire with both palms flat on a runestone, feeling the hum come up through her wrists, and her mother's hand was closing on the back of her collar—*not those, never those*. Then it was gone, and she was sitting a horse in the bracken with a boy gasping at her.

"Did she fight?"

"No, General. She—" He frowned, hunting for the report word and not finding it. "She argued. She hasn't stopped. She says the leylines are wrong."

Beside her, Lor'themar's saddle creaked as he shifted his weight. Sylvanas kept her eyes on the runner.

"Wrong how?"

"She didn't say. Or she said, and none of us could follow it." He wiped his mouth with the back of his glove, and the glove came away grey with dust. "She said it a great many times, General. Cyndia has her in irons in the storeroom. Her kit's impounded. Cyndia was starting through it when I left."

"Her name."

"She hasn't given one."

"Who sent her?"

"Won't say. Or doesn't know, or—" He shook his head. "Cyndia said to tell whoever I found that she doesn't know what the woman is or whether any of it means anything, and she isn't ranked to decide, and someone had better come down who is."

That was Cyndia, word for word; Sylvanas could hear her saying it, flat and put-upon, with her arms folded across her chest. Behind her the patrol had come up and bunched along the trail, and nobody was arguing about trolls now. The chestnut groaned and shifted, and a gob of foam dropped off its bit into the leaf litter.

So much for the post that never wanted anything from her.

"Dalaran," Lor'themar said.

"Possibly."

"Possibly a great many things." He was looking south down the road with his eyes narrowed against the bars of light. "But if it is Dalaran, and they learn from someone other than us that one of theirs is chained in a storeroom at the Pass, there will be a letter. Several letters. The Convocation will want to have known first."

"The Convocation always wants to have known first. It's the knowing that tires them."

He didn't smile. "Someone has to tell them."

"Yes."

There it was, laid out flat in front of her like Saltheril's map with nobody painted on it: two roads, and a body needed on each. Someone had to ride south and look at this mage and decide what she was, and someone had to ride north and stand up in a hall full of lilies and velvet and tell it that a human had walked through the first ward ring in the dark without a single ranger seeing her do it. She could send her sergeant south. She could send Lor'themar, and he would do it well, and she would sit in the gold chamber tomorrow counting window panes while a magister read out the cost of horseshoes, and somewhere at the Pass a stranger in irons would be explaining to someone else what was wrong with Sylvanas's stones.

No.

Her ring. Her stones. Her rangers had put the irons on. Whatever the woman turned out to be—a Kirin Tor spy who'd been clumsy, an envoy who'd lost her letters and her escort and her sense along with them, or both at once, which with Dalaran was never out of the question—she had been inside the wards with her hands on a stone, and that was not a thing Sylvanas intended to hear about secondhand, a week from now, from some magister holding a writ for her release.

"Take the patrol north," she said.

Lor'themar turned his head and looked at her.

"Brief the Convocation. Tonight, if anyone's awake to hear it. Tell them a human mage crossed the first ring unseen and was taken at the second, that we don't know who she is or who sent her, and that I'm finding out. Don't give them anything else. We don't have anything else."

"And the leylines?"

"Tell them she said it. Not that it's true."

He went on looking at her. It was the same look he'd given her in the hollow with the troll-stink still on them, steady and patient and far too knowing, and this time she held it and did not look away first.

"You realize," he said, "that you are sending me to the session."

"You're good at it. You told me so."

"I said I was good at it. I don't recall volunteering." But he was already gathering up his reins. "And Saltheril?"

"Tell him to paint a mage on his map."

That got a twitch at one corner of his mouth, there and gone before she'd properly seen it. He turned in the saddle and raised his voice up the trail. "Sergeant! North. We change horses at Fairbreeze."

"The runner stays with me," Sylvanas said. "Give him your remount, Sergeant. That one's finished."

The sergeant led the spare forward, a long-legged grey with a white sock, and Theris slid down off the chestnut and very nearly sat when his boots hit the ground; he caught himself on the stirrup leather and hung there a moment before he straightened. Someone pushed a waterskin into his hands. He drank as though he had forgotten what water was for, until Sylvanas said his name and he stopped, wiped his chin, and went to the grey. The youngest ranger took the chestnut's reins without being asked and stood stroking its wet neck with her scratched arm, murmuring to it.

"Walk him the whole way," Sylvanas told her. "Small sips at the streams. Nothing more till he's cool."

"Yes, General."

They rode the last hundred yards to the crossing together. North of it, the road ran broad and soft under the gold leaves toward Fairbreeze, its surface ground to pale dust by a century of supply carts; south, it narrowed into packed gravel and bare rock and went up, steep and straight, toward the Pass. The ring trace wandered off east and west into the bracken on either side, following its stones.

Lor'themar brought his gelding alongside her one last time. "Sylvanas."

She waited.

"Do try to leave her able to talk."

"I'll leave her whatever's useful."

He looked at her a moment longer, then nodded once and turned the gelding north, and the patrol went after him by twos, the youngest twisting round in her saddle to look back until the sergeant said something short and she faced front. Their hooves went quiet almost at once in the soft dust. Sylvanas sat and listened to them go—harness jingling, someone coughing, a laugh that started and was cut off—until the trees folded over the sound and there was nothing left but the thrush somewhere up in the canopy, trying the same three notes.

She turned the mare south, and the mare's shoes rang on the gravel.

The road climbed. Within a mile the gold canopy began to break apart overhead and let the sky through in long, ragged patches, and the silverleaf and the tall Eversong oaks gave way to something darker and harder: pine and juniper and twisted thorn, grey boulders shouldering up out of the thin soil like the backs of buried animals. The honey smell went out of the air. What came in to replace it was resin and stone dust and, under both, a cold mineral bite that poured down off the peaks ahead and got into her nose and the backs of her hands. The sweat that had dried on her neck under the leaves went chill. She drew her cloak closed at the throat without slowing.

She rode hard, and the mare let her. The mare had wanted to run all afternoon and now she had a whole mountain to do it on, and she took the grade in long, digging strides, her breath coming in steady blasts and her shoes striking sparks off the bigger stones. The wind found them in earnest once the trees thinned, flat and cold and smelling of snow that hadn't fallen yet, and it pushed at Sylvanas's left side until she leaned into it without noticing she had.

Theris rode behind her. She had told him nothing about where to ride, and he had dropped into place a length off the mare's left hip, the way a junior ranger would, and stayed there. Which left the space beside her stirrup empty.

Twice on the climb she turned her head to say something. The first time it was *that stone's set too close to the cut; if the bank goes, it goes with it*, and she had the words nearly out before she found nobody there but the wind; the second time she never did remember what it had been. She let her breath out long and slow through her nose and kept her eyes forward after that.

The ward-stones stood thicker here, as they always had near the Pass, where the border was narrowest and the old builders had set them close as stitches. The hum in her back teeth stopped being a hum. It became a pulse, swelling as she came up on a stone and fading as she passed it and swelling again at the next, so quickly now that the gaps between ran together into something like a second heartbeat felt through the saddle. That was right. That was how it was meant to feel at the Pass. Every stone she passed stood upright and mossed and clean-cut against the grey rock, and she looked at each of them anyway, and it was harder now than it had been that morning to look at one and then look away.

She worked at the mage the way she would work at a trail in hard ground, one mark at a time.

A spy, then. Dalaran had them; everyone had them. But a spy who got herself caught kneeling at a ward-stone in broad daylight was a poor one, and a spy who then sat in irons and told her captors over and over exactly what she had come to look at was no kind of spy Sylvanas had ever heard of. A spy would have had a story ready. Lost on the mountain road. Gathering herbs. Anything but the truth, and anything but a theory.

An envoy, then. Dalaran had those by the dozen—soft-handed, well-fed men and women in violet who came up the Pass road with banners and letters and an escort of guards, and waited at the post with their seals out until someone from Silvermoon rode down to fetch them. Envoys did not come over the rocks in the dark. Envoys did not come alone.

Both. With the Kirin Tor it was always worth considering both.

None of the three told a Farstrider post that the leylines were wrong.

She turned the word over and couldn't make it fit into any of them. The magisters talked about leylines the way farmers talked about weather—constantly, and in a language built to keep everyone else out of the conversation. She knew the lines ran under Quel'Thalas and that the wards drew on them, in the same way she knew there was bedrock somewhere under the forest floor. She could not have said what a right one looked like. She could not have said what a wrong one would.

Which meant that if the woman was lying, Sylvanas had no way yet to catch her at it. She could read a body. She could read a trail and a treeline and the set of a man's shoulders the moment before he lied about where he'd been. She could not read a leyline, and this mage, whoever she was, had walked straight into the one subject in Quel'Thalas where Sylvanas would have to take someone's word.

She went through the Farstrider watch-file in her head anyway, the handful of human mages whose power made them worth the ink, and got nowhere. A file told you what a mage could do. It didn't tell you what one would be fool enough to try.

The light had gone copper by the time the post came into view. It sat on a shoulder of rock above the road where the Pass began to narrow, a squat tower of grey stone with a timber palisade huddled behind it, a thread of chimney smoke being torn sideways by the wind. A ranger on the wall saw them coming and shouted something down into the yard, and the gate began to swing.

Sylvanas didn't wait for it to finish. She put the mare through the gap the moment it was wide enough, had her feet out of the stirrups before the mare had stopped, and dropped onto the packed earth of the yard with her bow still in her hand.

"Cyndia," she said. She didn't raise her voice. Everyone in the yard had already turned.

Including the two on the wall. Sylvanas looked up at them, a boy and a grey-haired woman with a longbow across her knees, both of them leaning on the parapet to watch the yard with the road and the whole open mountain at their backs.

"Eyes south," she said, and they jerked round as if she'd thrown something.

Cyndia came out of the tower door wiping her hands on a rag. She was a broad, square-built woman with pale hair cropped short as a recruit's, and she had the look of someone who'd been awake since before the mage crossed the first ring and fully expected to stay that way until the mage was someone else's problem. The rims of her eyes had gone pink. When she saluted, she forgot the rag, and it went up with her hand and hung beside her ear like a flag of truce.

"General." She lowered both. "Theris found you, then."

"He found me." Sylvanas held out the mare's reins without looking to see who took them. Someone did. Her thighs had stiffened on the climb, and the first few steps across the yard told her so in detail; the cold had gotten into the backs of her hands and the tips of her ears, and the yard smelled of horse, woodsmoke, and something scorching. "Inside. Tell me as we go."

The tower's ground floor was one room, and it was never warm. The walls were three feet thick and built of the same grey stone as the mountain, rough-dressed and sweating faintly where the outside cold met the fire, and the only window was a slit in the south wall that let in a blade of copper light and a steady whine of wind. A ward-lamp burned in an iron cage beside the door. It was fed off the grid like every lamp at every post along the border, and it gave the hard blue-white glare they all did, the kind that left no shadow worth hiding in and made everyone under it look a day from their deathbed. Four rangers were in the room. All of them were standing. Nobody had told them to.

On the hearth a pot of barley sat over the coals with a crust going black around its rim, and no one had stirred it in some time.

"I have Theris's version," Sylvanas said. "Give me yours. Start before dawn."

"Before dawn's the trouble, General. Nobody saw her before dawn." Cyndia tossed the rag onto the table, where it landed across a mess of tally sheets and a half-eaten heel of bread. "We only know what she did after the fact. Feren tracked her back this morning, once we had her in. She came up the scree east of the road—right up through it, in the dark, which I wouldn't have done with a rope and a lantern—and crossed the first ring between the sixth stone and the seventh."

"Theris told me that."

"He'd have told you she was taken at the second. What he wouldn't know, because Feren hadn't come back yet when I sent him, is that she didn't come straight on for the second ring. She went along the first. Stone to stone." Cyndia folded her arms over her chest; it seemed to be where they lived. "Six of them, west along the trace, and then she cut north to the second ring and started on that. Feren found where she'd knelt at every one. Two knee-marks in the dirt, and a flat place beside them where she'd set something down."

"The book."

"The book."

Sylvanas stood with her bow still in her hand and looked at the map pinned to the wall beside the hearth, the post's copy, stained at one corner where someone had once set a cup on it. Six stones of the first ring. In the dark, alone, on a mountain she didn't know, a stranger had walked a quarter-mile of Sylvanas's border and laid her hands on it six times, and no one had seen. Not one ranger on the wall. Not one.

"Go on."

"Feren came up on her at the second ring about an hour after sunup. He's not quiet, Feren, and the wind was behind him, and she heard him a good long way off. She looked up." Cyndia stopped and frowned at the floor, as though the floor had said something she disagreed with. "General, she had two hundred yards on him. She had the rocks, and she had the whole open hillside, and she's a mage. I've seen what they do when they'd rather be somewhere else. One breath and they're gone, and you're left standing in a smell like a struck flint."

"She didn't run."

"She stood up and asked him to wait." Cyndia's voice went flatter still, the way it did when she was handing over something she didn't want to be holding. "That's what he says, and I believe him, because he was still angry about it when he brought her in. She asked him to wait. She said she had three stones left in that stretch and it wouldn't take her an hour, and then he could do what he liked."

One of the rangers by the hearth shifted his weight. Sylvanas didn't look at him.

"And since?"

"Since, she's asked for her book back every time I've gone in. And an escort out to the stones. She says we can chain her to them if we'd like, she doesn't care. She says if we'd only let her finish she could tell us—" Cyndia made a small, irritated motion with one hand and folded it back under her arm. "And then it's the lines again. I've had it a dozen times now, General. I couldn't give it you back if you paid me. I'm not ranked for that kind of talk and I've never pretended to be."

"Theris says the stones are wrong."

"Theris says what she says. This is me." Cyndia drew a breath and let it out, and the breath had the whole day in it. "After we had her in, I sent Liron out to the second ring to look at the stone she'd been kneeling at. I thought she might've done something to it, so he put his hands on it the way the magisters showed us at Fairbreeze. He says it reads wrong. He says it reads the way a cracked stone reads, when you've got your palms on it."

"Is it cracked?"

"No. I went and looked myself. Not a mark on it. So he went on to the next one, and that reads the same way. And the one after." She paused. "He's done six since noon, General. Second ring and first. Every one of them stands up straight, and every one of them looks the way it's looked for a hundred years, and he says every one of them reads like the trouble's somewhere inside it, not on the face. He said it was like knocking on a cask you know is full and hearing it answer hollow."

Sylvanas had ridden past those stones an hour ago. She'd felt them come up through the saddle one after another, quick and close as stitches, exactly as they always did at the Pass; she'd looked at every one. Something tightened at the base of her skull, and she found she was standing very still.

"Did she do it?"

"I don't know." Cyndia didn't hesitate over it, which was the thing Sylvanas had always valued in her. "Liron can't say if it's her, or if it was there before her, or if it's nothing at all and his hands are tired. He says he isn't sure he'd know the difference. He says he wants a magister. I told him he could have one when I did."

"Where's her kit?"

"Locked down. Staff, pack, the book, and a belt full of little pouches I haven't opened and won't." Cyndia jerked her chin at the barred door in the back wall, where a ranger sat on a keg with a spear across his knees and his eyes on Sylvanas like everyone else's. "It's in there with her. On the spear rack, chained."

"In the same room."

"The tower's three rooms and a roof, General. Where would you have me put it, in the barley?" Cyndia didn't quite roll her eyes. "I wanted it somewhere she could see it wasn't going anywhere. And somewhere I could see her not touching it."

That was fair, and Sylvanas let it stand.

She went back through what she had. A human mage, come over the scree in the dark. Six stones of the first ring, a start on the second, and a book open in the dirt. A clean chance to run, and she'd used it to ask for an hour. And Liron with his tired hands out on the hillside, telling a stone it sounded hollow.

She could make three women out of that and couldn't choose between them. An arrogant one, so sure of her own importance that it never crossed her mind a border might have rangers on it. A frightened one, who'd seen something she couldn't carry by herself and had come running north with it. Or one who was neither, who'd simply been right about something, and had been right in the wrong country.

None of them was a spy. She was nearly certain none of them was an envoy.

"There's one more thing," Cyndia said.

Sylvanas waited.

"Something in her kit. I'd rather you'd looked at her first, General." Cyndia's arms tightened over her chest. "Then I'll show you, and you can tell me I've got it wrong."

"Have you?"

"I'd like very much to have."

Sylvanas looked at her a moment longer. Cyndia looked back, tired and stubborn and not budging, and the barley went on burning on the hearth behind her. Somebody would have to scrape that pot.

She crossed to the barred door. The ranger on the keg stood up so fast the keg rocked.

"Open it."

He lifted the bar out of its brackets and swung the door inward, and the cold came out to meet her.

The storeroom had no hearth. It was built of the same thick grey stone as the room behind her, but nobody had ever tried to warm it; it kept the post's salt pork and flour and lamp oil, and it held the mountain's chill the way a cellar does, so that her breath showed faintly the moment she stepped through. Someone had shoved the barrels and sacks back against the walls to clear the floor. A second ward-lamp hung from a hook in the ceiling, smaller than the one by the door, and it threw its blue-white light straight down in a hard circle, and in the middle of the circle was a chair.

Sylvanas took the room first, the way she always took a room. One door, the one at her back. A slit window high in the east wall, too narrow for a cat. A ranger in the far corner with his bow strung and an arrow laid across it, not nocked. Along the north wall stood the post's spear rack, its pegs mostly bare this late in the season, and on the end pegs, where the spare spears ought to have been, a staff—plain dark wood, shod in worn metal at the foot—with a length of chain run through its crook and padlocked to the rack's iron upright. A pack slumped on the floor beneath it. A leather belt hung from the next peg with every one of its pouches still buckled shut.

Then the chair, and the woman in it.

She had expected violet. She hadn't quite known she was expecting it until it wasn't there: silk, and rings on soft fingers, and the smooth, attentive, well-fed face of someone who had spent her whole life in rooms where people listened when she spoke. Sylvanas had met a good many Kirin Tor over the years, on the Pass road and in the gold chambers of Silvermoon, and they had come in a good many shapes, but every one of them had worn that face.

This one hadn't.

She was young. Even by human reckoning she was young, twenty or not much past it; by any elven measure she was barely out of the nursery. She wore a long travel coat of heavy blue-grey wool, double-breasted and buttoned high to the throat, cut like a ship's officer's coat and stiff with dried mud from the hem to the knee. Her boots had been good once. They had cracked across both toes and been resoled at least one time, and one lace had snapped and been knotted back together, and the knot was working loose. Her fair hair had been pinned up that morning, or the morning before, and had given up somewhere on the scree; most of it hung around her face and down over one shoulder, and there was grit in it. The irons sat on her wrists over the cuffs of the coat, and a chain ran from them to a ring-bolt in the floor, which left her room enough to sit up straight and not much else.

She was sitting up straight.

The ward-light came down cold on her face and found nothing soft in it. There were shadows under her eyes like thumbprints pressed into clay, and her lips were chapped and split at one corner, and she was looking at the door—at Sylvanas in the door—with the same look Sylvanas had seen on rangers ordered to stand down from a fight they knew they could win. Her jaw was set so hard that a muscle jumped along it. She had her hands clasped together in her lap, as far as the irons let her, and she was gripping her own fingers hard enough to whiten them.

Her knuckles were raw. On both hands the skin over them had gone red and shiny and was lifting at the edges in small pale flakes, the way it did on rangers who wintered at the Pass and got careless with their gloves. Sylvanas knew that burn. She'd had it herself, the first winter she rode the Pass, and hidden it from her mother for a month. But the Pass wasn't cold enough for that yet, not this early, not even on the tops, and the woman hadn't come over the tops.

The first two fingers of her right hand were stained dark to the second knuckle, the ink worked into every crease and down into the beds of the nails—the kind that never fully came out no matter how long anyone scrubbed. A scribe's hand. A student's.

The mage drew a breath as if to speak. Sylvanas watched it go into her and stay there. Her eyes went over Sylvanas once, fast—the bow, the leathers grey with road dust, the ranger at the door snapping straighter as Sylvanas passed him—and Sylvanas watched her decide something and close her mouth on it, and the breath went back out of her through her nose, slow and controlled and shaking very slightly at the end.

She'd had a speech ready. She'd been giving it all day, to Feren and Cyndia and Liron and anyone else who came through that door, and she had taken one look at Sylvanas and put it away. Whether that was sense or pride, Sylvanas couldn't tell yet. She wanted to know.

Nobody had believed her. It was in the set of her shoulders against the chair back, in her locked hands, in the way she held her chin up as if there were a weight hung on it that she would not let pull her head down. She was furious and she was exhausted, and she'd been both in this cold room, chained to a floor, the whole of the afternoon, while a barley pot burned in the next room and the stones she'd come to read stood out on the hillside without her.

Whatever this was, it hadn't come from any court.

Cyndia came in behind her. Sylvanas heard her stop just inside the door.

"Well?"

"She isn't what I expected."

"No, General. Nor me." Cyndia came forward into the light with something held against her chest in both arms. It was a book, a big one, near the size of a ledger, bound in blue leather gone soft at the corners and wrapped in a fold of oilcloth, and she peeled the oilcloth back now with her fingertips as if it might bite. "I'll tell you straight, I didn't want to open this. You hear things about mages' books. I had it sitting on the table a full hour after Theris left before I'd so much as touch the cover, and when I did, I did it with a knife." She turned it so the ward-light fell across it and opened it at the front. "Here."

There was an inscription on the flyleaf in a long, slanting, old man's hand, the ink faded to brown.

Cyndia read it aloud, carefully, the way she read dispatches. "'For Jaina Proudmoore, at the end of her first year in the Citadel. Write in the margins. The authors were wrong about a great deal. Antonidas.'" She looked up. "That's the name in it, General. And the margins are full, every page I've looked at. Small writing. Same ink as—" She tipped her head toward the mage's right hand and didn't finish. "If the book's hers, then she's Antonidas's apprentice."

The mage hadn't moved while Cyndia read. Sylvanas had been watching for it. She didn't flinch at the name, or look away, or look at the book. Her jaw only tightened a little further, and her eyes stayed on Sylvanas.

She didn't deny it.

Sylvanas went into the watch-file.

It was a thin file, the human mages, a few dozen names the Farstriders kept current because the Convocation would not, and she had read it through on enough long nights at the Spire that she could see the pages. *Proudmoore. Kul Tiran; daughter of Lord Admiral Daelin Proudmoore. Taken into the Violet Citadel very young; apprenticed to Archmage Antonidas personally, his only such apprentice in some years. Fair. Slight. Gifted well beyond her age. Worth watching.* Someone had underlined the last two words, years back, and someone else had added a line beneath it in a different hand: *Antonidas is said to be fond of her.*

Kul Tiran. Sylvanas looked at the coat, at the ship's-officer cut and the high double row of horn buttons. She looked at the fair hair coming down, and at the ink.

Her stomach dropped—a short, ugly lurch, like missing a stair in the dark—and all at once she could hear her own heartbeat, loud in her ears over the whine of the wind through the slit.

Antonidas's apprentice. Not a clerk who'd lost her letters. Not one of the hundred violet hangers-on Dalaran could spare and never miss. The one apprentice, the one he was fond of, chained by the wrists to a ring in the floor of a Farstrider storeroom beside the salt pork—and Lor'themar riding north this very hour to stand up in the gold chamber and tell them all, on her orders, that no one knew who the prisoner was.

There would not be several letters. There would be dozens, and every one of them sealed in violet.

And under that, colder: Antonidas would never have sent her up the scree alone in the dark. Not her. Either the old man had let his one apprentice walk out of Dalaran by herself, with no escort and no papers, for reasons Sylvanas couldn't begin to guess—or he had no idea where she was.

She didn't know which was worse. She suspected she was going to find out.

*Do try to leave her able to talk.*

The temper came up in her all at once, hot under the cold of the room: at the human for coming, at Dalaran for whatever it had done or failed to do, at six stones on her hillside that looked exactly the way they always looked and answered hollow under Liron's hands. It wanted somewhere to go. Sylvanas felt it reach her face and stopped it there. She let her breath out long and slow through her nose, until there was none left in her, and when she drew the next one the heat had gone down under it, and what remained was cold and clear and could be used.

She could put the mage out. Set her on a horse with an escort, send her down the Pass road to the first human post, and let Dalaran have her back with an apology and a bill for the barley. The Convocation would like that. It would also put the only person in Quel'Thalas who claimed to know what was wrong with the stones on the far side of the border by morning, and leave Sylvanas with Liron and his tired hands.

No.

She could leave her. One more night in irons in the cold, with nothing to do but sit and think about what she'd done; a night like that took the edge off most people, and by morning there might be a magister down from Silvermoon to sit across from her, someone who could tell a true thing about a leyline from a false one. But that was a night, and a hard ride, and another day after it. And every hour of it the stones would go on standing out on the hillside, upright and mossed and whole, and Sylvanas would go on not knowing what was inside them.

Waiting was no better than letting her go. It only looked more careful.

Which left this. It had always been going to leave this, from the moment Theris came up out of the south with his horse dying under him; she had only been taking the long way round.

She had no reason to trust a word the woman said. She would get none standing in the doorway.

She held her bow out to the side without looking, and Cyndia took it.

Sylvanas walked into the light. The rangers moved without being told—the one in the corner stepping back along the wall to give her a clear line, the one at the door drawing his spear in against his shoulder, Cyndia falling back to stand beside the rack with the staff on it and the book held against her chest. The ward-light lay on the stone floor in a hard blue circle, and it slid up over the toes of her boots as she crossed into it, over the pale dust of the Pass road ground into the seams. The cold came up off the flagstones into her shins. Nobody spoke. There was no sound in the room but the wind in the slit and a faint clink of chain as the mage's hands shifted in her lap, and then that stopped too.

She halted an arm's length from the chair, which was close enough. The mage had to tip her head back to hold her eyes. She did it, and didn't look away.

This close, she smelled of wet wool and old sweat and, very faintly, of something sharp and clean that Sylvanas couldn't place, like the air on the tops before snow.

Sylvanas looked down at her—at the raw knuckles and the ink and the chapped mouth that had been arguing all day and was shut now—and let the silence run on until it belonged to her.

"Start talking."
