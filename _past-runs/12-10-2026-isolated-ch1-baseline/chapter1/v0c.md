# Chapter One: The Border

The last troll broke from the ferns three heartbeats later than Sylvanas expected.

She had already drawn—had drawn when the one before him went down howling in the gully, holding the string at her cheek through the hush that came after. The woods had gone quiet around something large deciding whether to run. Her shoulder began to burn, a bead of sweat creeping from her hairline toward her left eye. She didn't blink it away. Below her the slope dropped in a tumble of moss-slick granite and bracken toward a stand of young silverleaf. Past the silverleaf the trees thinned to a gap no wider than a hay cart, the only clean way back over the ridge to the Amani side. He would have to cross it.

He came out low and fast, an axe in each fist and a third bouncing on a cord across his back, his long splayed feet tearing up the ferns. Copper rings glinted on his yellow tusks. Trolls always ran as though the ground owed them money. He took his first ten strides under the silverleaf, where she had no line. She let him have them. She let him have the eleventh too, when he jinked left around a boulder, looking back up the slope at nothing.

On the twelfth he reached the gap, and the light found him.

She loosed. The string slapped her bracer, the arrow going down the hill in a flat grey line. It took him under the arm, where the jerkin he'd stripped off some dead hunter didn't close. A breath later the sound came back up to her—a wet, heavy *thock*. He ran two more strides on legs that hadn't heard yet. Then he folded into the bracken and didn't get up.

Sylvanas lowered the bow, rolling her shoulder once, slowly, until the old ache settled back into the joint. She'd brought it home from a winter spent lying on a frozen ridge above Zul'Aman. It had never entirely left.

"That's eight," someone called down the slope. Someone else let out a breath that turned halfway into a laugh.

Standing on the rock with the bow loose in her hand, she counted them herself anyway. Two in the creekbed where the patrol had sprung them. Three along the deer track, one of them still twitching, which her sergeant was already seeing to with the butt of his spear. One in the gully. One under the oak, pinned to the trunk by her second arrow before her third finished it. And this one, in the gap, his axes scattered around him in the crushed ferns. Eight. The sign at the creek had said eight. Nobody had gone over the ridge to warn the rest of the warband.

Down the slope, her rangers came out of cover one at a time, keeping their bows half-drawn, eyes on the treeline instead of on each other. Good. The youngest had a long red scratch down her forearm where a thrown axe had kissed her on its way into a birch; she was holding it up to the light, scowling at it as though it had insulted her family. That was the worst of it. No one carried. No one limped.

Sylvanas went down the slope sideways, heel first on the wet stone, her body picking the footholds before her eyes had finished finding them. That root would hold. That slab would slide. The moss on the boulder's north face was thicker and drier, better for her weight than the bare rock beside it. She didn't think about any of it. She hadn't had to think about a hill in this forest since she was a girl.

Up close the troll smelled worse, sour and coppery under the musk of whatever grease he'd rubbed into his hair. His blood was soaking into the fern beds, darkening the fronds around him. She set a boot on his ribs, working her arrow loose with a twist and a pull, and turned the head to the light. Bent at the tip. She'd have to rehone it.

Crouching, she wiped the steel on a frond, both sides. As the troll-stink thinned, the other smell came back underneath it: the sweet green air of Eversong, honey and sap and the peppery bite of whatever was blooming in the gold canopy this week. Every day of her life she had smelled it. It still came back to her new each time.

Above her, a thrush tried a few notes. Stopped. Tried again. A second bird answered from farther up the ridge, then a third, and within ten breaths the woods had decided the killing was over and gone back to their business. Sylvanas stayed crouched a moment longer than she needed to, listening.

It was early afternoon. The light through the leaves had deepened from white to amber, the sun tipping past its height, and the air in the hollow was starting to cool. Behind her on the deer track, the horse-holder was bringing the mounts up on a long lead, bits jingling. She could pick out her own mare by sound alone, the impatient double snort she gave after standing too long. Her sergeant said something low, drawing a laugh from the youngest. Someone was already arguing about who had taken the troll in the creekbed, which meant they were all fine.

This was the part of the day she liked best. Not the kill. The quiet after it, the work done and done properly, everyone she'd brought out still alive to be irritating.

"Clean," Lor'themar said, coming down the deer track behind her with his sword already sheathed. A smear of something green crossed his jaw, fern sap or troll. A leaf had caught in his hair—he didn't know about it. "That last was a long shot."

"It was a short window. The shot was the easy part." She stood, sliding the arrow into her quiver with the bent ones. "He looked back. They always look back."

"Not all of them."

"The ones who don't are the ones who make it over the ridge." She glanced at him, lifting her chin at his head. "There's a leaf in your hair."

He found it on the second try, flicking it away. Then he looked up through the trees at the light, doing the sum she had been avoiding for the past hour. "If we turn north now, we're in Silvermoon by full dark," he said. "Fresh horses at Fairbreeze if we want them. You'd have a night's sleep before the session."

There it was. Her stomach went tight and small, as it did on the edge of a bad drop. Before she could stop it she was back in the Convocation chamber three months ago. High gold windows that didn't open. Lilies and lamp oil and warm velvet. A treasury magister reading out—line by line, for most of an afternoon—the cost of every fletching and bowstring and horseshoe the Farstriders had used in a year. She had counted the windows, then the panes in the windows. At one point she had counted the pearls on the neckline of the woman across from her, getting a different number each time because the woman kept breathing.

Tomorrow would be worse. Tomorrow was the frontier allotment, and the frontier allotment meant her.

"The southern stones," Sylvanas said. When Lor'themar only waited, she gestured south with the bow, toward the long climb of the ridge and the border post somewhere beyond it. "The ring along the Pass road. We're halfway there already. If we're this close, it's a waste not to walk them. We follow the ring as far as the post and come back up through Tranquillien. A day. Two at most."

He looked at her. He didn't raise an eyebrow, or sigh, or point out that the southern ring had been walked three weeks ago and that she had signed the report herself. He just looked at her, steadily, for exactly as long as it took her to know that he knew—and to know that he knew she knew. It was one of the more irritating things about serving beside someone for longer than most human kingdoms had existed.

Then he turned his head, calling up the track. "Mount up. We're going south."

A groan went along the patrol. Someone said, not quite quietly enough, "*South?*" Her sergeant told him to shut up and get on his horse.

Her mare swung her head around while Sylvanas checked the girth, lipping at her sleeve for the dried apple that usually lived in that cuff. Sylvanas pushed the nose away and swung up, turning onto the old ranger trace that followed the ward ring. The patrol strung out behind her and Lor'themar by twos, and for the first mile nobody talked.

She let the mare find her own pace, sitting loose in the saddle. The mare knew this trail almost as well as she did, settling into the long, rolling walk Thalassian horses could hold from dawn to dusk, white ears swiveling at every squirrel. The saddle creaked in its quiet, familiar way. Overhead the leaves turned the light the color of dark honey, laying it across the trail in long bars. Lor'themar rode through them at her side, going gold and shadow and gold again.

Under all of it, if she let herself listen, was the hum. It wasn't a sound exactly. Her back teeth knew when she passed a ward-stone, a faint pressure like a held note just below hearing. Here near the southern ring the stones stood close enough that the note never quite went away. It came up through the mare's hooves and into her spine, swelling as they passed one of the tall grey runestones half sunk in the bracken, fading, swelling again at the next. Farther south, toward the Pass, they stood thicker still. She had grown up with that hum. As a small girl she had thought the forest was singing to itself, and nobody had ever quite convinced her otherwise.

Three weeks had changed nothing. Moss on the north faces. Runes clean. Nothing chipped, nothing leaning. She looked at every stone anyway, because she had said she would, and because Lor'themar was watching her do it.

"Lord Saltheril," Lor'themar said at the fourth stone, "intends to propose consolidating the southern posts." When she didn't answer, he went on as if she had. "Three posts into one. He has a map. He's had it painted." He ducked a low branch, brushing the leaves off his shoulder as he came back up. "A very handsome map, I'm told. There's a little gilded elf on it, standing guard at the Pass."

"One elf."

"A large one. Very well armed."

"Then he can paint the trolls too, and they can fight it out on the parchment." A jay flicked across the trail ahead, vanishing into the undergrowth. "Three posts into one leaves forty miles of ring with nobody on it."

"Yes. I imagine someone ought to stand up tomorrow and say so." His voice stayed perfectly pleasant. "With figures. In front of the Convocation. Someone whose name is on the reports."

"You're the one who enjoys standing up," she said, watching the trail.

"I don't enjoy it. I'm *good* at it. There's a difference, and it's mostly that I've stopped expecting anyone to listen." He glanced over, holding the reins slack in one hand. "They'll vote on it whether you're in the room or not, Sylvanas."

"They'll vote the same way whether I'm in the room or not."

"Not necessarily." He eased the gelding around a root, letting the mare draw half a length ahead before he caught up. "Last time you were there, Saltheril lost his nerve halfway through his own motion and sat down."

"Last time I was there, I said one sentence."

"It was an excellent sentence." He let that sit for a few strides, the horses' hooves thudding soft in the leaf litter. "He still talks about it. He tells people you threatened him."

"I told him the trolls would be grateful for his support."

"Yes. That's the sentence."

Her fingers closed on the reins. Instead of answering, she touched her heels to the mare's sides. The mare had been waiting all afternoon for permission. She went from walk to a long, swinging canter in three strides, the trail opening ahead of them straight and soft and dappled. The wind of their passing pushed Sylvanas's hair back, cooling the sweat drying on her neck. Behind her the patrol scrambled to follow, someone cursing, harness jingling.

Lor'themar's gelding came up beside her inside fifty yards and stayed there, nose level with her stirrup, matching her stride for stride. He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He knew she would make him ride rather than make herself talk, so he rode, sitting deep in the saddle as if this were what he'd intended all along. It was the one thing about him she had never been able to fault. Most people, when she put her heels in, fell behind.

For a quarter mile she held the canter, easing back to the walk when the trail began to climb and the mare's breath roughened. Another stone went by, the hum swelling and fading around them. Her shoulder had stopped aching, and the air smelled of crushed bracken and horse and the cool green of a stream somewhere off to their left. Red-faced and grinning, the patrol came straggling up in twos and threes, falling back into file on their own.

A day, she thought. Two if she was careful. She would walk the ring down to the border post, sleep in the bunkhouse, and eat whatever terrible stew they were making this week. Nothing at that post would want anything from her—nothing there ever did. Their reports came up every ten days without fail: a troll sighting near the Pass, a lynx in the grain store, a request for bowstrings, the stones all reading clean. Nobody down there expected trouble from the south any more than she did. By the time she rode back through Tranquillien the session would be over. Whatever the Convocation had done would be done, and she could be angry about it from horseback.

Lor'themar was looking at her sidelong. She knew that look, too. "Don't."

"I said nothing."

"You were about to."

"I was about to observe," he said mildly, "that the stones look very well."

She opened her mouth to answer him and stopped, listening. Hoofbeats. South of them, coming fast—not the easy walk of patrol traffic, nor a supply rider's trot, but a single horse at full gallop, the beats running together into one long drumroll under the canopy. Her mare's head came up, ears pricking forward. All down the line, the birds had gone quiet again. Sylvanas's fingers found the bowstring before she had decided to reach for it.

The rider came out of the south where the old Pass road crossed the trace a hundred yards ahead, bent low over his horse's neck. She knew the cloak before she knew the face—Farstrider green gone nearly black with road dirt, the hood thrown back and flapping behind him—and she let the string go slack.

He saw them a heartbeat later, hauling on the reins. The horse, a rangy chestnut with the cropped mane of a post animal, fought the bit for three strides before dropping into a stiff, lurching trot that nearly put its rider over its ears. Foam hung in long ropes from the bit rings. Lather whitened its chest and neck; where it had worn through, the coat was dark as wet bark and steaming in the cool under the trees. When it stopped at last it stood splay-legged, head hanging, every breath sawing in and out of it.

"Ranger-General." He was young, with the kind of thin, freckled face that would still look nineteen when he was two hundred. He tried to salute, finding his hand still wrapped in the reins. "Theris. From the Pass. Ranger Cyndia's post." He stopped, dragging in air. "Riding for Silvermoon. Didn't think—" Another breath, ragged at the bottom. "Didn't think to find you down here."

"You found me. Report."

He gave it in pieces, as Farstriders were taught when they had no breath to spare, gulping air through his mouth between them.

"One human. A woman. A mage." Breath. "Crossed the first ring before dawn, in the rocks south of the road. Nobody saw her cross." Breath. "Taken at the second ring. Just after sunup. Kneeling at a stone."

"Kneeling."

"Hands on it, General. Studying it. She had a book open on the ground beside her." He swallowed. "No papers. No seal. No escort. Nothing on her that says who sent her."

Her breastbone went cold. For an instant she was five years old at the bottom of the garden at Windrunner Spire, both palms flat on a runestone, the hum coming up through her wrists. Her mother's hand was closing on the back of her collar—*not those, never those*. Then it was gone. She was sitting a horse in the bracken with a boy gasping at her.

"Did she fight?"

"No, General. She—" He frowned, hunting for the report word and not finding it. "She argued. She hasn't stopped. She says the leylines are wrong."

Beside her, Lor'themar's saddle creaked as he shifted his weight. Sylvanas kept her eyes on the runner. "Wrong how?"

"She didn't say. Or she said, and none of us could follow it." He wiped his mouth with the back of his glove, leaving the leather grey with dust. "She said it a great many times, General. Cyndia has her in irons in the storeroom. Her kit's impounded. Cyndia was starting through it when I left."

"Her name."

"She hasn't given one."

Sylvanas watched a gob of foam drop off the chestnut's bit into the leaf litter. "Who sent her?"

"Won't say. Or doesn't know, or—" He shook his head, still blowing. "Cyndia said to tell whoever I found that she doesn't know what the woman is or whether any of it means anything. She isn't ranked to decide. Someone had better come down who is."

That was Cyndia, word for word; Sylvanas could hear her saying it, flat and put-upon, arms folded across her chest. Behind her the patrol had come up, bunching along the trail. Nobody was arguing about trolls now. So much for the post that never wanted anything from her.

"Dalaran," Lor'themar said.

"Possibly."

"Possibly a great many things." He was looking south down the road, narrowing his eyes against the bars of light. "But if it is Dalaran, and they learn from someone other than us that one of theirs is chained in a storeroom at the Pass, there will be a letter. Several letters. The Convocation will want to have known first."

"The Convocation always wants to have known first. It's the knowing that tires them."

He didn't smile. "Someone has to tell them."

"Yes."

The choice lay flat in front of her, like Saltheril's map with nobody painted on it. Two roads, and a body needed on each. Someone had to ride south, look at this mage, and decide what she was. Someone had to ride north and tell a hall full of lilies and velvet that a human had walked through the first ward ring in the dark without a single ranger seeing her. She could send her sergeant south. She could send Lor'themar, and he would do it well. Then she would sit in the gold chamber tomorrow counting window panes, while at the Pass a stranger in irons explained to someone else what was wrong with Sylvanas's stones.

No.

Her ring. Her stones. Her rangers had put the irons on. A clumsy spy, an envoy who'd lost her letters and her sense with them, or both at once—with Dalaran, both was never out of the question. It didn't matter which. She had been inside the wards with her hands on a stone. Sylvanas did not intend to hear about that secondhand, a week from now, from some magister holding a writ for her release.

"Take the patrol north," she said. Lor'themar turned his head, and she went on before he could speak. "Brief the Convocation. Tonight, if anyone's awake to hear it. Tell them a human mage crossed the first ring unseen and was taken at the second. Tell them we don't know who she is or who sent her, and that I'm finding out. Don't give them anything else. We don't have anything else."

"And the leylines?"

"Tell them she said it. Not that it's true."

He went on looking at her, the same look he'd given her in the hollow with the troll-stink still on them, steady and patient and far too knowing. This time she held it, and she did not look away first.

"You realize," he said, "that you are sending me to the session."

"You're good at it. You told me so."

"I said I was good at it. I don't recall volunteering." But he was already gathering his reins, turning the gelding's head. "And Saltheril?"

"Tell him to paint a mage on his map."

That got a twitch at one corner of his mouth, there and gone before she'd properly seen it. He twisted in the saddle, raising his voice up the trail. "Sergeant! North. We change horses at Fairbreeze."

"The runner stays with me," Sylvanas said. "Give him your remount, Sergeant. That one's finished."

The sergeant led the spare forward, a long-legged grey with a white sock. Theris slid down off the chestnut and very nearly sat when his boots hit the ground, catching himself on the stirrup leather and hanging there a moment before he straightened. Someone pushed a waterskin into his hands. He drank as though he had forgotten what water was for, until Sylvanas said his name. Then he stopped, wiped his chin, and went to the grey. The youngest ranger took the chestnut's reins unasked, stroking its wet neck with her scratched arm and murmuring to it.

"Walk him the whole way," Sylvanas told her. "Small sips at the streams. Nothing more till he's cool."

"Yes, General."

They rode the last hundred yards to the crossing together. North of it, the road ran broad and soft under the gold leaves toward Fairbreeze, its surface ground to pale dust by a century of supply carts. South, it narrowed into packed gravel and bare rock, climbing steep and straight toward the Pass. On either side the ring trace wandered off east and west into the bracken, following its stones.

Lor'themar brought his gelding alongside her one last time, leaning close. "Sylvanas." When she turned her head, he said, "Do try to leave her able to talk."

"I'll leave her whatever's useful."

He looked at her a moment longer, then nodded once, turning the gelding north. The patrol went after him by twos. The youngest twisted round in her saddle to look back until the sergeant said something short, and she faced front. Their hooves went quiet almost at once, swallowed by the soft dust. Sylvanas sat listening to them go—harness jingling, someone coughing, a laugh that started and was cut off—until the trees folded over the sound. Nothing was left but the thrush somewhere up in the canopy, trying the same three notes.

She turned the mare south. The mare's shoes rang on the gravel.

The road climbed. Within a mile the gold canopy began to break apart overhead, letting the sky through in long, ragged patches. The silverleaf and the tall Eversong oaks gave way to something darker and harder: pine and juniper and twisted thorn, grey boulders shouldering up out of the thin soil. The honey smell went out of the air. Resin and stone dust came in to replace it, and under both, a cold mineral bite pouring down off the peaks ahead. It got into her nose and the backs of her hands. The sweat that had dried on her neck went chill. Without slowing, she drew her cloak closed at the throat.

She rode hard, and the mare let her. The mare had wanted to run all afternoon, and now she had a whole mountain for it. She took the grade in long, digging strides, her breath coming in steady blasts, her shoes striking sparks off the bigger stones. Once the trees thinned, the wind found them in earnest, flat and cold and smelling of snow that hadn't fallen yet. It pushed at Sylvanas's left side until she was leaning into it.

Theris rode behind her. She had told him nothing about where to ride; he had dropped into place a length off the mare's left hip, as a junior ranger would. Which left the space beside her stirrup empty.

Twice on the climb she turned her head to say something. The first time it was *that stone's set too close to the cut; if the bank goes, it goes with it*. She had the words nearly out before she found nobody there but the wind. The second time she never did remember what it had been. She breathed out through her teeth, keeping her eyes forward after that.

The ward-stones stood thicker here, as they always had near the Pass, where the border was narrowest and the old builders had set them close as stitches. The hum in her back teeth stopped being a hum. It became a pulse, swelling as she came up on a stone and fading as she passed it, the gaps running together until it beat through the saddle like a second heart. That was right. That was how the Pass was meant to feel. Every stone she passed stood upright and mossed and clean-cut against the grey rock. She looked at each of them anyway, running her eye from foot to crown. It was harder now than it had been an hour ago, under the gold, to look at one and then look away.

One mark at a time, she worked at the mage as she would at a trail in hard ground.

A spy, then. Dalaran had them; everyone did. But a spy caught kneeling at a ward-stone in daylight was a poor one. A spy who then told her captors exactly what she'd come to look at was no spy Sylvanas had ever heard of. A spy would have had a story ready. Lost on the mountain road. Gathering herbs.

An envoy, then. Dalaran kept those by the dozen, soft-handed and well-fed in violet, riding up the Pass road with banners and letters and an escort. Envoys did not come over the rocks in the dark. Envoys did not come alone. Or both. With the Kirin Tor it was always worth considering both.

None of the three told a Farstrider post that the leylines were wrong.

She turned the word over, trying it against each of them. It fit none. She knew the lines ran under Quel'Thalas and that the wards drew on them, the same as she knew there was bedrock somewhere under the forest floor. What a right one looked like, she could not have said. Still less a wrong one. If the woman was lying, Sylvanas had no way yet to catch her at it. She could read a trail, a treeline, the set of a man's shoulders the moment before he lied about where he'd been. She could not read a leyline. Whoever this mage was, she had walked straight into the one subject in Quel'Thalas where Sylvanas would have to take someone's word.

The light had gone copper by the time the post came into view. It sat on a shoulder of rock above the road where the Pass began to narrow: a squat tower of grey stone, a timber palisade huddled behind it, a thread of chimney smoke torn sideways by the wind. A ranger on the wall spotted them, shouting something down into the yard, and the gate began to swing.

Sylvanas put the mare through the moment the gap was wide enough, dropping to the packed earth before the mare had stopped.

"Cyndia." She didn't raise her voice. Everyone in the yard had already turned. So had the two on the wall—a boy and a grey-haired woman with a longbow across her knees, both leaning on the parapet with the open mountain at their backs. "Eyes south," she said, and they jerked round as if she'd thrown something.

Cyndia came out of the tower door, wiping her hands on a rag. Lean and scout-built, young for a post commander, she wore her auburn braid frayed loose after a day and a night without sleep. She had the look of someone who'd been awake since before the mage crossed the first ring and expected to stay that way until the mage was someone else's problem. The rims of her green eyes had gone pink, and when she saluted she forgot the rag, which went up with her hand to hang beside her ear.

"General." She lowered both. "Theris found you, then."

"He found me." Sylvanas held out the mare's reins without looking to see who took them. Someone did. Cold had gotten into her hands and the tips of her ears, and the yard smelled of horse, woodsmoke, and something scorching. "Inside. Tell me as we go."

The tower's ground floor was one room; it was never warm. The only window was a slit in the south wall, letting in a blade of copper light and a steady whine of wind. Beside the door a ward-lamp burned in an iron cage. Fed off the grid like every lamp along the border, it gave the same hard blue-white glare they all did, leaving no shadow worth hiding in. Four rangers stood about the room, none of them sitting. On the hearth a pot of barley sat over the coals with a crust going black around its rim, and no one had stirred it in some time.

"I have Theris's version," Sylvanas said. "Give me yours. Start before dawn."

"Before dawn's the trouble, General. Nobody saw her before dawn." Cyndia tossed the rag onto the table, where it landed across a mess of tally sheets and a half-eaten heel of bread. At the table's far end, squared away from everything else, sat a thick bundle wrapped in oilcloth. "Feren tracked her back once we had her in. She came up the scree east of the road—in the dark, which I wouldn't have done with a rope and a lantern—and crossed the first ring between the sixth stone and the seventh. Then she went along it. Stone to stone." Cyndia folded her arms over her chest; it seemed to be where they lived. "Six of them, west along the trace, before she cut north for the second ring. Feren found where she'd knelt at every one. Two knee-marks in the dirt, and a flat place beside them where she'd set something down."

"The book."

"The book."

Sylvanas studied the map pinned beside the hearth, finding the first ring with her eyes. Six stones. Alone, in the dark, on a mountain she didn't know, a stranger had walked a quarter-mile of Sylvanas's border and laid her hands on it six times. Not one ranger on the wall had seen her. Not one. "Go on."

"Feren came up on her at the second ring about an hour after sunup. He's not quiet, Feren, and she heard him a long way off." Cyndia's mouth went flat. "She had two hundred yards on him, General, and she didn't run. She stood up and asked him to wait. Said she had three stones left in that stretch and it wouldn't take her an hour, and then he could do what he liked. He was still angry about it when he brought her in."

One of the rangers by the hearth shifted his weight. Sylvanas didn't look at him. "And since?"

"Since, she's asked for her book back every time I've gone in. And an escort out to the stones—she says we can chain her to them if we like. Then it's the lines again." Cyndia made a small, irritated motion with one hand, tucking it back under her arm. "I'm not ranked for that kind of talk, General, and never pretended to be."

"Theris says the stones are wrong."

"Theris says what she says. This is me." Cyndia drew a breath and let it out, the whole day in it. "I sent Liron out to the stone she'd been kneeling at, in case she'd done something to it. He put his hands on it the way the magisters showed us at Fairbreeze. He says it reads wrong. Reads like a cracked stone reads under your palms—only there's not a mark on it. I went and looked myself. So he tried the next one, and the one after. Six since noon, General, second ring and first. Every one stands up straight and looks as it has for a hundred years. He says every one reads like the trouble's inside it, not on the face. Like knocking on a cask you know is full and hearing it answer hollow."

Sylvanas had ridden past those stones an hour ago. She'd felt them come up through the saddle one after another, quick and close, exactly as they always did at the Pass. She'd looked at every one. The muscles at the base of her skull drew tight. She had gone very still. "Did she do it?"

"I don't know." Cyndia didn't hesitate over it, which was the thing Sylvanas had always valued in her. "Nor does Liron. Could be her, could be something there before her, could be nothing but his hands are tired. He wants a magister. I told him he could have one when I did."

"Where's her kit?"

"In there with her, chained to the spear rack. Staff, pack, and a belt full of pouches I haven't opened and won't." Cyndia jerked her chin at the barred door in the back wall, where a ranger sat on a keg with a spear across his knees. "Not the book. The book I kept."

Sylvanas could make three women out of that: one arrogant, one frightened, and one who'd simply been right in the wrong country. None of them was a spy. She was nearly certain none of them was an envoy.

"There's one more thing," Cyndia said, arms tightening over her chest. "Something in her things. I'd rather you looked at her first, General. Then I'll show you, and you can tell me I've got it wrong."

"Have you?"

"I'd like very much to have."

Sylvanas held her eyes a moment longer, weighing her. Cyndia looked back, tired and stubborn and not budging, while the barley went on burning behind her. Somebody would have to scrape that pot.

Three strides took her to the barred door. The ranger on the keg stood up so fast the keg rocked. "Open it."

He lifted the bar from its brackets, swinging the door inward, and the cold came out to meet her.

The storeroom had no hearth. It kept the post's salt pork and flour and lamp oil; nobody had ever tried to warm it, the stone holding the mountain's chill like a cellar. Her breath showed faintly as the cold reached her in the doorway. Someone had shoved the barrels and sacks against the walls, clearing the floor. A second ward-lamp hung from a ceiling hook, smaller than the one by the door, throwing its blue-white light straight down in a hard circle. In the middle of the circle was a chair.

Sylvanas took the room first, as she always took a room. One door, the one she stood in. A slit window high in the east wall, too narrow for a cat. A ranger in the far corner, bow strung, an arrow laid across it but not nocked. Along the north wall stood the post's spear rack, its pegs mostly bare this late in the season. On the end pegs, where the spare spears ought to have been, hung a staff of plain dark wood shod in worn metal. A length of chain ran through its crook, padlocked to the rack's iron upright. A pack slumped beneath it. From the next peg hung a leather belt, every pouch still buckled shut.

Then the chair, and the woman in it. She had expected violet. She hadn't known she was expecting it until it wasn't there: silk, and rings on soft fingers, and the smooth, well-fed face of someone who had spent her life in rooms where people listened. Sylvanas had met a good many Kirin Tor—on the Pass road, in the gold chambers of Silvermoon. They had come in a good many shapes. Every one of them had worn that face. This one hadn't.

She was young. Even by human reckoning she was twenty or not much past it; by any elven measure she was barely out of the nursery. She wore a long travel coat of heavy blue-grey wool, double-breasted and buttoned to the throat, cut like a ship's officer's coat and stiff with dried mud from hem to knee. Her boots had been good once. Both had cracked across the toes and been resoled at least one time, a snapped lace knotted back together and working loose. Her fair hair had been pinned up that morning, or the morning before, and had given up somewhere on the scree. Most of it hung around her face and down one shoulder, gritty with stone dust. The irons sat on her wrists over the coat cuffs, a chain running from them to a ring-bolt in the floor. It left her room enough to sit up straight and not much else.

She was sitting up straight.

The ward-light came down cold on her face, finding nothing soft in it. The skin under her eyes had gone dark as a bruise, and her lips were chapped and split at one corner. She was looking at the door—at Sylvanas in the door—with the look Sylvanas had seen on rangers ordered to stand down from a fight they knew they could win. A muscle jumped along her set jaw. Her hands were clasped in her lap as far as the irons let her, gripping her own fingers hard enough to whiten them.

Her knuckles were raw. On both hands the skin over them had gone red and shiny, lifting at the edges in small pale flakes. Sylvanas knew that burn. Rangers who wintered at the Pass got it when they were careless with their gloves; she'd had it herself, her first winter there, and hidden it from her mother for a month. But the Pass wasn't cold enough for that yet. Not this early, not even on the tops, and the woman hadn't come over the tops.

The first two fingers of her right hand were stained dark to the second knuckle, ink worked into every crease and down into the nail beds—the kind that never fully scrubbed out. A scribe's hand. A student's.

The mage drew a breath as if to speak, and Sylvanas watched it go into her and stay there. She looked Sylvanas over once, fast—the bow, the leathers grey with road dust, the ranger at the door snapping straighter as Sylvanas stopped beside him. Then she decided something, closing her mouth on it. The breath went back out through her nose, slow and controlled, shaking very slightly at the end.

She'd had a speech ready. She'd been giving it all day to Feren and Cyndia and Liron and anyone else who came through that door. One look at Sylvanas, and she had put it away. Whether that was sense or pride, Sylvanas couldn't tell yet. She wanted to know.

The woman had the look of someone who'd said the same thing all day to people who wrote it down and didn't listen. It was in her shoulders, set against the chair back. It was in her locked hands, and in the chin she held up as if a weight hung from it. She had spent the whole afternoon in this cold room, chained to a floor, while a barley pot burned next door and the stones she'd come to read stood out on the hillside without her. Whatever this was, it hadn't come from any court.

Cyndia came up at her shoulder, carrying the oilcloth bundle from the table against her chest in both arms. "Well?"

"She isn't what I expected."

"No, General. Nor me." Cyndia stepped past her to the edge of the light, peeling the oilcloth back with her fingertips as if it might bite. Under it was a book near the size of a ledger, bound in blue leather gone soft at the corners. "I'll tell you straight, I didn't want to open this. You hear things about mages' books. It sat on my table a full hour after Theris left before I'd so much as touch the cover, and when I did, I did it with a knife." She turned it, angling the flyleaf to the ward-light. "Here."

An inscription ran across it in a long, slanting, old man's hand, the ink faded to brown. Cyndia read it aloud, picking over each word as she would a dispatch. "'For Jaina Proudmoore, at the end of her first year in the Citadel. Write in the margins. The authors were wrong about a great deal. Antonidas.'" She looked up. "That's the name in it, General. And the margins are full, every page I've looked at. Small writing. Same ink as—" She tipped her head toward the mage's right hand and didn't finish. "If the book's hers, then she's Antonidas's apprentice."

The mage hadn't moved while Cyndia read. Sylvanas had been watching for it. She didn't flinch at the name, or look away, or look at the book. Her jaw tightened a little further, but her eyes stayed on Sylvanas.

She didn't deny it.

Sylvanas went into the watch-file. It was a thin file, a few dozen human mages the Farstriders kept current because the Convocation would not. She had read it on enough long nights at the Spire that she could see the pages. *Proudmoore. Kul Tiran; daughter of Lord Admiral Daelin Proudmoore. Taken into the Violet Citadel very young; apprenticed to Archmage Antonidas personally, his only such apprentice in some years. Fair. Slight. Gifted well beyond her age. Worth watching.* Someone had underlined the last two words years back. Beneath them, in a different hand, someone else had added a line: *Antonidas is said to be fond of her.*

Kul Tiran. Sylvanas looked at the coat, taking in the ship's-officer cut and the high double row of horn buttons. She looked at the fair hair coming down, and at the ink.

Her stomach dropped—a short, ugly lurch, like missing a stair in the dark. All at once she could hear her own heartbeat, loud over the whine of wind through the slit.

Antonidas's apprentice. Not a clerk who'd lost her letters. Not one of the hundred violet hangers-on Dalaran could spare and never miss. The one apprentice, the one he was fond of, chained by the wrists to a ring in a Farstrider storeroom beside the salt pork. And Lor'themar riding north this very hour to tell the gold chamber, on her orders, that no one knew who the prisoner was.

There would not be several letters. There would be dozens, every one of them sealed in violet.

And under that, colder: Antonidas would never have sent her up the scree alone in the dark. Not her. Either the old man had let his one apprentice walk out of Dalaran with no escort and no papers, for reasons Sylvanas couldn't begin to guess, or he had no idea where she was.

She didn't know which was worse. She suspected she was going to find out.

*Do try to leave her able to talk.*

Heat climbed into her face, sudden under the cold of the room. At the human for coming. At Dalaran for whatever it had done or failed to do. At six stones on her hillside that looked exactly as they always had and answered hollow under Liron's hands. Her jaw locked. Her fingers closed on the bow grip, tightening until the wrapping creaked. She let her breath out long and slow through her nose, emptying herself of it. When she drew the next one, the heat had gone down under it. What remained was cold and clear and could be used.

She looked at the staff, chained to the rack well out of the mage's reach. Put her out, then. Send her down the Pass road under escort with an apology and a bill for the barley, and let Dalaran have her back. The Convocation would like that. It would also put the only person in Quel'Thalas who claimed to know what was wrong with the stones across the border by morning, leaving Sylvanas with Liron and his tired hands. Or leave her in irons one more night, let the cold take the edge off her, and wait for a magister from Silvermoon who could tell a true thing about a leyline from a false one. That was a night, a hard ride, and another day after it. Every hour of it the stones would stand out on the hillside, upright and mossed and whole, while Sylvanas went on not knowing what was inside them.

Waiting was no better than letting her go. It only looked more careful.

Which left this. It had always been going to leave this, from the moment Theris came up out of the south with his horse dying under him. She had only been taking the long way round.

She had no reason to trust a word the woman said. She would get none standing in the doorway.

Without looking, she held her bow out to the side. Cyndia took it, shifting the book into the crook of one arm.

Sylvanas walked into the light. The rangers moved without being told—the one in the corner stepping back along the wall to give her a clear line, the one at the door drawing his spear in against his shoulder. Cyndia fell back beside the rack, holding the book against her chest. The ward-light lay on the floor in a hard blue circle. It slid up over the toes of her boots as she crossed into it, over the pale dust of the Pass road ground into the seams. Cold came up off the flagstones, seeping into her shins. Her shadow pooled under her, small and black, the warmth of the outer room falling away at her back. Nobody spoke. There was no sound but the wind in the slit and a faint clink of chain as the mage's hands shifted in her lap. Then that stopped too.

An arm's length from the chair, she halted. Close enough to reach. Close enough to be reached, if the woman had anything left to reach with. The mage had to tip her head back to hold her eyes. She did it, and didn't look away.

From here Sylvanas could see what the doorway hadn't shown her. The eyes were blue, the whites shot red with sleeplessness—and they didn't drift to the staff or to Cyndia or to the open door. A fine tremor ran through the clasped hands, cold or exhaustion, and the mage was holding them still against it by will alone. The split at the corner of her mouth had bled and dried and bled again. She smelled of wet wool and old sweat and, very faintly, of something sharp and clean that Sylvanas couldn't place, like the air on the tops before snow.

Sylvanas looked down at her—at the raw knuckles and the ink and the chapped mouth that had been arguing all day and was shut now—and let the silence run until it belonged to her.

"Start talking."
