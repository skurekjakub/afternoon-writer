# The Ravine

The ravine narrowed where an old flood had dropped a slab of shale across half the track, and the mules had to go past it single file. Dace rode at the front with the lead mule's rope wrapped twice around his fist. Behind him came the string of six, then Halloran with the crossbow across his saddlebow, then Ide on foot, because her mare had cast a shoe at noon and she had said she would sooner walk than ride double with Halloran.

Up on the rim it was still afternoon. The grass there was gold and the sky was a hard, clean blue. Down here the shadow had pooled and gone cold, and the stream beside the track ran the colour of slate.

Dace was looking at the slab. A cart could get around it, if you unloaded it first. Not quickly. If he had meant to stop a caravan in this ravine, he would have done it here.

He turned in the saddle to say so, and an arrow went into the lead mule's neck.

The mule screamed and threw its head and the rope burned through his palm. A second arrow struck his saddle an inch from his thigh and stood there humming. He was already off the horse, on the side away from the slope, with his falchion out and no clear memory of drawing it.

"Left side," Halloran shouted. "High. Two of them."

The crossbow went off with a flat clack. Someone on the slope yelled, more in surprise than pain. Dace looked under his horse's belly and saw boots coming down the scree, three pairs, maybe four, sliding and catching, and behind them a man still crouched up in the rocks with a bow.

"The bowman," he said.

"I know where the bloody bowman is." Halloran was cranking. It took him nine or ten heartbeats to span the thing, and that was a long time.

The first of them reached the track. He was young, bareheaded, carrying a boar spear, and he came straight at the mules because the mules were what he wanted. Dace stepped out from behind the horse and cut at him. The spearman got the shaft up in time and the falchion bit into the ash and stuck. For a moment they stood holding the same piece of wood, close enough for Dace to smell him, woodsmoke and sour wine. Then Dace kicked him in the knee and the spear came free, and the young man went down on the stones, and Dace hit him across the back of the neck with the flat of the blade because the edge was facing the wrong way and there wasn't time to turn it.

The second one had an axe and more sense. He did not come straight. He came round the far side of the mule string, using the animals as a wall, and Dace lost him behind the packs.

"Ide," he called. "Behind the mules."

She did not answer. He could hear the stream. He could hear the wounded mule, which had gone down on its knees and was making a noise like a saw in wet wood. He could hear someone breathing hard, close, and could not tell if it was himself.

The axeman came out between the fourth and fifth mules, and he was not looking at Dace at all. He was looking down the track at Halloran, who had his head bent over the crossbow and his back half turned.

Dace shouted. The man raised the axe.

Ide hit him from the side. She had a cleaver from the cook's pack, a square-ended thing for jointing goats, and she put it into the meat of his shoulder where it met the neck. He dropped the axe. He turned to look at her with an expression of great offence, and she pulled the cleaver out and hit him again, lower, and he sat down against the mule's legs and the mule kicked him, and after that he lay still.

Halloran's bow went off a second time. Up on the slope the archer stood up very straight, as though someone had called his name, and then folded forward and slid a short way down the scree and stopped against a thornbush.

The last one, the one Dace had only half seen, turned and went back up the slope on his hands and feet. Nobody shot at him. Halloran had no bolt spanned and Dace had no wish to chase a man uphill in the near dark. They watched him scramble over the rim, black against the blue for an instant, and then he was gone.

Then it was quiet, except for the mule.

Dace stood with the falchion hanging from his hand. His palm was raw where the rope had gone through it, and blood was running between his fingers onto the hilt, and he could not feel it at all. That would come later.

"Anyone hit?" he said.

"No," said Halloran. Then: "Yes. Damn it." He was looking at his own leg. There was a broken shaft sticking out of the outside of his thigh, a hand's width above the knee. "When did that happen?"

"Can you stand on it?"

"I am standing on it."

Ide had not moved. She was still holding the cleaver. She was looking at the axeman, who lay with his face in the gravel and one arm under him, as if he had decided to sleep in an awkward place.

"Ide," Dace said.

"He was going to hit Hal."

"He was."

"I didn't think about it." She said it slowly, as though she were reporting something she had read. "I just had it in my hand."

Dace went to the lead mule. It was down on its side now, the arrow standing out of its neck, its eye rolled white and its breath coming in great wet heaves. Its name was Pruneface. The cook had named it. He knelt and put his hand flat on its jaw, the way you would to steady it for a farrier, and it quieted a little under the hand, and he cut its throat with the falchion. It took longer than it should have, because the falchion was not the right blade for it, and he kept his hand on the jaw the whole time.

When he stood up the young spearman was moving. He had rolled onto his back and was staring up at the strip of sky. There was blood in his hair.

"Water," he said. He had an accent from the hill villages, where they dropped the ends of words. "Please."

Halloran had limped up. He had a fresh bolt in the crossbow now, though he was not pointing it at anyone. "What do you want to do?"

Dace did not know. Up on the rim the light had gone from gold to rust. Somewhere up there was the man who had run, and he might come back with others, or he might keep running until he got home. The caravan master was two hours behind them with the wagons and would want to know why the track was blocked with a dead mule. Halloran needed the arrowhead out before the leg stiffened. Ide needed to put down the cleaver.

He went to the stream and filled his cup. He brought it back and knelt, and held the young man's head up, and let him drink.

"Ide," he said, without looking round. "Put that down now. Come and help me with Hal's leg."

He heard the cleaver ring on the stones. Then her footsteps, uncertain on the gravel, coming toward him in the dark.
