# The Gates of Esk

The council of Esk met in the long room above the corn exchange, and on the night they voted on the gates every chair was filled and the windows were shut, though it was warm, because from the river side you could hear the camp.

Anwen Toller sat at the small table below the dais with her ink and her minute book. She had kept the council's minutes for six years. In that time she had learned to write without looking at the page, which meant she could watch faces, and she had found that faces told her more than the words did.

Provost Hale opened the session at the eighth bell. He was a careful man who liked to have things settled before they were spoken of, and she could see from the way he arranged his papers that tonight nothing was settled.

"The question before the council," he said, "is whether the River gate and the Mill gate should be closed and barred to all persons not resident in the city, from dawn tomorrow, until the council rules otherwise. Master Corrin has asked to speak first."

Corrin of the granaries stood. He had a sheet of figures and he did not look at it.

"There are nine thousand people inside these walls," he said. "There are, by the Watch's count this morning, something over three thousand in the camp on the flats. A week ago it was fifteen hundred. Our stores were laid in for nine thousand through to the barley harvest, which is eleven weeks off, and that was a thin reckoning to begin with. I am not here to tell the council what is right. I'm here to tell it what is in the bins. If we take in the camp, we are on half rations by midsummer. If the camp doubles again, which it will, we are eating seed corn by harvest, and the year after that there is no harvest." He sat down. "That's all."

There was a murmur. Anwen wrote: *Master Corrin set out the state of the granaries.* She underlined *eleven weeks*.

"Mother Ide," said the Provost.

The prioress of the almshouse did not stand; her knees were bad, and everyone knew it. "I have been down to the flats every day this week," she said. "I've seen what's down there. Most of them are from Lower Vey and the valley farms. They walked here because the Duke's men burned them out and Esk is the nearest wall. There are a great many children. There is a fever among the children that I don't like the look of. Master Corrin says he's not here to tell the council what's right. I am. If we close the gates, a good number of those people will die on the flats this summer, and they'll die within sight of our walls, and we will all of us stand on those walls and watch it. That is what we're voting on. I want it called by its name."

"With respect, Mother," said Captain Brand of the Watch, "the fever is the other half of the matter."

"I know it is."

"Then let it be said too." Brand was a big man who spoke quietly, which made people lean in. "If that fever is what I've heard it is, and it comes inside the walls, we lose more than three thousand. I've seen a city take a camp fever. Harrowfield, when I was a boy. I'm not saying close the gates for good. I'm saying close them until we know what it is."

"And how long is that?" said Mother Ide.

"I don't know."

"Then you are saying close them for good."

Anwen wrote: *Captain Brand urged caution on account of the fever. Mother Ide asked for how long. The Captain could not say.*

Guildmaster Sallow of the weavers asked to be heard and was nodded on. "I want to raise a practical matter," she said. "My looms are short a hundred hands since the levy took the young men south. So are the dyers. So are the tanners. Half the men down on those flats were farmhands, and most of the women can spin. I'm not proposing charity. I'm proposing we take in the able ones under bond, house them in the guild halls, set them to work, and feed them out of their wages. The rest can be dealt with as the council sees fit."

"The rest," said Mother Ide.

"I said as the council sees fit."

"You said the able ones. Who decides who's able? You? At the gate? Do we look at their teeth?"

"Mother," said the Provost.

"I'll withdraw it. I won't withdraw the question."

The youngest member of the council, a wine merchant named Tobiah Marsh, had been sitting very still. Anwen had noticed that he had not looked at the Provost or anyone else since the session opened. He was looking at his hands. When he spoke it was so low that the Provost had to ask him to repeat himself.

"My grandfather came in through the Mill gate," he said, louder. "The year of the floods. With my father on his back and nothing else. They let him in because the Provost of the day said, and I'm told this is in the minutes, that a city that would close its gate on a man carrying a child was not a city worth defending." He looked up at last, at Anwen, which startled her. "Is that in the minutes, Mistress Toller?"

"I'd have to look," Anwen said. "It isn't my volume."

"It was a different year," said Corrin, not unkindly. "And it was one man."

"It is always one man," said Marsh. "And then another one."

The Provost let the silence sit. Then he said: "Is there any member who wishes to move an amendment before we put the question?"

Captain Brand raised his hand. "That the gates be closed to entry, but that the city send bread and its physicians out to the flats each day, at the council's cost, and that the matter be put again in fourteen days."

"Seconded," said Guildmaster Sallow, after a moment.

"Fourteen days," said Mother Ide. "In fourteen days you will have a hundred graves on the flats."

"And if we open the gates, Mother," said Brand, "in fourteen days we might have a thousand in the city. I don't like it either. I'm offering what I can live with."

They voted on the amendment by show of hands. It was not close. Mother Ide and Tobiah Marsh voted against. Everyone else, after the smallest hesitation, raised a hand. Corrin raised his slowly and did not look at anyone while he did it.

Anwen wrote it down. *Amendment carried. River and Mill gates to be closed to entry from dawn. Bread and physicians to the flats at the council's charge. To be reviewed in fourteen days.*

When the Provost dismissed them, nobody lingered. Anwen sanded the page and closed the book and sat alone at her little table while the lamplighter came round to put out the lamps. One of the windows on the river side had been left unlatched. Through the gap she could hear the camp: not loud, only many voices and the crackle of fires and, somewhere, a child crying on and on in a thin, exhausted way that never changed its pitch.

She got up and latched the window. Then she stood with her hand on the latch for a while before she went down the stairs.
