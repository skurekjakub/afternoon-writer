# The Odd Mark

The purse sat in the middle of the table between the three of them, and the goose sat underneath it.

"One hundred marks," said Wil Tansy, laying a hand flat beside the purse the way a priest lays a hand beside a holy book, near it but not presuming to touch. "Counted twice. By me, in front of witnesses."

"I was the witness," said Gorran.

"In front of a witness."

"I wasn't really watching."

Mags Coote drank half her beer in one go and set the mug down. "Thirty-three each," she said. "One over. Odd one buys the next round. Done."

"It is not done," said Wil.

"It's done. I've said it. It's the way we always do it."

"It is the way we have always done it," Wil agreed, "and I've let it go, out of affection, for eleven years. But I think we have to acknowledge that this job was not, in its essentials, an equal-thirds job."

Gorran looked down at the goose. The goose looked back at him with an expression of settled malice. "Here we go," he told it.

"Who," said Wil, "talked old Bannerman up from eighty to a hundred?"

"You did," said Mags.

"I did. Twenty marks I added to this purse with nothing but my wits and my voice. I think it only reasonable that some portion of that twenty—"

"Who stood at the cellar door from midnight to dawn," said Mags, "in the rain, while the two of you slept in the hayloft?"

"That was the job. Guarding the cellar was the job."

"Yes. And I did it. You talked. Talking's free."

"Talking is not free. Talking is a skill. You can't do it. I've heard you try."

"I don't need to talk. I've got a sword."

"Well," said Wil, "the sword didn't come into it, did it? Nobody came. Nobody tried the cellar. You stood in the rain for nothing."

"I stood in the rain so that nobody would come. That's how guarding works. If I'd been asleep in a hayloft, somebody would have come."

"You can't prove that."

"I can prove you were asleep in a hayloft."

Gorran raised a hand. They both stopped and looked at him, which happened rarely enough that he lost his nerve and put the hand down again.

"Go on," said Mags.

"I got bit," said Gorran.

There was a pause.

"By the goose," he added.

"We know it was the goose," said Wil. "We were there."

"It's a bad bite." He held out his forearm. There was a purple crescent on it the size of a coin. "I think that should come into it."

"You got bitten because you tried to pick it up."

"Someone had to pick it up. Bannerman said, here's your hundred and here's a goose for your trouble, and the two of you just stood there."

"Because neither of us wanted the goose."

"Well, we've got the goose," Gorran said. "So someone has to want it."

Mags put both elbows on the table. "All right. Let's do the goose. What's a goose worth?"

"At market? Four marks," said Wil. "Five, if it's fat."

"It's fat," said Gorran, with some pride.

"Five, then. Call it a hundred and five in all. Thirty-five each. We sell the goose in the morning, split the five, done."

"We're not selling her," said Gorran.

They both turned to look at him again.

"Her?" said Wil.

"She's a her. You can tell from the—" He gestured vaguely at the goose. "I've been calling her Bannerman."

"After the man who gave her to us."

"After the man who gave her to us," Gorran agreed. "She's got his eyes."

Wil put his face in his hands.

"We could eat it," said Mags. "That's a thing people do with geese. I had goose at Martinmas when I was nine. It was very good."

"We are not eating Bannerman."

"Then you buy us out of her," said Mags. "We've said she's worth five. Our share's two-thirds of that, so—"

"Three marks and a third," said Wil, without lifting his face.

"Three marks and a third. You pay that to me and to Wil, you keep the goose. We settle the purse at thirty-three and the odd mark goes on beer."

"What about my twenty?" said Wil.

"There is no 'your twenty.'"

"What about my arm?" said Gorran.

"There is no 'your arm.' Your arm's your own business. You put it near the goose."

Wil lifted his head. "Can I say one thing?"

"No," said Mags.

"Harrowgate."

The table went very quiet. Even the goose seemed to settle.

"We agreed," said Mags slowly, "never to talk about Harrowgate."

"We did. And I haven't. I'm only saying the word. I'm only reminding the table that at Harrowgate a person who will not be named lost a horse that was not theirs, in circumstances that will not be described, and the cost of that horse was split three ways, equally, out of affection. And nobody said a word about whose fault it was."

"You've just said a word," said Gorran.

"I'm saying the word now, eleven years late, as a kind of interest."

Mags looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at Gorran, who was looking at the goose. Then she reached out and untied the purse.

She counted it into three heaps on the table, quick and flat, the way she did everything, and pushed one in front of each of them. The last coin she set on its edge in the middle and spun, and they all watched it rattle down. "One for beer. And you—" She pointed at Gorran. "Three and a third each, for the goose."

"I can't do a third."

"Then four each and we'll owe you."

"You'll never pay it."

"No," said Mags. "We won't."

Gorran pushed four coins across to her and four to Wil and drew the rest toward himself with both hands. Wil looked at the thirty-seven in front of him, and at the twenty-five in front of Gorran, and opened his mouth.

"Don't," said Mags.

He closed it.

The odd mark went to the girl for three more beers. By the time they came, Gorran had found a crust of bread in his coat and was breaking it into pieces under the table, very gently, holding each one out on the flat of his palm so the goose would not get his fingers again. It got his fingers again. He said nothing. He only winced, and held out the next piece.

"Eleven years," said Wil, to nobody in particular.

"Drink your beer," said Mags, and pushed it toward him.
