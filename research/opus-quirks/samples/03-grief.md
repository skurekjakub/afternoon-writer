# Float

The key was on his ring with the house key and the van key and a small brass one that Helen never did find a lock for. She let herself into the workshop on the Tuesday, a week to the day, because the estate agent wanted to come and look at the house and she did not want him looking at the shed first.

It smelled of two-stroke oil and cold. Martin had kept a paraffin heater by the bench but she did not know how to light it and was not sure she should try. She kept her coat on.

She had brought rubbish sacks, a roll of them, and a stack of the cardboard crates the supermarket gave away. She had a plan. Things that were plainly rubbish would go in the sacks. Tools would go in crates for the man from the auction house, who had said on the telephone that there was always a market for good hand tools, and she had heard in his voice that he did not expect Martin's to be good. Anything personal would go in the one crate she had marked with a felt pen. She had not written anything on it. She had just drawn a line.

She started with the shelf above the door, because it was the furthest from the bench.

Paint tins, mostly dried. A box of spark plugs, each one in its own little cardboard sleeve. A jam jar of washers. Another jam jar of washers. A third, of what looked like washers but turned out, when she tipped it into her palm, to be the ring-pulls off drinks cans, hundreds of them, kept for some reason she would now never know. She stood holding them for a while. Then she put them back in the jar and the jar in the sack, and then took the jar out of the sack again and put it on the floor, not in any crate, just on the floor, to decide about later.

That was the first hour.

It was not that everything meant something. Most of it meant nothing at all. It was that she couldn't tell which was which. A hacksaw was a hacksaw. But this hacksaw had a strip of blue tape wound round the handle, carefully, each turn overlapping the last by half, and she could see him doing it at the bench with his tongue between his teeth the way he had done his shoelaces at five years old. So the hacksaw went into the tool crate, but she put it in last, on top, where she would see it.

He had written on everything. On the wall above the bench, in carpenter's pencil, straight onto the plaster: phone numbers without names, a list of measurements, the word FLOAT underlined twice. On the side of the tool cabinet, a column of dates and figures that she worked out after a while were oil changes for the van. On a scrap of cardboard taped to the vice: LEFT LOOSE. She did not know if that was a warning or an instruction. She loosened it.

There was a lawnmower at the back of the shed that was not his. It had a brown paper luggage tag tied to the handle with string. ABARA, the tag said. 14 Linden Rd. Won't start. Tues. Under that, in a different pencil, as if added later: carb. And under that: part ordered.

She found the part on the end of the bench in a padded envelope, opened along the top and folded shut again. It was a small brass thing with a hinge, about the size of her thumbnail, in a plastic bag with a printed label she could not make sense of. A float, she supposed. The invoice was in the envelope too. It was dated the day before he died.

Helen sat down on the stool.

She had been all right at the funeral. She had been all right with the solicitor and the bank and the woman at the council who needed the death certificate in the original and not a copy. She had been all right with her own children, who had cried, and with Martin's friends from the pub, who had told her stories about him she had pretended to find funny. She had thought, at intervals through the week, that she was perhaps a cold person, and had perhaps always been one, and that this was what people had meant when they said, as they sometimes had when she was younger, that she and Martin were nothing alike.

Now she sat in his shed with Mrs Abara's float in her hand, and she thought: he was going to do this on Wednesday. He had it all worked out. He knew what was wrong and what it needed and he had sent off for it and it had come. It was only a matter of the Wednesday.

She did not cry. It was something else, a weight that settled on her and stayed, so that for a while it was simply too much effort to stand.

The radio was on the shelf by the window. She reached over and switched it on, for the noise. It was tuned to a station she would never have chosen, a man talking quickly about football over a crackle of interference. She left it.

When she could, she got up and looked at the lawnmower. She did not know where a carburettor was. She found it eventually by looking for something the float might go into, a squat metal bowl underneath the engine held on by a single bolt. She found a spanner that fitted the bolt. She was careful. When the bowl came off a little petrol ran onto her hand and she wiped it on her coat.

There was a float already in there, a plastic one, cracked across the middle. She took it out and looked at the hinge and the pin that held it, and at the new one, and they were the same shape. The pin was the hard part. It took her a long time. Twice she dropped it in the sawdust and had to find it on her knees with the torch on her phone.

When it was done she put the bowl back on and did up the bolt. She did not try to start it. She did not know whether there was petrol in it or how much, and she was afraid, a little, of what it would mean if it started, and of what it would mean if it didn't.

She took a pencil from the jar on the bench, one of his flat red carpenter's pencils, sharpened with a knife. Under part ordered, on the brown luggage tag, she wrote: fitted. And then, after a moment, the date.

The man on the radio was still talking about football. She tied up the first sack, and put the jar of ring-pulls in the crate with the line on it, and went on with the next shelf.
