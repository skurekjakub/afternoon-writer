# Avens Ford

The mill at Avens Ford had been chosen because it stood on the river itself, its foundations sunk in the water halfway between the two banks, and so belonged to neither army. Somebody had swept the floor. Somebody had put a table in the middle of it, with two chairs and a sandglass, and nothing else.

Maren arrived first. Her escort stopped at the end of the causeway on the Crown side, as agreed, and she walked the last fifty yards alone with her hands where they could be seen. Inside, it smelled of old flour and river damp. The great wheel had been chained years ago, but the water still moved under the boards, and she could feel it through the soles of her boots.

She sat. She did not touch the glass.

When the door on the far side opened she stood up again without meaning to.

Ilka had cut her hair. That was the first thing. She had worn it to her waist at nineteen, in a plait their mother had done for her every morning until the morning she left, and now it was short as a boy's and grey at the temples. She wore the blue coat of the Republic with a quartermaster's braid on the cuff. She was thinner. She was thirty years old and looked older, and Maren supposed she did too.

They stood on either side of the table and looked at each other.

"Well," said Ilka. "Turn it, then."

Maren turned the glass. The sand began to run. An hour, the go-betweens had said. Neither side's officers had wanted to allow even that.

They sat.

"You look like Father," Ilka said.

"So do you."

"I don't. I look like Mother. Everyone always said."

"You used to," Maren said, and then wished she hadn't, because she watched Ilka hear it and understand what it meant: that it had been ten years, that she had changed, that her sister had been keeping the old face of her all this time and had just had to put it down.

"How is she?" Ilka said.

Maren had known she would have to say it. She had practised on the ride. It did not help.

"She died. Two winters ago. Her chest. It was quick at the end."

Ilka nodded slowly. She did not cry. She looked down at the table and smoothed her hand across it as though there were a cloth there that needed straightening.

"Nobody wrote," she said.

"There was nowhere to write to. We didn't know if you were alive. I tried through the Sisters at Holm, but they said the letters were being burned at the line."

"They were. Ours too." Ilka was still smoothing the table. "Did she say anything? About me?"

"She asked for you. Near the end. She thought you were in the next room."

"What did you tell her?"

"That you were. That you'd just gone to fetch water."

Ilka laughed, a short sound that broke in the middle. "That's good. That's what I'd have been doing."

Maren reached into her coat. She had wrapped the thimble in a handkerchief, and she put it on the table and pushed it across. Ilka unfolded the cloth and sat looking at the small silver cup with its dents and its worn rim, and she put the tip of her finger into it the way their mother used to, and left it there.

The sand ran. A third of it was gone already.

"I have a daughter," Ilka said, without looking up.

Maren had not known. She found she had put both hands flat on the table.

"She's six. Her name's Agathe."

That was their mother's name. Maren said, "Is she—is she well?"

"She's very well. She's dreadful. She bites." Now Ilka looked up. "She has your hands. Long fingers. She can't keep them still."

"I'm sorry," Maren said, and did not know what she was apologising for. All of it, perhaps.

Ilka took her finger out of the thimble. She folded it back into the handkerchief and put it inside her coat, against her chest. Then she sat back and folded her arms, and her face changed. Maren had seen officers do it. She had done it herself. It was the face for a report.

"I have to ask you something," Ilka said. "Is the Fourth Foot still quartered at Corran?"

Maren went cold.

"They'll ask me if I asked," Ilka said, very evenly. "When I go back. My colonel signed the pass on that condition. So I've asked."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The water went on under the floor.

"I was told to find out where your supply depot is," Maren said. "The one for the river line. Same terms."

Ilka let out a breath. Something in her shoulders came down.

"Then let's be quick about it," she said. "The Fourth is at Corran?"

"No. They moved to Hesketh in the spring." It was not true. "Where's your depot?"

"Tollbridge." That was not true either. Maren knew it because the Crown had burned Tollbridge in March, and Ilka knew she knew. "And you'll tell them I lied."

"And you'll tell them I lied. And they'll be pleased with us both for spotting it."

Ilka smiled. It was their mother's smile after all, lopsided, pulling to the left. "There. Duty done."

Half the sand was gone.

They used the rest. Maren told her about the house, which had been requisitioned and then given back, missing its doors. About the pear tree, which still fruited. About old Brannock from the forge, who had married at seventy, to everyone's horror. Ilka told her about Agathe's first word, which had been a curse she'd learned from a carter, and about her husband, who was a surgeon and kind and very bad at cards, and about a winter in the east when the ink froze in the quartermaster's office and they'd had to keep the pots inside their shirts. Neither of them said anything about the war. Neither of them said anything about the man Maren had been going to marry, who had died at Sallow Hill on a day when, Maren knew, the Republic's guns had been supplied through a quartermaster's office very like Ilka's. Some things could go into an hour, and that could not.

As the last of the sand was slipping through, a whistle sounded from the far bank. Then one from the near.

They both stood.

"Will you tell her about me?" Maren said. "Agathe."

"I already do," said Ilka. "I tell her she has an aunt who could climb the pear tree faster than any boy in the parish. She doesn't believe me."

"It's true."

"I know it is."

There was a moment when Maren thought one of them would come round the table. Neither of them did. It was not allowed, and there were men watching from both ends of the causeway, and if either of them broke the terms, the next family that asked would be refused.

Ilka went to her door. At the threshold she stopped and put her hand to the front of her coat, where the thimble was.

"Ten years," she said.

"Don't make it ten more."

"It isn't up to me."

"I know."

Ilka went out. Maren stood at the table and listened to her boots on the causeway, going away toward the far bank, until the sound was lost in the sound of the river under the floor. The glass was empty. After a while she turned it over again, for no reason at all, and watched the sand begin to fall before she went out to her own side.
