# The Bind

People still ask me about the Pask bout, after eight years, as if there's some part of it they haven't already heard in a wineshop. Usually I tell them to buy me a drink and then I tell them nothing. But you've bought the drink, and you've got an honest face, and it's raining. So.

You'll know how the Court of Steel worked in Merrow then. They've reformed it since, partly on account of me, which is a thing I'm meant to be proud of. When two parties had a quarrel the magistrates couldn't settle, a debt, a boundary, a charge of fraud, they could put it to the sword. Not their own swords. Ours. Each side hired a champion, the champions met on the sand in the Cockpit with the magistrates watching from the gallery, and whoever was left standing, their client was in the right. God's judgement, they called it. God had very strong opinions about footwork.

I was good. I don't say that to boast. I'd fought forty-one bouts in the Cockpit and lost two, both in my first year. By the time of Pask I was charging two hundred crowns a bout and turning work away.

Hollis Pask was a goldsmith on Lantern Street. The Assayers' Guild accused him of debasing, putting copper in the mint's gold, which was a hanging matter. He came to me with his hat in his hands and swore on his children he was innocent, and I took the job, because he paid in full and up front and because I believed him. I want that understood. I believed him.

The Guild hired Lucan Fell.

Fell was older than me by ten years, and slower, and he knew it. He'd been the best in the city once. By then he was the second best, and he fought like a man who knew exactly how much time he had left and meant to spend none of it on show. He was very hard to hit. He talked during bouts, which wasn't forbidden but was thought low. Mostly he talked rubbish, to put you off. Your mother this, your guard that. I'd fought him twice and beaten him twice, and both times I'd learned to let it go past me like wind.

It was a cold morning, I remember, and the sand was damp. The galleries were full. Pask was in the dock under guard, bareheaded, and as I walked out he looked at me in a way I've never managed to forget.

We saluted the magistrates and we saluted each other and we began.

I had the better of him from the start. I was quicker and younger and I'd slept well. I took his blade twice in the first minute and twice he got out of it by a hair. I was pushing him back toward the barrier and I could see him starting to breathe through his mouth. I thought, a minute more. Two at the outside.

Then we closed. It happens. You both thrust, and the blades bind near the hilt, and you're chest to chest with your crossguards locked, shoving, each of you trying to get a wrist free. It's ugly and it's close, and nobody in the gallery can hear what's said.

He put his mouth by my ear and said, very quietly, not in his usual voice: "He did it, Sabine. The sixth crucible on his bench has a false floor. His apprentice knows. Ask the boy."

That was all. Then he shoved and we broke apart.

I want to explain why it worked, because people who haven't fought never understand. It wasn't the accusation. Anyone can accuse anyone. It was the detail. The sixth crucible. A false floor. It was so particular, so dull, so exactly the kind of thing that would be true. And it came in that voice, quiet and tired, not the rubbish voice. The voice of a man telling you something because it had been weighing on him.

I'd been in Pask's shop. I'd seen his bench. There was a row of crucibles along the back of it. I hadn't counted them.

I stood on the sand with my sword up and Fell coming in again, and for the space of a breath I saw the whole thing: Pask lying to me with his hat in his hands, my two hundred crowns in clipped gold, and me winning, and God's judgement saying a thief was innocent because Sabine Corve had good footwork.

I didn't decide anything. I want to be honest about that. I didn't stand there and choose to lose. I just didn't move quite fast enough, once. That's all it takes, at that level. His point went into my forearm above the wrist, through the muscle, and my hand opened on its own, and my sword went in the sand.

The magistrates called it. The Guild had its judgement. Pask was hanged eleven days later at the Wheat Gate, still saying he hadn't done it.

They didn't need to prove I'd thrown the bout. Everyone who'd watched me for six years knew how I moved, and everyone knew I hadn't moved like that. The Court struck me from the roll inside a month. That's the disgrace you've heard about. It's real, but it isn't the part that matters.

The part that matters is that after the hanging I went to Lantern Street. The shop was shuttered, but the apprentice was still sleeping in the back because he had nowhere else to go, a boy of fifteen with a burn scar on his cheek. I asked him about the crucibles. He showed me. There were five. There had only ever been five. I made him lift each one and turn it over, and I put my fingers inside all of them and felt the floors. Solid clay.

I went to Fell's house after that. I'm not proud of what I meant to do there. He was at his supper. He didn't get up. He listened to me shout for a while, and then he said, quite kindly, "I didn't know if he did it or not. I didn't care. I only knew you were the sort who'd listen." And then he went back to his soup.

He retired the next year, rich. He died in his bed.

I teach children now. Merchants' children, mostly, in a yard behind the tannery, with wooden swords. The first thing I teach them, before the guard, before the feet, is the bind. I put them chest to chest with a partner and make them lean on each other until their arms shake. And I tell them the only rule I know for certain.

In the bind you do not listen. Whatever they say. However quiet. However tired they sound.

They will always say something.
