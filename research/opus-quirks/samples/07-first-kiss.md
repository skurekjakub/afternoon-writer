# The Crossing

By nine o'clock the reading room of the Harker Collection had emptied of everyone except the two of them and the night porter, who came round every hour with a torch and looked at them through the glass panel in the door as if they might be stealing the furniture.

Clara had the Ashby letters on the foam cradle in front of her, bundle eleven, the last of them. Jonah Pryce had bundle ten across the table. They had been working opposite each other for nineteen days now, since the collection was released, and in that time they had exchanged perhaps a hundred words, most of them some form of "Are you finished with the magnifier?"

It was not that they disliked each other. Or not only. It was that his book had come out two years ago and argued that the Ashby letters, eighty-four of them, written from a farmhouse in Norfolk between 1791 and 1796, were the work of Eleanor Ashby, wife of a naval surgeon, and constituted the most important record of a provincial woman's intellectual life in the period. And her article, which had come out eight months later and been rather more widely read than she'd expected, argued that about half of them were not written by Eleanor at all, but by her unmarried sister Kitty, who lived in the same house and whose existence his book had relegated to a footnote.

He had reviewed her article. She had reviewed his review. They had been on a panel together in Leeds where the chair had had to intervene.

Now they were both here, because the Ashby family had at last released the originals, and it was going to be one of them or the other.

"Pryce," she said.

He looked up. He had taken his glasses off to rub his eyes and he looked younger without them, and more tired.

"Come and look at this."

He came round the table. She moved the cradle so he could see. Letter seventy-one, dated April 1795, two sheets, the second one crossed, the way people wrote when paper was dear: a page written once, then turned through ninety degrees and written over again, so that the lines lay across each other like a lattice.

"I've seen seventy-one," he said. "There's a transcription in my appendix."

"I know there is. You transcribed it as one letter."

"It is one letter."

"Look at the crossing." She pointed with the end of her pencil, not quite touching the paper. "The horizontal text. That's Eleanor. The rounded d, the long s. Fine. Now the vertical text. Look at the d."

He leaned down. She could smell the archive on him, the dust and the cold, and under that something faintly like cedar, and she was annoyed with herself for noticing.

He was quiet a long time.

"That's Kitty's d," he said at last.

"That's Kitty's d."

"The whole crossing?"

"The whole crossing. She's answering. Look, here, Eleanor writes, *I cannot think what the Bishop means by it.* And across that, Kitty writes, *He means nothing, he never does, it is only that he likes the sound.* It's a conversation. They passed the same sheet back and forth at the kitchen table and wrote across each other and then sent it."

He straightened. His face had a look on it she had not seen before, not on the panel in Leeds or anywhere, and it took her a moment to recognise it as delight.

"So you're right," he said.

"I'm half right. You said all Eleanor and I said half Kitty, and it's neither. It's both of them on the same page. They were writing together. Your whole chapter on her private voice—"

"Is wrong," he said. "Yes. And your whole argument about Kitty as a separate correspondent—"

"Is also wrong." She found she was laughing, very quietly, the way you were allowed to laugh in a reading room. "We've both spent four years being wrong at each other."

"In print."

"In Leeds."

"God, Leeds." He sat down on the edge of the table, which was not allowed. "I said your methodology was impressionistic."

"You said it was *charmingly* impressionistic. That was worse."

"It was worse. I'm sorry."

"I said your book had the confidence of a man who had never been contradicted by a woman."

"That was quite good, actually."

"I thought so."

The porter's torch passed the glass panel and moved on. Neither of them looked round. Clara realised she was still holding the pencil, and that he was close enough for her to see a thin line of ink along the edge of his thumb where it had rubbed off a page somewhere, and that she had been looking at it for some time.

"Clara," he said.

He had never used her first name. She had not been sure he knew it.

She put the pencil down beside the cradle, very carefully, the way you put down anything near a manuscript. Then she leaned toward him, or he toward her, she was never afterward sure which, and he kissed her.

It was brief and careful, as if he were not certain of the reading. His mouth was dry. He tasted of the peppermints the archive allowed because they were not technically food. She put her hand flat on his chest, not to push him away, only to have it there, and felt his heart going under the wool of his jumper, fast, faster than hers, which pleased her more than it should have.

When they drew apart he did not move back far.

"That was," he began.

"Don't say impressionistic."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

"I was going to say overdue," he said. "But I'll take impressionistic, if you'd rather."

She looked down at the letter on the cradle between them, the two hands crossing each other on the old paper, Eleanor's lines running one way and Kitty's the other, neither of them quite making sense on its own.

"We'll have to write it together," she said. "Whatever we write next. Otherwise it's just another round."

"I'd thought of that."

"You'll want first author."

"Alphabetical," he said. "Calder before Pryce. I checked."

"When did you check?"

He did not answer. The porter's torch came back along the corridor and stopped at the panel, and they both sat up very straight, a respectable distance apart, until it went away.
