# Group Flashing Two

Teague had kept the Skerran light for nine winters, and in all that time nothing had ever answered it.

It was not meant to be answered. That was the point of it. The Skerran was a black shelf of rock eleven miles off the coast with nothing beyond it but the open Atlantic, and the light stood on it to say one thing to ships: here, and keep away. Two white flashes every fifteen seconds. Group flashing two. You could set a watch by it. Teague did, most nights, out of habit.

So when he came out onto the gallery on the night of the fourth of November to clean the salt off the lantern glass, and saw, far out to the southwest, a small light flash twice and go dark, he took it for a ship. He watched for a while. Fifteen seconds. Two flashes. Fifteen seconds.

He looked up at his own lamp turning above him. Two flashes. Then, out on the black water, two flashes. It came just after his, a heartbeat after, like an echo off a wall.

He went down and wrote in the log: *2240. Light observed SW, est. 10 miles. Character similar to Skerran. No vessel expected. Probably reflection off cloud. Weather fair, wind light W.*

He did not think it was reflection off cloud. There was no cloud. But it was a thing you could write.

The next night it was closer.

He knew it was closer because it was lower. The night before it had sat on the horizon like a star. Now it was plainly on the water, below the line of the sky, perhaps five miles out, and when it flashed he could see it lie along the backs of the swells for an instant, a smear of white, before it went out.

He watched it for an hour. It kept time with him exactly, always just after. Two flashes. Dark.

At midnight he took the signalling lamp from its hook, the one with the shutter, and went out onto the gallery and pointed it southwest and sent three short flashes, which meant nothing in particular. Just to see.

Out on the water, three short flashes came back.

The relief went through him like a drink. A ship, then. Some fool of a coaster out where it had no business, with a man on deck who knew how to work a lamp. He worked the shutter again, slower, spelling it out the way he'd learned it in the Navy forty years before. W-H-A-T S-H-I-P.

He waited.

The answer came back clean and quick, the letters well formed, a practised hand.

W-H-A-T S-H-I-P.

He lowered the lamp.

He sent it again. It sent it again. He sent his own name, TEAGUE, and his own name came back to him, and he stood on the gallery in the wind with the lamp in his hands and did not send anything more.

In the log he wrote: *0010. Light SW, est. 5 miles. Signalled.* Then he looked at the last word for a long time, and did not add anything after it.

He slept badly through the day, and when he went up to light the lamp at dusk there was a fog coming in from the west, low and grey, rolling over the water toward the rock. He wound the clockwork and lit the mantle and the great lens began to turn, and the beam went out into the fog and lit it from inside, two long pale bars sweeping round, and then the dark.

He found the other light almost at once. It was much nearer now. A mile, perhaps less. And it was not on the water.

It was under it.

He could see the swell lit from beneath, a broad patch of green-white glow spreading and shrinking as the light flashed, the way a bathing pool looks at night with a lamp at the bottom. The fog above it was faintly lit too. Twice. Then dark.

He made himself watch it through three full turns of the lens, and on the third he understood what had changed. It was no longer just after him.

It was just before.

The glow under the water flashed, and a heartbeat later his own light swept round and flashed after it. As if his were the echo. As if it always had been.

He went down to the clockwork room with the lantern swinging in his hand and stood over the mechanism. The weights were descending as they should. The governor was spinning. He took out his watch and timed it: fifteen seconds, true to the second. There was nothing wrong with it. There was nothing wrong with any of it.

Ships needed the light. That was the first rule and the last one. You did not put the light out for illness or storm or grief. The winter Mary died he had sat up in the lamp room the whole of that night and kept it going.

He reached in and put his hand on the governor and stopped it.

The lens slowed overhead. He heard it grind and settle. When he went back up, the beam was standing still, a single fixed bar of light pointing northeast into the fog, toward the coast and the sleeping town, and the rest of the world around the rock was dark.

He went out onto the gallery and looked southwest.

Under the water the light flashed twice. Fifteen seconds. Twice again. It did not falter at all. It did not need him.

And then it began to come in.

He watched the glow slide toward the rock under the surface, quite steadily, lighting the weed and the swell and the undersides of the waves. It reached the outer ledges, where the sea broke white even on calm nights, and the white water was lit green from below. It reached the landing stage. Then it was out of his sight, below the curve of the gallery, at the foot of the tower itself.

Teague went back inside and stood at the head of the stairs.

The tower had a window at every turn of the spiral, small deep-set squares of glass running up the wall from the door to the lamp room, and from where he stood he could look down the well of the stair and see the lowest of them. It flashed twice, green-white, lit from outside. Then dark. Fifteen seconds.

Then the next window up flashed twice.

Then dark.
