# The Salt Room

They had taken her rings and her boots and the little knife she wore at her belt for cutting bread, and they had put her in the room under the gatehouse where the garrison kept its salt. It was the only room in the fort with a door that locked from the outside. Captain Oren Hask came down at the second bell with a lamp and a stool and a jug of small beer, and set all three on the floor, and sat.

She was sitting on a sack of salt with her bare feet tucked under her. She was older than he'd been told, fifty at least, with grey hair cropped close to the skull in the manner of the Vessine colleges, and her hands were loose in her lap. Nobody had put irons on them. Nobody had been willing to get close enough.

"You're the captain," she said.

"I am."

"They said you'd come. They seemed to think I should be afraid of you."

"Are you?"

She considered it. "Not yet."

He poured beer into the jug's cup and held it out. After a moment she leaned forward and took it, and he noticed she was careful not to touch his fingers.

"Your name," he said.

"Iselle Varrow."

"Of?"

"Of nowhere, now, I think."

"You crossed the river at the Tench ford on the night of the thirteenth," he said. "Alone, on foot, in the dark, in water to your waist. My sentries found you on the bank wringing out your skirts. You didn't run. You didn't use whatever you carry in those hands. You asked them, and I'm quoting the corporal, whether this was Hask's fort."

"That's all true."

"So you knew my name."

"Everyone on the other bank knows your name, Captain. You're the man who burned the mill at Orl."

He did not let anything move in his face. "Is that why you came? For the mill?"

"No."

"Then why?"

She drank. She set the cup on the floor beside the salt sack, very precisely, as if it mattered where it stood.

"How many men do you have here?"

"I'm asking the questions."

"I know. I'm only curious. It's a small fort for so many chimneys. And there's a village, isn't there, behind the hill. I could smell the bread when they walked me in."

"Mistress Varrow."

"Iselle, if you like. We may be at this a while."

He leaned forward with his elbows on his knees. "Here is how it goes. The magistrate comes up from Carrow in nine days. He will want to know who you are and why you're on this side of the water. If I can tell him you came to trade, or to see a lover, or because you're mad, he'll fine you and send you back over. If I can tell him nothing, he will decide you're a spy, because that is the easy thing to decide, and he'll hang you in the yard. I would rather not watch that."

"That's kind."

"It isn't. It's a nuisance. The yard's where we drill."

For the first time something like a smile went across her mouth and was gone.

"You have a well," she said. "In the yard."

"We do."

"Deep?"

"Deep enough. Why?"

"And the village. Do they draw from it as well, or do they have their own?"

"They have a spring. Iselle. Why did you cross?"

"I'm afraid I can't tell you that."

"Can't, or won't?"

She looked at him for a long moment. The lamp was between them and it put a small bright point in each of her eyes.

"There's a distinction in Vessine law," she said, "that you don't have on this side. Your people say a thing is treason if it harms the realm. Ours say a thing is treason if it is spoken. A college mage who speaks against the Chair to a foreign officer is a traitor, whatever she says, whatever comes of it. Her name is struck. Her students are dismissed. Her sister loses her house." She paused. "I have a sister. She keeps bees."

"So you won't speak."

"I won't tell you why I crossed. No."

"Then we're done." He reached for the lamp.

"The spring the village uses," she said. "Does it freeze?"

His hand stopped.

"In a hard winter," he said. "Sometimes."

"And when it freezes they come to the fort and draw from your well."

"Yes."

"It's been a hard winter."

"It has."

She picked up the cup again, looked into it, and put it down without drinking.

"I was a hydromancer," she said. "Before I was anything else. It's an unglamorous art. Mostly drains. Mostly people who want to know where to dig. But you learn how water moves under the ground, and how far, and how fast. Your river and your well are the same water, Captain, a mile apart. What goes into one at the Tench ford comes up in the other about four days later. Less, after a thaw."

He sat back on the stool. Outside, faintly, he could hear the change of watch on the wall, the call and the answer.

"What goes in at the ford," he said.

"I didn't say anything went in at the ford. I was talking about hydromancy. You asked."

"I didn't."

"Then I've been rambling. I'm sorry. It's the cold."

He looked at her hands in her lap. They were shaking now, very slightly, and she had laced the fingers together to stop it.

"When," he said.

"I couldn't possibly tell you why I crossed."

"Iselle."

"The thaw is early this year," she said. "Everyone says so." And then, more quietly: "Not the children. They didn't say anything about the children. They only said a fort. Someone in the Chair has a map with a fort on it, and they thought, well, it's only a fort."

He stood. The stool scraped on the flags.

"I'll have the well capped tonight," he said. "And riders to the village."

"I didn't ask you to do anything."

"No."

"I want that understood. If anyone asks. I never told you why I crossed."

He picked up the lamp and the jug and left her the cup. At the door he stopped.

"When the magistrate comes," he said, "he'll read my report. It will say the prisoner refused to answer any question put to her."

"Thank you."

"Then he'll hang you, most likely."

"Most likely," she agreed. "But my sister keeps her house."

He locked the door behind him and went up the stairs into the yard, where the well stood under its little slate roof with the bucket hung on its hook. He stood looking down into the dark of it for a while before he went to wake the sergeant.
