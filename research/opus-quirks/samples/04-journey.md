# The Pale

They went out onto the Pale an hour before dawn, when the salt was still cool enough to walk on and the cairns that marked the crossing showed as small black humps against the grey. Oda went first. She had crossed four times. Pell came behind her with the waterskins, three of them, slung from a yoke across his shoulders so that they swung against his hips with every step and made a sound like someone swallowing.

"Two days," Oda had said in the village. "If we keep to the cairns. Three skins is plenty for two days."

The flat was not quite flat. It was laid out in plates, each a few paces across, their edges pushed up into low ridges where the salt had dried and cracked and swelled. The ridges crunched underfoot. Between them the crust was smooth and white and so hard that their boots left no mark at all. By the time the sun was a hand's width up, Pell could no longer look at it. He wound the gauze Oda had given him across his eyes and walked by the sound of her feet.

They stopped at midmorning in the lee of the fifth cairn and rigged their cloaks into a lean-to on the two staves. That was when he found the leak.

It was the middle skin, the one that had hung against his spine. There was a dark tide line down its belly and a wet patch on the salt beneath it, already drying at the edges. He turned it over. A split, no longer than a fingernail, along a seam that had been sewn too tight.

He held it up for Oda to see. She looked at it, and then at the other two skins, and then out at the white. Her face under the gauze did not change.

"How much is left in it?"

He weighed it in his hands. "A quarter. Less."

"Drink it now. Both of us. As much as we can hold. No sense carrying it to leak out."

They drank sitting under the lean-to with the sun beating on the cloth above them. The water tasted of goat and tar. When it was gone he sat with the empty skin in his lap and tried not to look at the other two.

"It's fine," Oda said. "We go at night now. Rest through the heat. We'll be slower, but we'll sweat less."

"Is it enough?"

"It'll have to be."

They slept, or he did. When he woke the cloth above him had gone from white to orange, and Oda was sitting at the open end of the lean-to with her knees up, watching the cairn line run away east until it vanished into a shimmer that looked very much like water.

They walked through the night by starlight. It was easier than he had thought. The salt held the last of the day's light and gave it back faintly, so that the ground glowed a little under them and the cairns stood out black. Oda let him drink at every third cairn. Three swallows. She counted them aloud the first time and after that she did not need to.

At dawn she unstoppered the second skin and shook it by her ear and listened, and then stoppered it again.

"That's the last of this one," she said. "We start the other at noon."

The second day was worse. There was no wind. The lean-to made a square of shade on the salt that was hardly cooler than the sun, and the air inside it was thick and close and tasted of his own breath. His lips split. He felt them go, a small sharp tearing at the corner of his mouth, and when he touched it his fingers came away pink. He wanted very badly to drink, and did not ask, because Oda had not asked, and she had been out here four times before and was older than his mother.

She gave him a pebble from her pocket. "Suck it."

"What does it do?"

"Gives your mouth something to think about." She had one in her own cheek. He could see the shape of it.

In the late afternoon he saw a second line of cairns running beside theirs, a quarter-mile to the north, blacker and taller and very clear. He watched it for a long time before he said anything.

"Oda."

"I see it."

"Is it real?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've seen it before. It goes when the sun does." She shifted the pebble in her mouth. "There was a man the second time I came over. He went to that line. He was sure. I couldn't stop him."

Pell did not ask what had happened to him. Toward dusk the second line thinned and wavered and went, as she had said it would, and he felt a loosening in his chest that he could not entirely account for.

They set out again at dark. The last skin was light now. He could feel it on the yoke, bumping against his hip with a flat slap where before it had sloshed. At every third cairn Oda took it from him and gave him his three swallows, and then put it to her own mouth and tipped it back and handed it to him again, and walked on.

Near midnight the ground changed under his boots. The crust grew soft and gritty, and then there was sand, and then a low rise, and a smell he did not recognise until he was nearly on it, which was the smell of something growing. A thorn scrub. A ram's skull wedged into the top of the last cairn, white even in the dark. And beside it a ring of piled stones with a wooden frame over it and a rope on a pulley.

Oda let the bucket down. He heard it strike water a long way below, a hollow, ringing sound that made his whole body lean toward it. She drew it up hand over hand and set it on the lip of the well and said, "You first. Slowly. A little at a time, or you'll bring it all up again."

He drank. It was cold, and tasted of iron, and was the best thing he had ever had in his life. When he lifted his head she was holding out the last skin for him to hang back on the yoke. He took it, and it was heavier than it should have been. He shook it by his ear, the way she had. There was nearly a cup still in it.

He looked at her mouth then. Her lips were split in three places, worse than his, cracked and crusted black at the corners.

"You didn't drink," he said. "At the cairns. You put it to your mouth and you didn't drink."

Oda took the bucket from him and lifted it with both hands.

"I've done this four times," she said. "You hadn't."

She drank, slowly, a little at a time, the way she had told him to, while the stars turned over the Pale behind them and the salt went on faintly shining all the way back to where they had begun.
