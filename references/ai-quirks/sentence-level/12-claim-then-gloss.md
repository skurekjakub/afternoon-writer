# Claim, Then Gloss

A sentence states something, and the next sentence explains it, caps it with an aphorism, or turns one of its words. The second sentence depends on the first. That dependency is the tell, not a reason to keep it.

**Hard limit:** zero uses.

---

### 1. Word-turn callback
**Bad:**
She had no reason to trust a word the woman said. She would have to go and find one.

**Good:**
She had no reason to trust a word the woman said.

Without looking back, she walked into the light.

**Why:** "Find one" is a pun on "reason". Walking in is already the decision.

### 2. Epigram cap
**Bad:**
Waiting was no better than letting her go. It only looked more careful.

**Good:**
Waiting was no better than letting her go.

**Why:** The second line is the narrator admiring the first.

### 3. Deduction fragment
**Bad:**
Waiting was no better than letting her go.

Which left going in herself.

**Good:**
Waiting was no better than letting her go. Sylvanas stepped into the circle.

**Why:** The reader can do the subtraction. Show the remaining option being taken.

### 4. Stepped repetition
**Bad:**
An arm's length from the chair, she halted. Close enough to reach. Close enough to be reached.

**Good:**
She halted an arm's length from the chair.

**Why:** The mirrored fragments are a flourish. The distance is already in the sentence.

### 5. Gloss on dialogue
**Bad:**
"I'll ride with you," Lor'themar said. It was not an offer.

**Good:**
"I'll ride with you," Lor'themar said, and turned his horse.

**Why:** The gloss tells the reader how to hear the line. The action makes it plain.

### 6. Keep (two separate facts)
**Keep:**
The well was dry. The bucket rope had been cut.

**Why:** The second sentence adds a new fact. It does not explain, cap or echo the first.
